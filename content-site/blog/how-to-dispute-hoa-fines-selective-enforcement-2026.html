How to Dispute an Unfair HOA Fine: Selective Enforcement, Due Process Rights, & Defense Playbook

Published September 4, 2026 • 11 min read • Property Rights Guide

Over 75 million Americans live under the jurisdiction of a Homeowners Association (HOA) or condominium board. While HOAs were established to protect neighborhood property values, abusive or arbitrary fines—often triggered by overzealous board members or third-party property management inspectors—can create immense financial stress.

1. The 4 Proven Legal Defenses Against HOA Fines

Courts hold HOA boards to fiduciary duties. A board cannot arbitrarily issue penalties without clear legal and procedural foundation. When challenging an HOA fine, your response should be anchored in one or more of these four primary legal defenses:

Defense A: Selective Enforcement

Selective enforcement is the most common and powerful defense in HOA disputes. Under established common law, an HOA cannot enforce a rule against you if it knowingly permits similar violations by other owners. If you were fined $100 for leaving your trash can on the curb past 6:00 PM, but photographic evidence demonstrates that three board members and seven neighbors do the same without penalty, the board has waived its right to enforce that restriction.

Defense B: Lack of Procedural Due Process

Most state HOA statutes (e.g., California Civil Code § 5855, Florida Statutes § 720.305, and Texas Property Code § 209.006) require an explicit procedural sequence before a fine can be assessed:

  • Notice to Cure: The HOA must provide written notice describing the violation and giving a reasonable statutory window (typically 14 to 30 days) to cure the issue without fine.
  • Hearing Notice: The notice must inform you of your right to request a hearing before an independent committee or the board.
  • Impartial Committee: In states like Florida, fines must be approved by a committee of homeowners who are neither board members nor related to board members.

If the board skipped straight to assessing a fine on your monthly statement without this procedure, the fine is legally void.

Defense C: Rule Not Authorized by Governing CC&Rs

An HOA board only possesses powers explicitly granted in the Declaration of Covenants, Conditions, and Restrictions (CC&Rs). Frequently, boards pass unratified "internal guidelines" or "community policies" that exceed their CC&R authority. If the CC&Rs do not explicitly give the board fine-levying power for the specific category of conduct, the fine cannot stand.

Defense D: Statutory Preemption (Solar, Xeriscaping, Flags, EV Chargers)

State laws increasingly supersede restrictive HOA covenants. Major examples include:

  • Solar Access Laws: Over 25 states (including CA, TX, FL, AZ, and CO) strictly prohibit HOAs from banning rooftop solar installations.
  • Drought-Tolerant Landscaping (Xeriscaping): States with arid climates forbid HOAs from fining owners for replacing high-water turf with native plants.
  • Display of American Flags / Political Signs: Federal Freedom to Display the American Flag Act and state election statutes strictly protect modest flag displays.

2. Step-by-Step Response Protocol

  1. Document Everything Immediately: Take date-stamped photographs of your property showing compliance or existing conditions. Take wide-angle photographs of neighboring properties showing identical conditions to document selective enforcement.
  2. Review Your CC&Rs and Bylaws: Locate the specific section referenced in the violation letter. Check whether the wording is unambiguous and whether the fine schedule was properly published.
  3. Send a Timely Written Dispute Letter: Never rely on phone calls or casual conversations with property managers. Send a formal written dispute letter via certified mail or trackable email within the statutory response window (typically 14 days).
  4. Formally Request an In-Person Hearing: Explicitly invoke your right to a hearing before an independent committee. This legally freezes fine accrual in most jurisdictions pending resolution.

3. What Never to Do

  • Never Withhold Regular Monthly Dues: Even if you are disputing a fine, continue paying your base monthly HOA assessments on time. Withholding dues allows the HOA to initiate foreclosure proceedings under state super-lien statutes regardless of your fine dispute.
  • Never Ignore the Violation Notice: Uncontested fines frequently compound daily or weekly, turning a $50 warning into a $2,500 legal dispute with added attorney collection fees.
