How to Tell If Text Was Written by AI (5 Free Checks)

Published August 19, 2026 · 7 min read

Something about that email, essay, or five-star review feels off. Not wrong exactly — just smooth in a way that doesn't quite sound like the person who supposedly wrote it. You're probably right to be suspicious. A huge share of the text you read in 2026 passed through an AI model before it reached you, and even the best models leave fingerprints if you know where to look.

You don't need a detection algorithm or a computer science degree to catch most of them. You need about two minutes and five things to check. Here they are, plus a free tool that runs all five automatically if you'd rather skip the manual work.

Why these five checks actually work

AI models are built to predict the statistically safest next word, then the safest next sentence, then the safest structure for the whole piece. That makes them fast and coherent — and predictable in ways real human writing almost never is. People ramble. They get specific for no reason, lose the thread mid-sentence, and occasionally just stop being careful. A model doesn't do any of that unless you fight it into doing so. Every check below is really the same question, asked five different ways: does this read like something generated to be safe, or written by someone who wasn't thinking about being safe at all? (We're aware of the irony of writing carefully about detecting careful writing. Judge for yourself whether we pulled it off.)

Check 1: Read it out loud and listen for the rhythm

Human writing has a pulse: short sentence, long sentence, fragment, question, another long one. AI text tends to settle into one gear and stay there. Pick five sentences in a row and count the words in each. If they all land within a couple of words of each other — say 16, 18, 15, 19, 17 — that's a flag. Nobody self-regulates that tightly on purpose. Real writers bang out a nine-word sentence and then a forty-word one because that's just how the thought came out.

AI-typical: "The meeting covered several important topics. The team discussed the budget in detail. Everyone agreed on the new timeline. Action items were assigned to each team member."

Human-typical: "Meeting ran long — mostly budget stuff nobody wanted to sit through, but we walked out with a timeline, and everyone knows what they owe by Friday."

Check 2: Watch for suspiciously balanced opinions

Ask the text a simple question: does it actually take a side? AI models are tuned to avoid controversy, so they default to "there are strengths and weaknesses to consider" even on topics where a real person would clearly just prefer one thing. A movie review that won't say whether the movie is good. A product comparison with pros and cons listed in exactly equal measure. A restaurant recommendation with zero opinion about what to actually order. That studied neutrality is one of the easiest tells there is, because real people rarely manage to stay that fair for an entire paragraph.

Check 3: Search for the stock transition words

This is the fastest check on the list. A handful of words and phrases show up in AI writing at a rate way above normal human usage: furthermore, moreover, additionally, consequently, in conclusion, it's worth noting that, delve into, leverage, robust, seamless, comprehensive, boasts, and testament to. Hit Ctrl+F (or Cmd+F) and search for a few of these. One hit doesn't mean much on its own. Three or four in a short piece of writing is a real signal — people just don't reach for "moreover" in a text message or a casual email.

Check 4: Look for detail you could actually fact-check

AI defaults to generic because generic is statistically safe. Human writing accumulates weird, specific detail almost by accident — an exact date, a dollar figure, a brand name, a complaint about one particular coworker. Read a paragraph and ask yourself: could I verify any single claim in here, or could this sentence describe literally any situation of this type? "Customers love our product" could be about anything. "Forty of our first fifty customers came back within two weeks and asked about bulk pricing" could only be about one company, on one timeline. If a piece is all atmosphere and no anchors, be suspicious.

Check 5: Notice grammar that's a little too clean

This one feels backwards, but it holds up. Real human writing — even from careful professional writers — has small irregularities: a sentence fragment left in on purpose, a comma splice, an em dash doing too much work, a contraction sitting next to an oddly formal word. AI output, especially a first draft straight out of a chatbot, tends to be grammatically immaculate in a way that reads a little sterile. A three-paragraph message with zero typos, zero fragments, and perfectly parallel structure in every single list is itself a data point worth noticing.

None of these checks are proof by themselves

One flag doesn't mean much. Perfect grammar doesn't prove a robot wrote something — some people are just careful. A slightly generic paragraph might just be a rushed first draft. What matters is how many of the five stack up on the same piece of text. One flag, shrug it off. Three or four flags together, especially alongside a detector score in the 60s or higher, and you're almost certainly looking at AI output, or at minimum AI-assisted output, rather than an unedited human draft.

If it turns out AI did write it, now what?

Depends why you're checking. If you're a teacher or an editor, that's a conversation to have, not an automatic rejection — plenty of institutions allow AI-assisted drafting under specific rules. If you're vetting a freelancer's writing sample or a dating profile, it's context, not a verdict. FAQ

Can these checks be wrong?

Yes. A careful, formally trained human writer can trip several of these flags — especially the clean-grammar one — without ever touching an AI tool. Treat every check, manual or tool-based, as evidence toward a conclusion, not a verdict on its own.

Do these checks work on ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini equally?

Mostly. Each model has its own house style — GPT models lean on rule-of-three lists and the word "delve," Claude tends to overuse em dashes, Gemini leans formal — but the core tells (uniform rhythm, balanced tone, generic transitions, thin specificity, overly clean grammar) show up across all of them because they come from how these models are trained, not from any one company's particular product.

Is it wrong to submit or publish AI-written text?

That depends entirely on the rules that apply to your situation — a school's academic integrity policy, an employer's AI-use policy, a publication's editorial guidelines — not on anything a detection check can tell you. Check the actual policy before deciding what to do with the result.

Do school tools like Turnitin use the same checks?

Turnitin and similar academic tools run their own proprietary models trained on far larger datasets, but they're built on the same underlying idea: AI-generated text has statistically different patterns than human writing. The five checks in this guide are the human-readable version of what those systems measure automatically.

What's the single fastest check if I only have ten seconds?

Search the text for "delve," "moreover," "furthermore," "boasts," and "testament to." It won't catch everything, but word for word, it's the highest hit-rate check on this entire list.
