Jury Duty Deferral vs Excusal: Which Should You Ask For?

Published October 12, 2026 · 6 min read

You got the summons. You cannot serve on that date. Now you have to write the court a letter, and the first decision you make determines whether the letter works: do you ask for a deferral or an excusal?

Most people ask for the wrong one. They ask to be excused when they need a postponement, and the request gets denied even though the reason was legitimate.

Here is the difference, which one courts actually grant, and how to ask for the right one.

What a deferral actually is

A deferral moves your service to a specific future date. "I cannot serve on November 17, but I can serve in January." The court keeps your name on the list and calls you again on the new date.

Here is why this matters: courts approve deferrals routinely. In many jurisdictions, you can get one deferral with no reason at all, just by calling the clerk's office or filling out the form on the summons. It is the path of least resistance for both you and the court. The clerk moves a name on a list. No judgment call required.

The deferral works best when your conflict has an end date: a surgery with a recovery window, finals week, a project deadline, a caregiving gap until family visits in January. If you can name the date the conflict ends, you can name the date you can serve.

What an excusal actually is

An excusal releases you from this summons entirely. You are done. The court removes your name for this round.

Courts grant excusals sparingly. The bar is real and documented: a serious ongoing medical condition, full-time caregiving with no available alternatives, a permanent disability that prevents service. The common thread is that the situation will not improve with time. There is no future date where you can serve.

That is the test, and it is worth saying plainly: if a deferral to March would fix your problem, you do not have an excusal argument. Asking for excusal on a temporary conflict is the most common reason legitimate hardships get denied. The clerk reads the letter, sees the conflict ends in a month, and stamps it denied.

The mismatch trap: A temporary conflict framed as an excusal request looks unserious. A permanent situation framed as a deferral leaves you writing another letter in three months. Match the request to the duration of the situation.

Which one should you ask for?

Use this rule and you will not go wrong:

  • Temporary conflict with a clear end? Ask for a deferral and name the specific date. Surgery recovery, exam season, a work deadline, a short-term caregiving gap. This is most people.
  • Ongoing situation that will not improve? Ask for an excusal and prove permanence. A degenerative condition your doctor says will never allow full days in a courtroom. Full-time caregiving with no alternatives and no end in sight.
  • Not sure which? Default to a deferral. A denied deferral request still shows the court you acted in good faith. An excusal request on a weak showing can read as an attempt to dodge service.

How to ask for a deferral (the easy version)

Naming a specific future date is what makes deferrals easy to approve. The letter says: I cannot serve on this date, I can serve on this other date. That gives the clerk a decision to execute rather than a judgment to make.

Keep it simple: state the summons date, state the requested new date, give the reason in two sentences, list the supporting documents enclosed. You do not need to convince anyone of anything. You are scheduling, not arguing.

Example: "I am writing to request a deferral of my jury service scheduled for November 17, 2026, to January 12, 2027. I had knee surgery on November 12 and my surgeon has ordered recovery through December, including physical therapy and no extended sitting. I have enclosed a note from my surgeon confirming the surgery date and recovery timeline."

How to ask for an excusal (the harder version)

Excusal letters need more weight because the ask is bigger. State the permanence directly: this situation will not improve with time, and explain why. The documentation carries the letter. A doctor's statement about a long-term condition, medical records showing the caregiving role, an employer letter about an irreplaceable obligation with no end date.

Keep the tone respectful and plain. You are not pleading; you are presenting facts that add up to a conclusion. "I am requesting excusal from jury service" followed by the facts, the proof enclosed, and a respectful close.

What if your request is denied?

It happens. If your deferral or excusal request comes back denied, show up anyway and bring your documentation to check-in. Courts routinely reconsider hardship claims in person, and a clerk who denies a letter often grants the same claim face to face.

What you must not do is treat the denial as permission to stay home. A no-show after a denied request is the worst outcome on this page: the court has your name flagged, and a fine or contempt exposure now attaches to the absence. The day at the courthouse is the cheap option.

The bottom line

Most people need a deferral, not an excusal. Name the date you cannot serve, name the date you can, give the reason in two sentences, and attach the proof. If your situation is permanent, ask for excusal and document it thoroughly. Match the ask to the duration of the situation and your odds go way up.

FAQ

What is the difference between a jury duty deferral and an excusal?

A deferral moves your service to a specific future date. An excusal releases you from the summons entirely. Deferrals are granted far more easily than excusals.

Which is easier to get, a deferral or an excusal?

A deferral, by a wide margin. Courts approve most deferral requests, sometimes with no reason required. Excusals require a strong, documented showing of permanent or long-term hardship.

Should I ask for a deferral or an excusal?

If your situation is temporary, ask for a deferral to a specific future date. Only ask for an excusal if the situation will not improve with time, like a serious ongoing medical condition or permanent caregiving with no alternatives.

Can I get a second deferral if the first one runs out?

Many courts allow one deferral per summons and some allow a second with documentation. Rules vary by jurisdiction, so check your summons or call the clerk before assuming.

What if my deferral request is denied?

Show up as ordered and bring your documentation to check-in. Courts often reconsider hardship claims in person, and a no-show after a denied request carries real penalties.
