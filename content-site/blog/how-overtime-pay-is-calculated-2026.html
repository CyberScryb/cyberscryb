The Federal Overtime Formula

Under the Fair Labor Standards Act, non-exempt employees earn 1.5× their regular hourly rate for every hour worked beyond 40 in a single workweek. That's the federal floor, and it's where every state starts.

Worked example: $22/hr, 46 hours worked in a week. Regular pay = 40 × $22 = $880. Overtime pay = 6 × $22 × 1.5 = $198. Gross pay for the week = $1,078. Simple, as long as there's no differential or double-time involved.

Where Shift Differentials Change the Math

A shift differential is extra pay for less desirable hours — nights, weekends, holidays. It's standard across healthcare, manufacturing, retail, and anywhere that has to staff around the clock. Here's the detail that trips up most calculators: under 29 CFR 778.110, a non-discretionary differential has to be folded into your "regular rate of pay" before the 1.5× multiplier applies. It is not a flat bonus you add after the overtime math is done.

Same example, with a $2/hr night differential added: effective rate = $22 + $2 = $24/hr. Regular pay = 40 × $24 = $960. Overtime pay = 6 × $24 × 1.5 = $216. Gross pay = $1,176 — about $98 more than a base-rate-only calculation would show. On a paycheck you check every two weeks, that gap adds up fast.

Double-Time and State Rules

Double-time, 2× your regular rate, isn't required anywhere by federal law. A handful of states require it in specific situations. California is the best-known: double-time after 12 hours in a single day, and after 8 hours on the 7th consecutive day of a workweek.

Who Actually Gets Overtime

Overtime applies to "non-exempt" employees, generally hourly workers. Salaried employees can be exempt, but only if they meet specific duties tests and clear a minimum salary threshold the Department of Labor updates periodically — a salaried title alone doesn't make someone exempt. Misclassification is common, especially in healthcare, retail management, and office roles. If you're salaried and routinely working well past 40 hours, it's worth checking your actual duties against current DOL guidance rather than assuming your paycheck format settles the question.
