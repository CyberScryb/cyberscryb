Collecting Custody Evidence for a Co-parent with Dementia

Published June 2, 2026 · 8 min read

Co-parenting is a challenge. Navigating co-parenting responsibilities when a former spouse experiences cognitive decline or early-onset dementia creates profound emotional and legal trials. Protect child safety and well-being without appearing vindictive in family court. Build an objective, fact-based custody case focused on child safety. Use clear, documented logs instead of emotional accusations.

The Unique Challenge of Cognitive Decline in Custody Cases

Unlike cases involving substance abuse or physical abuse, cases involving cognitive decline are tragic. The co-parent does not act out of malice or bad intentions. The brain is experiencing degenerative changes. Family courts prioritize the best interests of the child. A parent unable to supervise children, remember medication schedules, manage feeding, or drive safely requires a modified custody arrangement to protect the children.

Tone is critical when presenting cognitive concerns to a judge. An angry or vindictive tone leads the court to suspect parental alienation. Present evidence strictly as a safety concern. Demonstrate care for the health of the co-parent while insisting on supervised visitation or structured care limits to protect the children.

What Kind of Evidence Counts in Court?

Family courts rely on concrete facts. Generalized statements like "he is getting forgetful" or "she seems confused" will be dismissed as hearsay. To build a solid case, you must collect the following types of objective evidence:

  • Factual Logs of Incidents: Detailed records of specific safety failures. For example: leaving the stove on, forgetting to pick up a child from school, getting lost while driving with the children, or leaving young children unattended.
  • Written Communication: Text messages, emails, or messages from co-parenting apps (like OurFamilyWizard) demonstrating confusion, repetitive questioning, agitation, or an inability to follow schedules.
  • Third-Party Observations: Statements from teachers, doctors, babysitters, or neighbors who have witnessed safety issues or unusual behavior.
  • Medical Reports and Evaluations: If the co-parent has a medical diagnosis, subpoenaing medical records is critical. If the co-parent refuses evaluation, request a court-ordered neurological evaluation.

Using the ABC Logging Method for Custody Cases

Keep records objective with the ABC (Antecedent-Behavior-Consequence) logging method. The method separates emotions from facts by recording exactly what triggered an event, what occurred, and the final resolution:

A (Antecedent): The context or trigger of the event (e.g., "Exchange of children scheduled at 15:00 at the local library").

B (Behavior): The objective behavior observed (e.g., "Co-parent arrived at 15:45, appeared disoriented, forgot the child's car seat, and did not recognize the child lacked shoes").

C (Consequence): The resolution of the situation (e.g., "I drove the child home and rescheduled visitation pending supervised oversight").

Keep the log in a digital spreadsheet or document with automatic timestamps. Courts value records updated in real-time immediately after incidents occur, rather than logs compiled months later from memory.

Requesting a Professional Neurological Evaluation

If the co-parent denies experiencing cognitive issues, your attorney files a motion for a professional mental and physical examination under state family code rules (such as California Family Code Section 3111 or equivalent). The court appoints an independent medical examiner to perform neuropsychological testing. Medical reports provide the judge with clinical proof of cognitive capacity. Testing removes the burden of proof from you and places the assessment with medical professionals.

FAQ

Is recording phone calls with the co-parent legal evidence?

Proceed with caution. Depending on state laws, recording a phone call without consent is a felony in two-party consent states (including California, Florida, Illinois, and Massachusetts). Check local laws or restrict communication to text and email to ensure automatic documentation and legal admissibility.

What custody modifications are typical in dementia cases?

Courts rarely terminate parental rights completely unless active danger exists. Instead, judges order supervised visitation with another adult or professional supervisor, prohibit driving children, or restrict visitation to daylight hours to avoid sundowning symptoms.
