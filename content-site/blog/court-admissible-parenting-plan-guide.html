A Self-Represented Parent’s Guide to Structuring a Court-Admissible Custody Agreement

Published June 24, 2026 · 6 min read

Walking into custody mediation without a lawyer is stressful enough. But here's what actually decides whether a judge signs off on your plan: how clear and specific it is. Vague plans get kicked back. Detailed ones get approved.

Here's what courts actually look for, broken down so you can build it yourself.

1. Physical Custody vs. Legal Custody

Two different things, and your plan needs to spell out both:

  • Physical custody — where the kid actually lives day to day. You need a specific weekly schedule written out (like "2-2-5-5" or alternating weeks), not just "we'll figure it out."
  • Legal custody — who makes the big decisions: school, religion, non-emergency medical stuff. Most courts default to joint legal custody, meaning both parents have to agree before acting.

2. Setting Holiday and Vacation Priorities

This is where a lot of plans fall apart — people forget holidays entirely. Spell out:

  • Who gets which holidays in odd vs. even years (Mom gets Thanksgiving in even years, Dad in odd years, that kind of thing).
  • Exact pickup and dropoff times for holiday handoffs ("9:00 AM Christmas morning" — not "Christmas morning").
  • Summer vacation rules — how many consecutive days each parent gets, and how far in advance they have to give written notice (like "14 days, notice by April 30th").

3. Communication and Dispute Resolution Rules

Judges love seeing that you've already thought about how you'll handle disagreements. Drop in a dispute resolution clause like this one:

"In the event of a dispute regarding our child's physical or legal care, both parents agree to engage in professional mediation for a minimum of two sessions before filing any modification requests with the court."

Having this in there shows the judge you're thinking ahead and willing to cooperate. That's the kind of plan that gets signed without expensive back-and-forth.
