How to Write a Tenant Repair Demand Letter

When the repair request was ignored, this is the next step: a formal letter with facts, dates, a deadline, and proof it was received. Here is the structure, a template, and the mistakes that weaken it.

What a Tenant Repair Demand Letter Actually Is

A repair demand letter is a formal written notice telling your landlord that a defect exists, that you already asked for it to be fixed, and that you are setting a written deadline. It is not a request. The request already happened. The letter exists to create a paper trail that proves the landlord knew about the problem and had a chance to fix it. That paper trail is what makes every later step possible: code enforcement complaints, small claims court, repair-and-deduct, or lease termination, depending on your state.

The letter also resets the dynamic. Phone calls and texts are easy to stall, forget, or deny. A dated letter listing specific defects, prior request dates, and a deadline is hard to argue with and easy for a housing inspector or judge to read. Most landlords respond to it because it signals you know the process and you are documenting everything.

Request vs. Demand: When to Escalate

Do not start with a demand letter. Start with a normal repair request, in writing, with a date. The demand letter is the escalation after the request fails. Use this test:

  • Repair request: first notice of a problem. Tone is polite and informational. "The bathroom sink has been leaking since Tuesday, please schedule a repair."
  • Repair demand letter: written after the request was ignored or the promised repair never happened. Tone is formal and documented. "I reported this on October 2 and again on October 9. I am setting a deadline of October 23."

The typical trigger: you submitted a written repair request, a reasonable time passed, and nothing happened. Or the landlord acknowledged the request and promised a repair by a date that came and went. At that point, a second casual request is just another thing to ignore. The demand letter says, on paper, that you are done asking casually.

When a Demand Letter Works (and When It Does Not)

A demand letter works best on routine defects the landlord is responsible for under the lease or state law: plumbing leaks, broken heating, appliance failures the landlord owns, pest problems caused by building conditions, water damage, broken locks on common areas. These are clear, fixable, and squarely the landlord's job. A documented letter with a deadline usually gets them moving because the alternative is worse for them.

It does not work as well on problems that are your responsibility under the lease (clogged drain from misuse, damage you caused, cosmetic wear), on defects in common areas you share with many units where the fix is slow and complex, or when the landlord genuinely lacks access because you never let them in. It also does nothing if you never sent the letter in a provable way. The letter is only as strong as its delivery proof.

One honest warning: a demand letter cannot make a landlord who is out of money fix anything. If the property is in foreclosure or the landlord is unreachable, the letter still builds your paper trail for court, but the repair itself may need code enforcement or a court order.

What Every Demand Letter Must Include

Six elements. Miss one and the letter gets weaker. Miss the delivery proof and the rest barely matters.

  • 1. Your identity and the unit. Full name, rental address and unit number, phone and email, and the lease or account number if you have one. The reader should be able to route the letter in thirty seconds.
  • 2. Each defect, dated. List every problem separately with the date it started or was discovered. "The kitchen sink has leaked since October 4" beats "plumbing issues." Add dates of any worsening: "the leak spread to the cabinet floor by October 12."
  • 3. Your prior requests. Every repair request you made, with dates and the method (email, text, in person). This is the core of the letter: it proves notice. "I reported this by email on October 5, by text on October 8, and in person to the property manager on October 11."
  • 4. The landlord's obligation, plainly. State that maintaining the unit is the landlord's responsibility under the lease and applicable law. Do not cite specific statute numbers unless you have verified them for your state. Generic but accurate beats specific but wrong.
  • 5. The specific repair and a deadline. One clear ask per defect and one date. "Please have a licensed plumber repair the kitchen sink leak by October 26, 2026." Vague asks ("fix everything soon") are ignorable.
  • 6. The consequence, realistically. State what you will do if the deadline passes, and only name steps you actually intend to take: contact code enforcement, consult a tenant organization or attorney, file in small claims court, or exercise a remedy your state allows. Empty threats are the fastest way to be ignored twice.

Set a Deadline You Can Defend

The deadline is the part tenants get wrong most often. Too short and it looks unreasonable to anyone who reads it later. Too long and the landlord keeps stalling. Match the deadline to two things: the severity of the defect and what your state requires.

For emergencies, things that affect health or safety right now (no heat in winter, no hot water, sewage backup, gas smell, no working toilet), 24 to 72 hours is standard. For routine repairs (leaky faucet, broken dishwasher the landlord owns, cosmetic damage from a leak), 7 to 14 days is typical. Many state statutes define a reasonable time as 14 to 30 days after written notice, so a letter that gives 10 days for a routine repair is defensible almost everywhere, while a letter that demands a water heater replacement in 48 hours looks unserious.

One more rule: the deadline in your letter should start from when the landlord receives it, not when you write it. "Within 10 days of receipt of this letter" removes the argument about when the clock started.

State Laws Vary: The Honest Version

Tenant rights are state law, and they differ in ways that matter here. Written-notice requirements, how long the landlord has to respond, whether you can withhold rent, whether you can repair and deduct the cost, and whether you can break the lease over uninhabitable conditions: all of it varies. Some states require notice by certified mail specifically. Some cap repair-and-deduct at one month's rent. Some allow rent withholding only into a court escrow account. Some allow none of these.

So this guide keeps the claims generic on purpose. Before you name a remedy in your letter, check your state's tenant law through your state attorney general's office, legal aid, or a local tenant organization. Do not copy a statute citation from a random template and hope it applies. And this page is general information, not legal advice. If the defect is severe or the amount at stake is large, a local tenant attorney or legal aid clinic is worth the consult.

Annotated Template: Tenant Repair Demand Letter

Here is the full structure above, applied to a realistic scenario: a kitchen sink leak reported three times and ignored. The bracketed numbers point to the notes below. Replace everything in brackets with your facts.

[1] [Your Full Name]
[Street Address, Unit #, City, ST ZIP]
[Phone] · [Email]
Lease/Account #: [number]

[2] October 19, 2026

[Landlord or Property Manager Name]
[Management Company, if any]
[Mailing Address]

[3] Subject: Demand for Repair: Kitchen Sink Leak, [Unit #] (Reported Three Times, No Action)

[4] Dear [Name],

[5] I am the tenant at [address, unit #] under lease # [number], which began [date]. I am writing to formally demand repair of the following defect.

[6] The kitchen sink has leaked continuously since October 4, 2026. Water now pools in the cabinet below and has warped the cabinet floor. I reported this problem three times: by email on October 5 (copy attached), by text message on October 8, and in person to the on-site manager on October 11. No repair has been made and no timeline has been given.

[7] Maintaining the plumbing in a safe and working condition is your responsibility under our lease and applicable law. A persistent leak is causing ongoing water damage to your property.

[8] I am requesting that you have the kitchen sink leak professionally repaired within 10 days of your receipt of this letter, that is, by [date]. Please confirm in writing when the repair is scheduled.

[9] If the repair is not completed by that date, I will report the condition to [city/county] code enforcement and consult a tenant organization regarding my legal options. I would prefer to resolve this directly with you.

[10] Sincerely,
[Your Name]

Enclosures: (1) October 5 email to management, (2) photos of the leak and cabinet damage dated October 4 and October 12, (3) text message exchange of October 8

  • [1]-[2] Full contact details for both sides. Nobody should have to search for a phone number or address
  • [3] The subject line carries the defect, the unit, and the history ("reported three times"). A busy manager reads the subject first
  • [5]-[6] Every fact is specific and checkable: dates, methods, who received the reports. This is the notice record
  • [7] Plain statement of responsibility without unverified statute citations
  • [8] One ask, one deadline, tied to receipt. "Confirm in writing" creates the next piece of your paper trail
  • [9] A real consequence you intend to follow through on, stated calmly
  • [10] Enclosures listed and numbered, so nothing can be claimed missing later

How to Send It So It Counts

The delivery method is half the letter. In many states, the landlord's repair clock only starts when written notice is received, so you need proof of receipt, not just proof of sending.

  • Certified mail with return receipt requested is the gold standard in most states. You get a signed receipt back showing who signed and when. Keep it.
  • Email counts in some states and leases, but keep the sent message, and consider following up with a mailed copy. A read receipt or reply acknowledging it helps.
  • Hand delivery works if you get a signed, dated acknowledgment from the recipient. Without a signature it proves nothing.
  • Keep a complete copy of the letter, every enclosure, and every delivery receipt together in one folder. Photograph or scan everything. This is the file a code inspector, mediator, or judge will want to see.

Do not send only a text message or only a voicemail and call it a demand letter. Texts get deleted, numbers change, and "I never got it" is the oldest defense in this process.

7 Mistakes That Weaken a Demand Letter

  1. Leading with anger instead of facts. Insults and accusations give the landlord something to react to besides the defect. Firm, professional, and specific wins.
  2. Threatening remedies your state does not allow. "I will withhold rent" in a state that requires escrow makes you look uninformed and gives the landlord ammunition.
  3. No dates on the defect or the prior requests. A letter without dates is a complaint, not a record. Dates are what turn it into evidence.
  4. A deadline with no basis. Demanding a full renovation in 5 days signals you do not understand reasonable time. Match the deadline to the defect.
  5. Lumping unrelated issues into one letter. One letter per defect group keeps each deadline enforceable. A ten-item letter invites a partial response and a stalled argument.
  6. Sending it in a way you cannot prove. A letter with no delivery proof is a story, not a notice. Certified mail costs a few dollars and removes the argument entirely.
  7. Threatening a consequence you will not follow through on. If you name code enforcement, be ready to call code enforcement. Empty threats teach the landlord that your deadlines are decoration.

Keep Your Paper Trail Organized

The demand letter is one document in a longer file. Keep everything in one place: the original repair request, this letter, delivery receipts, photos with dates, inspection reports, rent receipts, and every message with the landlord. A chronological timeline, not a folder of loose screenshots, is what works in a dispute. Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between a repair request and a repair demand letter?

A repair request asks the landlord to fix something. A repair demand letter is the formal escalation after requests were ignored: it documents the problem with dates, cites the landlord's obligation, sets a written deadline, and states what you will do next if the deadline passes. The demand letter exists to create a paper trail, not just to ask again. Start with a repair request; escalate to a demand letter when it fails.

How do I write a demand letter to my landlord for repairs?

Start with your name, unit, and contact details, then date, recipient, and a subject line naming the defect. State who you are and your lease or address, describe each defect with specific dates, list your prior repair requests with dates, reference the landlord's duty to maintain the unit, state the exact repair you want, set a written deadline, and name the consequence if it passes. Keep it factual and under one page. The template above shows the full structure.

What should a tenant demand letter include?

Six things: your identity and the rental address, a dated list of each defect, a record of your prior repair requests, the deadline by which the repair must be done, the consequence if the deadline passes, and proof of delivery. Photos or inspection reports referenced as enclosures strengthen it. Never include threats you do not intend to carry out.

How long does a landlord have to fix repairs after receiving a demand letter?

It depends on the state and the severity. Emergencies like no heat, no water, or a gas leak usually require action within 24 to 72 hours. Routine repairs typically get 7 to 14 days, and many state statutes define reasonable time as 14 to 30 days after written notice. Your letter's deadline should match what your state actually requires, not what feels fair.

Should I send a repair demand letter by certified mail?

Yes, if you want proof the landlord received it. Send it certified mail with return receipt requested, or another trackable method your state recognizes, and keep a copy of the letter plus the receipt. In many states, repair-and-deduct rights and habitability claims only start the clock after the landlord received written notice, so delivery proof is the part most tenants skip and most disputes turn on.

Can I withhold rent if my landlord ignores a repair demand letter?

Sometimes, but it is state-specific and often comes with strict rules: written notice first, an escrow account, or a court filing. Some states allow repair-and-deduct instead, usually capped at a fraction of monthly rent. Do not threaten or withhold rent in your letter unless your state actually permits it. This is general information, not legal advice.

Do I need a lawyer to write a demand letter?

No. A tenant repair demand letter is a plain-language formal letter, and most tenants write their own. A lawyer helps when the defect is severe, the amount at stake is large, or you are heading toward court. If the landlord ignores the letter, that is when legal help or your local tenant organization usually enters the picture.

What do I do if my landlord ignores the demand letter?

Follow up once in writing referencing the original letter and deadline, then act on the consequence you named: contact your local code enforcement or health department, reach out to a tenant organization or legal aid, file in small claims court, or use a repair-and-deduct or rent-withholding remedy if your state allows it.
