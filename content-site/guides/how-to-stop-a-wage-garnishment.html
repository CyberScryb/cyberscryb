How to Stop a Wage Garnishment

Your paycheck is being garnished or a notice just arrived. The federal limits, the exemptions, the challenge process, and what to do first.

By CyberScryb 📅 Updated November 15, 2026 ⏱ 11 min read
⚡ Key Takeaways (TL;DR)

A wage garnishment is a court order, or a government order, that tells your employer to take part of your paycheck and send it to a creditor. For most debts, federal law caps the garnishment at 25 percent of your disposable earnings, or the amount above 30 times the federal minimum wage per week, whichever is less. Child support, taxes, and federal student loans have higher limits or no percentage cap. You stop a garnishment by paying the debt, negotiating a payment plan the creditor accepts in writing, filing a claim of exemption with the court, challenging the judgment or the procedure, or filing for bankruptcy as a last resort. The first thing to do is read the order, check the math against the federal limit, and mark the deadline to object, because exemption claims have short deadlines. This guide is general information, not legal advice.

This guide is general information, not legal advice, and it is not a substitute for a lawyer. Garnishment law is federal and state law together, the deadlines are short, and the right steps depend on your debt, your income, and your court papers. If a garnishment has started or a notice has arrived, talk to a legal aid attorney or a consumer debt attorney as soon as you can, and confirm every deadline against your own court papers, not against anything you read here.

What wage garnishment is

Wage garnishment is a legal order that tells your employer to withhold part of your paycheck and send it to a creditor. The money never reaches you. Your employer takes it out before payday, sends it to the creditor or the court, and gives you a pay stub that shows the deduction. The garnishment keeps running every pay period until the debt is paid off, the court stops it, or the creditor releases it.

A garnishment is not the same as a creditor calling you or sending letters. It is the end of a legal process. For most debts, the creditor had to sue you and win a court judgment first. For some debts, like taxes, child support, and federal student loans, the government can garnish without suing you first, but it still has to send you notice and give you a chance to object. Either way, by the time your employer gets the order, someone has already decided you owe the money. Your job now is to check whether that decision was right, whether the amount is right, and whether you have a defense.

Garnishment applies to wages, which means money you earn from a job. A creditor who wants your bank account uses a different order, called a bank levy. A creditor who wants your property uses another process entirely. This guide covers wage garnishment, with bank levies covered where they overlap.

Who can garnish your wages

Not every creditor can garnish. The rules depend on who is owed the money, and the limits are different for each type. The four kinds you will run into:

  • A creditor with a court judgment. A credit card company, a lender, a medical provider, a landlord, or a debt buyer who sued you and won. They need a judgment first, then a separate garnishment order, called a writ of garnishment in many states. This is the most common kind, and the federal 25 percent limit applies. If you never got sued, or never got the lawsuit papers, that matters, and the section on challenging a garnishment covers it.
  • The IRS. The IRS does not need a court judgment to levy your wages. It sends a series of notices first, including a Final Notice of Intent to Levy, and then sends a levy to your employer. An IRS levy has no percentage cap like a creditor garnishment. Instead, the IRS must leave you an exempt amount based on your standard deduction and the number of dependents you claim. The exempt amount is set by IRS tables and changes every year. If you get an IRS levy, call the IRS number on the notice right away, because a payment plan or an installment agreement can stop or replace the levy.
  • Child support. A state child support agency or a court can garnish your wages for child support, and the limits are higher than for other debts. Federal law allows up to 50 percent of disposable earnings if you are supporting another spouse or child, and up to 60 percent if you are not. If you are more than 12 weeks behind, another 5 percent can be added on top. Child support garnishment does not need a separate lawsuit. It usually starts with an income withholding order sent straight to your employer.
  • Federal student loans. The Department of Education and its collectors can garnish your wages for a defaulted federal student loan without a court judgment, through administrative wage garnishment. The cap is 15 percent of your disposable pay. You should get a notice at least 30 days before the garnishment starts, and you can request a hearing, ask for a payment plan, or rehabilitate the loan to stop it. Private student loans are different: the lender has to sue you and win a judgment, just like a credit card company.

The first thing to do with any garnishment order is to identify which kind it is. The order itself should say who the creditor is and what court or agency issued it. That tells you which limit applies, which deadline applies, and which office to call.

How much of your paycheck they can take

For most debts, the federal Consumer Credit Protection Act, called the CCPA, sets a ceiling on wage garnishment. The ceiling is the smaller of two numbers: 25 percent of your disposable earnings, or the amount by which your weekly disposable earnings exceed 30 times the federal minimum wage. At the current federal minimum wage of $7.25 an hour, 30 times the minimum wage is $217.50 a week. Whichever number is smaller is the most they can take.

Disposable earnings means your pay after the deductions the law requires: federal, state, and local taxes, Social Security, and unemployment insurance. It is not your gross pay. Voluntary deductions like health insurance premiums and retirement contributions are usually not subtracted when the law calculates disposable earnings, so your disposable earnings are often higher than your take-home pay. That is a detail worth checking, because it changes the math.

A worked example, using weekly pay:

  • You earn $600 a week in disposable earnings. 25 percent is $150. The amount above $217.50 is $382.50. The smaller number is $150, so the most they can take is $150 a week.
  • You earn $300 a week in disposable earnings. 25 percent is $75. The amount above $217.50 is $82.50. The smaller number is $75, so the most they can take is $75 a week.
  • You earn $250 a week in disposable earnings. 25 percent is $62.50. The amount above $217.50 is $32.50. The smaller number is $32.50, so the most they can take is $32.50 a week.
  • You earn $217.50 a week or less in disposable earnings. Nothing above $217.50, so an ordinary creditor garnishment takes nothing. Your wages are fully protected at that level, but only from ordinary creditor garnishment. Child support, taxes, and student loans have their own rules.

The limits by debt type, side by side:

  • Ordinary creditor with a judgment: 25 percent of disposable earnings, or the amount above 30 times the federal minimum wage, whichever is less.
  • Child support: up to 50 percent if you support another spouse or child, up to 60 percent if you do not, plus 5 percent more if you are over 12 weeks behind.
  • Federal student loans: up to 15 percent of disposable pay.
  • IRS levy: no percentage cap. The IRS must leave you the exempt amount from its tables, based on your standard deduction and dependents.

If more than one garnishment is running at the same time, the total taken still cannot exceed the federal limit for ordinary debts, except that child support and tax levies are treated separately. Check the order and your pay stub against these numbers the first week. If the amount taken is higher than the limit, that is a reason to file a claim of exemption, covered below.

State limits vary, and some states protect more

The federal limit is a ceiling, not a floor. States can protect more of your wages than the federal law does, and many do. Some states set a lower percentage cap. Some protect a higher weekly amount. Some exempt the wages of a head of household entirely. A few states do not allow wage garnishment for most consumer debts at all.

This guide does not list state limits, because they change and because the details matter. To find your state's limit, call the clerk of the court that issued the garnishment order and ask what exemption forms and limits apply, or check your state court's self-help page. A legal aid office in your state can tell you the limit in one phone call. Do not assume the federal limit is the one that applies to you. In many states it is not.

What to do when the notice arrives

The notice may reach you from the court, from the creditor, or from your employer, and it may arrive after the garnishment has already started. Move fast either way. The deadlines to object are short, often 10 to 30 days from the notice, and they run from the date on the papers, not from the day you open the envelope.

  1. Read the order and identify the debt. Who is the creditor, what court or agency issued the order, what is the total amount claimed, and what is the case number. Write all four down. If the creditor is a name you do not recognize, it may be a debt buyer that bought an old debt. That matters for the challenge steps below.
  2. Check the math against the federal limit. Work out your weekly disposable earnings from your pay stub, apply the 25 percent rule and the $217.50 floor, and compare the result to what the order says will be taken. If the order takes more than the limit allows, note it. That is a concrete error you can raise.
  3. Mark the deadline to object. The notice should state how many days you have to file a claim of exemption or an objection. Mark it on a calendar the same day the notice arrives. Missing it can mean losing the right to challenge the garnishment at all.
  4. Call the court clerk. Ask for the claim of exemption form, the filing fee, whether the fee can be waived, and the hearing date if one is set. Court clerks cannot give legal advice, but they can tell you which form to file and where to file it. Get the clerk's name and write down what they tell you.
  5. Call a legal aid office. Legal aid helps for free if your income qualifies, and many offices have a debt or garnishment clinic. Bring the order, your last three pay stubs, and any earlier lawsuit papers. A lawyer can spot defenses in ten minutes that take hours to find alone.
  6. Do not ignore it. A garnishment does not stop because you did not read the notice. It runs until the debt is paid, the court stops it, or the creditor releases it. Every week you wait is another week of deductions.

How to challenge a garnishment

A garnishment can be challenged on two fronts: the amount is wrong, or the garnishment should not exist at all. The procedure is different for each, and the deadline is short for both.

  • Claim of exemption. This is the form you file with the court when the garnishment takes more than the law allows, or when it leaves you unable to pay for basic needs like rent, food, utilities, and medicine. You list your income, your necessary expenses, and the people who depend on you, and you ask the court to reduce or stop the garnishment. The court sets a hearing, and the creditor can argue against you. Bring pay stubs, rent receipts, utility bills, and proof of dependents. The judge decides based on the numbers in front of them, so bring the numbers.
  • Motion to quash or vacate. This asks the court to throw out the garnishment because something in the process was wrong: the creditor never served you with the lawsuit, the judgment was entered against the wrong person, the debt was already paid, the statute of limitations had run out, or the garnishment order has an error. A motion to quash is a stronger claim than a claim of exemption, because it attacks the garnishment itself, not just the amount. It also has stricter deadlines and usually needs a lawyer.
  • Hardship claim. In many states, you can ask the court to reduce the garnishment because it causes undue hardship, even if the amount is technically within the legal limit. This is the claim to make when the garnishment is legal but you cannot feed your family or keep your housing on what is left. It is a separate argument from the exemption claim, and in some states it is filed on the same form. Bring a household budget that shows your income, your necessary expenses, and the shortfall the garnishment creates.
  • Challenge the underlying judgment. If you were never served with the lawsuit, you can ask the court to set aside the default judgment. This is called a motion to vacate a default judgment in most states. You usually have to show that you were not served and that you have a defense to the debt. If the court vacates the judgment, the garnishment falls with it, but the creditor can sue you again. Talk to a lawyer before filing this, because the timing rules are strict and vary by state.

For IRS levies, federal student loan garnishment, and child support withholding, the challenge process is administrative, not a court filing. The notice tells you how to request a hearing or file an objection, and it gives a deadline. An IRS levy can be appealed through the IRS Collection Appeals Program or by requesting a Collection Due Process hearing. A student loan garnishment can be challenged by requesting a hearing with the Department of Education. A child support order can be modified by asking the court or the support agency for a review, usually because your income has dropped. In all three cases, the fastest move is usually to call the agency on the notice and ask what payment plan would stop the garnishment.

Exemptions and how to claim them

An exemption is a category of income or property the law protects from creditors. Some exemptions are automatic in the sense that the law says the money cannot be taken, but in practice you usually have to claim them. If you do not file the claim, the garnishment runs.

  • Social Security and SSI. Social Security benefits and Supplemental Security Income generally cannot be garnished by an ordinary creditor. The IRS, child support, and federal student loan collectors can reach some Social Security benefits. Keep benefits in a separate bank account and do not mix them with other money, because commingling makes it harder to prove the money is protected.
  • Veterans benefits and federal retirement. VA disability benefits and federal retirement payments are generally protected from ordinary creditors. Child support and federal debts can reach some of them.
  • Public assistance. TANF, SNAP, and other public assistance payments are generally exempt from creditor garnishment in most states.
  • Head of household. Many states exempt some or all of the wages of a person who provides more than half the support for a dependent. The definition and the amount vary by state. If you support children or a dependent adult, ask the court clerk whether your state has a head-of-household exemption.
  • The federal wage floor. The first $217.50 per week of disposable earnings is protected from ordinary creditor garnishment at the current federal minimum wage. This is the floor built into the CCPA calculation, and it applies even if your state has no separate exemption.

To claim an exemption, file the claim of exemption form with the court that issued the garnishment, send a copy to the creditor or their attorney, and show up for the hearing if one is set. Bring proof: pay stubs, benefit letters, bank statements showing the source of the money, and a household budget. If the exemption is granted, the court reduces or stops the garnishment. If it is denied, you can appeal, and you can still negotiate with the creditor.

Negotiating with the creditor

The fastest way to stop most garnishments is to negotiate. The creditor has a garnishment running, which means they are getting paid slowly. A lump sum or a larger monthly payment is often worth more to them than years of small deductions, and they know it. You have leverage as long as you can pay something.

  • Call the creditor or their attorney, not the collection agency. The name and number are on the garnishment order. Ask who has authority to settle the debt and release the garnishment. Get that person's name and direct number.
  • Know your number before you call. Work out what you can pay per month without missing rent, utilities, food, and medicine. Write it down. Do not agree to a payment you cannot keep, because a broken payment plan puts you back where you started, with the garnishment running again.
  • Offer a lump sum first if you can. Creditors often accept 40 to 60 percent of the balance as a lump sum settlement, but that range is a starting point for negotiation, not a rule. Old debts, debts the creditor bought cheap, and debts with weak paperwork settle for less. Offer less than your top number and let them counter.
  • Get the agreement in writing before you pay. The written agreement should say the total amount, the payment schedule, that the creditor will release the garnishment, and that the debt will be reported as settled or paid in full. Do not send money on a verbal promise. If the creditor will not put it in writing, do not pay.
  • Confirm the release. After you pay under the agreement, check with your employer that the garnishment has stopped, and check your pay stub the next payday. If the garnishment is still running after the release date, call the creditor and then the court clerk.

Payment plans and settlement

If a lump sum is out of reach, a voluntary payment plan is the next best option. A payment plan replaces the garnishment with an agreed monthly payment. The creditor releases the garnishment, you pay the agreed amount, and the debt shrinks on a schedule you can actually keep.

For the IRS, the formal version is an installment agreement. You can apply online or by phone, and the IRS will usually accept a plan if the monthly payment covers the debt within the collection statute period. An approved installment agreement stops a levy. For federal student loans, the options are rehabilitation, which means nine on-time payments over ten months, or consolidation into a new loan with an income-driven repayment plan. Both stop the garnishment. For child support, ask the support agency about a payment plan for arrears, and ask the court for a modification if your income has dropped since the order was set.

A nonprofit credit counseling agency can set up a debt management plan that covers several debts at once, with reduced interest and one monthly payment. Use an agency approved by the U.S. Trustee Program or a member of the National Foundation for Credit Counseling. Avoid for-profit debt settlement companies that charge large fees and tell you to stop paying your bills while they negotiate, because missed payments damage your credit and the creditor can keep garnishing while you wait.

Bankruptcy as a last resort

Filing for bankruptcy stops most wage garnishments immediately through the automatic stay, which is a court order that halts collection while the case runs. Chapter 7 discharges most unsecured debts, including credit card debt and medical debt, and the garnishment ends with the debt. Chapter 13 sets up a three-to-five-year repayment plan and stops the garnishment while you pay under the plan.

Bankruptcy does not stop everything. Child support and alimony are not discharged, and the garnishment for them continues. Some tax debts survive bankruptcy. Federal student loans are very hard to discharge. A bankruptcy filing also stays on your credit report for seven to ten years. That is a real cost, and it is why bankruptcy is a last resort, not a first move.

Talk to a bankruptcy attorney before you file. Most offer a free consultation, and they can tell you whether your debts would actually be discharged, whether you would lose any property, and whether a Chapter 7 or a Chapter 13 fits. A legal aid office can refer you to one. Do not file alone. Bankruptcy paperwork has strict deadlines, and a mistake can cost you the discharge.

The timeline of a garnishment

Knowing where you are in the process tells you which moves are still open. The timeline for a creditor garnishment usually runs like this:

  1. The debt goes unpaid. You miss payments, the account goes to collections, and the creditor or a debt buyer decides to sue.
  2. The lawsuit. The creditor files suit and serves you with a summons and complaint. You have a deadline to answer, usually 20 to 30 days. If you do not answer, the court enters a default judgment against you.
  3. The judgment. The court decides you owe the debt and sets the amount, including interest and court costs. The judgment is the legal basis for everything that follows.
  4. The garnishment order. The creditor asks the court for a garnishment order and serves it on your employer. The order tells the employer how much to withhold and where to send it.
  5. The deductions start. Your employer withholds the amount from each paycheck. You should get a notice from the court or the employer, and a statement showing the balance remaining.
  6. The debt is paid off, or the garnishment stops. The garnishment ends when the balance hits zero, when the creditor releases it, when the court orders it stopped, or when bankruptcy stops it.

The timeline is different for taxes, child support, and student loans, because those do not need a lawsuit or a judgment. The IRS sends a series of notices, then a Final Notice of Intent to Levy, then the levy. A child support agency sends an income withholding order straight to your employer. A student loan collector sends a notice at least 30 days before the garnishment starts. In all three cases, the notice gives you a window to object or set up a payment plan. Use it.

What your employer has to do, and what they cannot do

Your employer is not a party to your debt. They are following a court order, and the law tells them what they must do and what they cannot do.

  • Your employer must comply with the garnishment order. If they ignore it, the creditor can hold them liable for the money that should have been withheld. Do not ask your employer to ignore the order. They cannot.
  • Your employer cannot fire you for one garnishment. The CCPA makes it illegal to fire an employee because their wages are garnished for a single debt. The protection covers one garnishment. It does not cover two or more garnishments for separate debts, and it does not stop an employer from firing you for a separate reason. If you are fired right after a garnishment starts, write down the dates and what was said, and talk to an employment lawyer or your state labor agency.
  • Your employer can charge a small processing fee in some states. A few states let employers deduct a small administrative fee from your pay for handling the garnishment. The fee is usually a few dollars per pay period, and it is set by state law.
  • Your employer must give you a statement. You should get a statement showing the amount withheld and the balance remaining on the debt. If you do not get one, ask your payroll department for it. Keep every statement and every pay stub. They are your record of what was taken and what is left.

Mistakes that backfire

  • Ignoring the lawsuit. Most garnishments start with a default judgment because the person never answered the summons. Answer the lawsuit. Even a weak answer buys time and forces the creditor to prove the debt.
  • Missing the deadline to object. A claim of exemption has a short deadline, often 10 to 30 days. Miss it and you lose the right to challenge the garnishment, even if the amount is wrong.
  • Quitting your job to dodge the garnishment. The debt survives, it grows with interest, and the creditor can garnish your next job or levy your bank account. You lose the income you need to negotiate.
  • Paying on a verbal promise. A creditor who promises to release the garnishment if you pay, but will not put it in writing, can take the money and keep garnishing. Get it in writing first, then pay.
  • Moving exempt money into a regular account. Social Security and other protected benefits are harder to defend once they are mixed with other money in the same account. Keep protected income in its own account.
  • Hiding income or working under the table. It does not stop the debt, and it creates tax problems and legal exposure that are worse than the garnishment.
  • Paying a for-profit debt settlement company and stopping all payments. The creditor keeps garnishing while you wait for the company to negotiate, your credit takes more damage, and the fees eat the money you saved.
  • Assuming the garnishment will stop on its own. It runs until the debt is paid, the court stops it, or the creditor releases it. It does not expire because time passed.

FAQ

How can I stop a wage garnishment?

There is no single switch. The options are to pay the debt in full, negotiate a payment plan or settlement with the creditor and get the garnishment released in writing, file a claim of exemption with the court if the garnishment takes more than the law allows or leaves you unable to pay for basic needs, challenge the garnishment in court if the underlying judgment or the procedure was wrong, or file for bankruptcy, which stops most garnishments through the automatic stay. The fastest realistic path for most people is to call the creditor or their attorney and negotiate a voluntary payment plan. Get any agreement in writing before you send money. This guide is general information, not legal advice.

How much of my paycheck can they garnish?

For most debts under the federal Consumer Credit Protection Act, the limit is the smaller of 25 percent of your disposable earnings, or the amount by which your weekly disposable earnings exceed 30 times the federal minimum wage, which is $217.50 per week at the current $7.25 federal minimum wage. The limits are different for other kinds of debt: child support can take up to 50 or 60 percent of disposable earnings, federal student loan garnishment is capped at 15 percent, and an IRS levy has no percentage cap but must leave you an exempt amount based on your standard deduction and dependents. Some states set lower limits than the federal ones. Check the garnishment order against the federal limits first, then check your state.

Can they garnish my wages without notice?

A regular creditor cannot. They have to sue you, win a court judgment, and then get a garnishment order, and you should have received the lawsuit papers and the judgment notice along the way. If you never got any papers, the judgment may be a default judgment entered because you did not respond, and you may be able to challenge it. The IRS, child support agencies, and federal student loan collectors are different: they can garnish without a court judgment, but they still have to send you written notice first and give you a chance to object or set up a payment plan. If your employer hands you a garnishment order with no prior notice at all, find out which kind it is and what notice the law required.

How long does a wage garnishment last?

Until the debt is paid in full, including interest and fees, unless it is stopped another way. A creditor garnishment continues every pay period until the balance is zero, the court orders it stopped, the creditor releases it, or bankruptcy stops it. Child support garnishment continues until the support obligation ends and any arrears are paid. There is no fixed number of months. Your employer should give you a statement showing the balance remaining. Keep every pay stub and every statement so you can check the math and know when the balance should hit zero.

Can I quit my job to stop a wage garnishment?

Quitting stops the garnishment from your paycheck because there is no paycheck to garnish, but it does not stop the debt. The creditor can garnish your next job's wages, levy your bank account, or seize other property, and the debt keeps growing with interest and fees while you are unemployed. Quitting also costs you the income you need to negotiate a payment plan or pay for basic needs. It is almost never the right move on its own. If you are thinking about it, talk to a legal aid attorney first, because the trade is a short pause in the garnishment for a longer and harder debt problem.

Can they garnish my bank account too?

Yes. A creditor with a judgment can get a bank levy, which is a separate order that freezes and takes money from your bank account, in addition to or instead of a wage garnishment. Some money in a bank account is protected by law, such as Social Security benefits and certain other federal benefits, but the protection is not automatic in every state, and commingling protected money with other money can make it harder to prove. If you get a bank levy notice, act fast, because the bank may freeze the account before you can move the money. A claim of exemption can protect exempt funds, but it has a short deadline.

What income is exempt from garnishment?

Federal law protects some income from ordinary creditor garnishment. Social Security benefits, Supplemental Security Income, veterans benefits, and federal retirement benefits generally cannot be garnished by a regular creditor, though the IRS, child support, and federal student loan collectors can reach some of them. The federal limits also protect a floor of your wages: the first $217.50 per week of disposable earnings is protected from ordinary creditor garnishment at the current federal minimum wage. Many states add their own exemptions, such as a higher protected amount or a head-of-household exemption. You have to claim the exemption with the court. It is not automatic.

Is this legal advice?

No. This guide is general information about how wage garnishment works under federal law. Garnishment rules vary by state, deadlines are short, and the right steps depend on your debt, your income, and your court papers. For anything that could affect your paycheck or your case, talk to a legal aid attorney or a consumer debt attorney before your deadline passes. Many offer a free first consultation, and legal aid offices help for free if your income qualifies.
