How to Write a Performance Review Rebuttal Letter

A bad review does not have to stand unchallenged. A written rebuttal corrects the record point by point, with evidence, and gets attached to your personnel file next to the review. Here is what to dispute, what evidence wins, the exact structure, and a full sample letter.

By CyberScryb 📅 Updated November 7, 2026 ⏱ 9 min read
⚡ Key Takeaways (TL;DR)

You can dispute a performance review in writing at most employers, and the rebuttal usually gets attached to your personnel file next to the review. Check your handbook first: many companies give you only 5 to 10 business days to file. Sign the review to acknowledge receipt, but write "I disagree and will submit a rebuttal" if the form lets you. Dispute facts, not opinions: quote each claim, answer it with dated evidence, and leave out anything you cannot prove. Ask for a specific outcome, a corrected statement, a reconsidered rating, or at minimum that the letter be attached to your file. Keep it to one or two pages, professional throughout, and send it by email to HR and your manager so you have a dated record.

This guide is general information, not legal advice. Employment rules vary by state and by employer policy; confirm the process in your employee handbook, and talk to an employment lawyer if the review involves discrimination, retaliation, or termination.

Step 1: Do Not React in the Meeting

The worst rebuttal is the one written angry, in the meeting, out loud. A review meeting is not the place to win the argument. It is the place to listen, take notes, and find out exactly what is in writing. Arguing on the spot gives your manager a second thing to write down: that you got defensive.

  • Listen and take notes. Write down the exact claims, the examples your manager cites, and any rating that surprises you. Ask clarifying questions, not combative ones: "Can you give me the dates on that?" and "Which project are you referring to?"
  • Ask for a copy. You cannot rebut what you cannot read. Get the full written review, including any numeric ratings and the comments section, before you write anything.
  • Do not refuse to sign outright. At most companies, your signature means you received the review, not that you agree with it. Read the form. If it says "signature acknowledges receipt," sign it. If there is a comments box, write "I disagree with portions of this review and will submit a written rebuttal." If the form says you agree by signing, do not sign it as written, and tell HR why in writing.

Step 2: Check Your Handbook and the Deadline

Before you write a word, find the rules. Most mid-size and large employers spell out a rebuttal or appeal process in the employee handbook, and it usually has a deadline. Common windows are 5 to 10 business days after the review meeting, though some allow 30 days. A few employers have no formal process at all, which does not mean you cannot respond; it means you submit your letter to HR and ask for it to be filed with the review.

  • Find the deadline and the recipient. Some companies want the rebuttal sent to your manager first, some to HR, some to both. Send it to the right place or it may never reach your file.
  • Check for a formal appeal too. A rebuttal corrects the record. An appeal asks a higher level to change the rating. They are different processes, and some employers offer both. If the rating itself is wrong, ask whether an appeal exists and what its deadline is.
  • Note whether your state gives you file rights. In many states, employees have a legal right to inspect their personnel file and, in some, to add a written response to it. Your handbook or your state labor agency can confirm what applies to you.

Step 3: Separate Facts You Can Disprove From Opinions You Cannot

A rebuttal works when it corrects the record. It fails when it reads like a complaint about your manager. Go through the review line by line and sort every claim into one of three buckets before you write.

  • Wrong facts. Dates, numbers, project names, deadlines, attendance records. These are the strongest disputes, because they can be proven. "The review says I missed the March 15 deadline. The project shipped March 12, and here is the delivery confirmation." Lead with these.
  • Incomplete facts. True but missing context. "I missed the deadline because the client changed the scope on March 10 and the new date was approved by email." This is the second strongest bucket. Add the missing half of the story, with the document that proves it.
  • Opinions and judgments. "Needs to show more initiative" or "Not a team player." You cannot disprove a vague opinion, and arguing with it directly makes you look defensive. Answer opinions with counter-examples instead: list specific times you took initiative, with dates and outcomes, and let the reader draw the conclusion.

Drop anything you cannot support. One provable correction builds your credibility; one claim that falls apart on checking destroys it. If a statement is unfair but you have no evidence either way, leave it out and spend the space on the points you can win.

Step 4: Gather the Evidence Before You Write

A rebuttal without evidence is an opinion. A rebuttal with evidence is a correction. Collect your proof before drafting, so the letter writes itself around the documents.

  • Emails and messages. Approvals, praise from clients or other managers, deadline changes, assignment emails that show what you were actually asked to do.
  • Numbers. Sales figures, tickets closed, on-time delivery rates, error rates, customer scores, anything measured. A number beats an adjective every time.
  • Dates and timelines. When the work happened, when the goal changed, when you were out on leave, when your manager was on vacation and someone else was covering. Reviews often get dates wrong, and a timeline is easy to verify.
  • Witnesses. Coworkers, clients, or other managers who saw the work. Name them in the letter if they are willing to be named; do not name someone who asked not to be involved.
  • Your own records. Your self-evaluation, your goal sheet, the job description you were actually working against. If the review judges you against goals that were never set, or a job description that changed, that is a dispute worth making.

Keep copies of everything off company systems. If you leave the job, you lose access to your work email and files, and the evidence goes with it. Forward what you need to a personal email, or print it, following your company policy on confidential information. Do not take customer data, trade secrets, or anything your employment agreement bars you from copying.

Step 5: Write the Letter

One to two pages. No more. A five-page rebuttal does not get read carefully, and a five-page rebuttal that attacks the manager does not get read sympathetically. The structure that works:

  1. Header and opening. Your name, job title, department, date, and the review period you are responding to. One opening paragraph: "I am writing to rebut portions of my performance review for [period], dated [date]. I respectfully disagree with the following statements, and I am providing evidence for each."
  2. Point by point, in the review's own order. Quote the review's words, then answer them. "The review states: [quote]. In fact: [correction], as shown by [evidence]." Number each point so HR can match your response to the review. Address the three or four strongest points first if the review is long; do not try to answer every sentence.
  3. What the review left out. One short section for accomplishments and context the review ignored: extra projects, covering for a vacancy, results above target, positive feedback from clients. Keep it factual, not a second self-evaluation.
  4. The ask. Say plainly what you want: correction of the specific statements, reconsideration of the rating, or, at minimum, that this letter be attached to the review in your personnel file. A letter with no ask reads like a complaint. A letter with a clear ask reads like a professional document.
  5. Close and sign. One sentence thanking the reader for considering the response, your signature, your name, and your contact information. Attach or list your evidence by number so the reader can match each document to the point it supports.

Tone rules that matter more than wording: write about the work, not the person. "The deadline was March 12" is a correction. "My manager is biased and unfair" is an accusation, and it hands HR a reason to dismiss the whole letter. Never threaten, never call anyone a liar, and never mention a lawyer in the first letter. If the situation later needs a lawyer, the calm letter you already filed is a much better exhibit than an angry one.

Full Sample Rebuttal Letter

Copy this structure and replace every bracketed item with your own facts. The example is for a fictional employee disputing two points in a review:

Performance Review Rebuttal Letter Example

[Your full name]
[Job title], [Department]
[Date]

To: [Manager name] and Human Resources
Re: Rebuttal to Performance Review, [review period], dated [date]

I am writing to respectfully rebut portions of my performance review for [review period], dated [date]. I disagree with the statements below and am providing evidence for each. I request that this letter be attached to my review in my personnel file.

1. Missed deadlines. The review states that I "repeatedly missed deadlines in Q3." In fact, of the four deadlines cited, three were met on time. The March 15 deadline for [project] was moved to March 22 by [manager or client name] in an email dated March 10 (Attachment 1), and the work was delivered March 20. The April 2 deadline was met April 1 (Attachment 2). The one deadline I did miss, May 9, was delayed because [one-sentence factual reason, for example: the client changed the requirements on May 6]. I notified [manager name] the same day.

2. Communication. The review states that I "fail to keep the team informed." In fact, I sent weekly status updates to the team every Friday from January through June (Attachment 3), and I presented the project status at the March and May team meetings. If there are specific instances where communication fell short, I would welcome the details so I can address them.

Work not reflected in the review. During this period I also took on [extra work, for example: covering the [role] vacancy from February to April] and completed [result, for example: the client onboarding that came in two weeks early]. Neither appears in the review, and I ask that it be noted.

I am requesting that the statement about repeated missed deadlines be corrected to reflect the record above, and that my overall rating be reconsidered. At a minimum, I ask that this letter be attached to my review in my personnel file.

Thank you for considering this response. I remain committed to my role and to working with [manager name] on clear goals for the next review period.

Sincerely,
[Signature]
[Printed name]

Attachments: 1. Email, [date], deadline change. 2. Delivery confirmation, [date]. 3. Weekly status updates, January to June.

Notice the pattern: quote, correct, prove. Every disputed point follows the same three moves, and nothing in the letter is an accusation. That is what makes it fileable, and what makes it hold up if someone reads the file a year later.

Step 6: Submit It, Then Follow Up

How you submit matters as much as what you write. A letter handed over in person with no copy and no record can vanish. A letter sent by email leaves a trail.

  • Send it by email to HR and your manager. Attach the letter as a PDF and paste the text in the email body. Ask explicitly, in the email, for the letter to be attached to your performance review in your personnel file.
  • Keep your own copy off company systems. Save the sent email and the attachments to a personal device. If you ever need to prove the rebuttal was filed, the sent email is your proof.
  • Follow up in one week. A short email: "Confirming my rebuttal letter dated [date] has been attached to my review." If HR confirms, save that reply. If HR does not reply, follow up again and keep the record.
  • Confirm it is in the file. If your state gives you the right to inspect your personnel file, use it. A rebuttal that was never filed is a rebuttal that never happened.

What Happens After You File

Set expectations honestly. A rebuttal rarely flips a rating on the spot. What it usually does is narrower and still valuable: the inaccurate statement is corrected or noted, the rating is reviewed by HR or a higher-level manager, or nothing changes now but your side of the story sits in the file next to the review. That last outcome matters more than it feels like in the moment, because promotion, transfer, and termination decisions are made from the file, not from memory.

If the rating does change, get the corrected review in writing. If it does not, and you believe the review was retaliation for reporting harassment, discrimination, or a safety issue, or that it was based on a protected characteristic, that is a different situation from a disagreement about a rating. Document it, tell HR in writing, and talk to an employment lawyer before your next step. Mistakes That Backfire

  • Writing it angry. A letter written the same night as the review meeting is almost always too hot. Sleep on it, then cut every sentence that attacks the manager instead of correcting the record.
  • Disputing everything. A rebuttal that fights every line looks like a refusal to hear feedback. Pick the three or four points that are wrong and provable. Conceding a fair point, briefly, makes the disputed points more credible.
  • Claims with no evidence. "I did great work all year" is not a rebuttal. A date, a number, or a document is.
  • Refusing to sign the review. At many companies this gets written up as insubordination and becomes a second problem. Sign to acknowledge receipt, note your disagreement in writing, and file the rebuttal.
  • Missing the deadline. A late rebuttal may not be accepted at all, and a rebuttal filed after a raise or promotion decision is already made has nothing left to influence.
  • Threatening legal action in the first letter. It escalates a record dispute into an adversarial one, and HR stops talking to you and starts talking to its lawyers. Keep the first letter professional. If you need a lawyer, get one, but do not use the threat as leverage in the rebuttal itself.
  • Checking out afterward. The rebuttal corrects the past. Your work after it determines the next review. Meet the goals, document the results, and do not give the next review a reason to repeat the last one.

FAQ

Can you dispute a performance review?

Yes, in most workplaces. Many employers have a formal rebuttal process, and even where they do not, you can usually submit a written response that HR attaches to your personnel file next to the review. A rebuttal rarely changes the rating on its own, but it corrects the written record, which is what future promotion, transfer, and termination decisions are based on. Check your employee handbook for the exact procedure and deadline first, because some companies give you only 5 to 10 business days.

Should I sign a performance review I disagree with?

Signing usually means you received the review, not that you agree with it, but read the form before you sign. If there is a comments box, write "I disagree with portions of this review and will submit a written rebuttal by [date]." If there is no comments box, ask for a copy and submit your rebuttal separately. Refusing to sign outright can be written up as insubordination at some companies, so signing with a written disagreement noted is usually the safer move. Never sign a statement that says you agree if you do not.

How long do I have to write a performance review rebuttal?

It depends on your employer. Many companies set a deadline of 5 to 10 business days after the review meeting, and some allow 30 days. Your employee handbook or HR policy is the source that controls. If no deadline is stated, submit within one to two weeks while the review is fresh and before the rating is used for a raise, bonus, or promotion decision. Waiting months weakens the rebuttal and can make it look like an afterthought.

Does a rebuttal letter go in my personnel file?

In most cases, yes, if you submit it through HR or your manager and ask in writing for it to be attached to the review. That is the whole point of a rebuttal: the review stays, but your response sits next to it, so anyone who reads the file later sees both sides. Send it by email so you have a dated copy, and keep your own copy off company systems. In some states, employees also have a legal right to inspect their personnel file, which lets you confirm the rebuttal was actually filed.

Can I be fired for writing a performance review rebuttal?

Writing a professional, factual rebuttal is not itself a fireable offense at most employers, and many handbooks explicitly invite it. What gets people fired is the tone, not the act: accusations, threats, personal attacks on the manager, or refusing to do the work while the dispute is pending. Keep the letter calm, factual, and focused on the record. If you believe the review is retaliation for reporting harassment, discrimination, or a safety issue, say so plainly to HR and consider talking to an employment lawyer, because retaliation is protected in a way a disagreement about a rating is not.

What should be included in a performance review rebuttal letter?

Four things. First, the facts you dispute, quoted from the review, one at a time. Second, the evidence that proves each one wrong or incomplete: dates, emails, metrics, project results, and witness names. Third, the accomplishments and context the review left out, such as extra work you took on or goals that changed mid-year. Fourth, a clear ask: correct the specific statement, reconsider the rating, or at minimum attach the letter to your file. Close with your name, date, and signature.

What if my manager will not accept my rebuttal?

Take it to HR. A manager can disagree with your rebuttal, but HR is the office that owns the personnel file, and most companies require HR to attach a written employee response. Submit it by email to HR, state that you are requesting it be attached to your performance review dated [date], and keep the sent email. If HR also refuses, check your handbook for a formal grievance or appeal process. If none exists, keep the letter and your delivery records anyway, because the paper trail matters if the review is later used in a termination or a legal dispute.
