How to Upgrade a Military Discharge

Direct answer: a discharge upgrade changes the characterization on your DD-214, for example from other than honorable to general under honorable conditions. You apply on a DD-293 to your service's Discharge Review Board if you separated less than 15 years ago, or on a DD-149 to the Board for Correction of Military Records if more than 15 years have passed or your discharge came from a general court-martial. The board decides on three grounds: inequity, impropriety, or clemency. Your packet wins or loses on records: the DD-214, the service record, treatment and employment proof, and a personal statement that names its grounds and proves them.

Step 1: Get your records before you write anything

A discharge upgrade is decided on paper, and the board reads your service record before it reads your argument. Before you write a word of your personal statement, get the documents the packet is built from:

  • Your DD-214. The characterization, the separation date, the separation code, and the narrative reason for separation all live here. If the separation code looks wrong, note it: a wrong code is itself an impropriety argument.
  • Your full service record. Request it through the National Personnel Records Center (eVetRecs) if you do not already have it. The board will pull it anyway, so read it first and know what is in it before you file.
  • Service treatment records. Medical and mental health records from service, including any PTSD, depression, or substance use treatment, or the lack of it when you asked for help.
  • Performance evaluations and awards. These are your proof of good service, and they matter most when the discharge followed a short bad stretch at the end of an otherwise solid record.
  • Counseling statements, the Article 15 or court-martial paperwork, and the separation paperwork. The board wants to see what the command actually documented, not your memory of it.

If a record is missing, say so in the packet and say what you did to get it. A gap you name and explain is better than a gap the board discovers.

Step 2: The 15-year line decides the form

There are two forms and two boards, and the separation date picks between them:

  • DD-293, Discharge Review Board. For separations less than 15 years ago. Each service has its own board, and it reviews the discharge for inequity, impropriety, or clemency.
  • DD-149, Board for Correction of Military Records. For separations more than 15 years ago, or for a discharge from a general court-martial at any age. The Discharge Review Board cannot review a general court-martial discharge, so the DD-149 is the only route for those cases.

The 15-year line runs from your separation date, which is on your DD-214. Filing the wrong form wastes months: the wrong board will send it back or forward it, and the clock on your case does not start until the right board has it. Confirm the form with the board or an accredited VSO representative before you file, and use the packet builder's form check to do the date math from your separation date.

Step 3: Pick your grounds, and argue one clearly

Boards decide on three grounds. Most strong packets argue one ground clearly and prove it with records, rather than arguing all three weakly:

  • Inequity. The discharge was unfair given the circumstances. The classic case is a single incident, or a short bad stretch, ending an otherwise good record: years of good evaluations, then one mistake. The argument is proportion, and the proof is the record before the incident.
  • Impropriety. An error was made, or a rule was not followed, in the discharge process. The wrong separation code on the DD-214, a required step that was skipped, a regulation the command did not follow. The argument is the paper trail, and the proof is the regulation next to the record that shows it was not followed.
  • Clemency. What has changed since service warrants a second look. Sobriety, completed treatment, education, steady employment, family, community service. The argument is who you are now, and the proof is documents: treatment completion letters, employment records, certificates, and character letters.

Behavior connected to untreated PTSD, depression, or substance use sits across all three grounds: it can make the discharge inequitable, it can show the command missed something it should have caught, and it is the start of a clemency case when treatment since service has worked. Name the ground, then prove it. A packet that says “the discharge was unfair” without records is a request, not a case.

Step 4: Gather the evidence that fits your grounds

Evidence is not a pile. It is the proof for the grounds you picked, in the order the board reads it:

  • For inequity: evaluations and awards from before the incident, character letters from people who served with you, and anything showing the incident was out of character.
  • For impropriety: the separation paperwork, the counseling statements, the investigation or court-martial record, and the rule or regulation the command was supposed to follow.
  • For clemency: treatment completion records, sobriety or counseling records, education certificates, employment records showing steady work, tax returns or pay stubs, community service records, and character letters from employers, family, or community members.
  • Buddy statements. Signed statements from people who served with you fill gaps the record leaves: what the command climate was, what you actually did, and what happened that the paperwork never captured. A specific, dated statement beats a general one.

Label every exhibit and list them in the packet in the order you cite them. The board reads dozens of packets; a packet it can follow is a packet it finishes.

Step 5: Write the personal statement like a story, not a complaint

The personal statement is the only part of the packet in your voice, and it is the part the board remembers. Keep it short, factual, and in first person:

  • Who you were in service. What you did, where you served, what your record looked like before the discharge. Two or three sentences.
  • What happened. The events behind the discharge, in order, without blaming and without minimizing. If you made a mistake, say so. Boards trust veterans who own their part.
  • What has changed. Treatment, sobriety, school, work, family. Dates and specifics, not adjectives.
  • What you are asking for. The new characterization you want, and which grounds support it. One sentence.

Do not invent facts, do not exaggerate, and do not write what you think the board wants to hear. The statement is checked against the record, and a statement the record contradicts damages the whole packet. Step 6: File, and know what happens next

Send the packet to the board for your service, at the address on the form, and keep a copy of everything you send. Most boards offer two tracks: a records review on the papers only, or a personal appearance hearing where you present your case in person. A hearing lets you speak and answer questions, and it can add months to the timeline.

Expect the case to take many months, and a year or more is not unusual. The board has to pull your full service record and work through a large caseload. While you wait, do not stop building the record: new treatment records, new employment, new character letters can be added, and a packet that keeps improving is a packet worth filing.

If the Discharge Review Board denies your DD-293, you can apply to the Board for Correction of Military Records on a DD-149, which can review the same discharge and can consider new evidence. Read the denial letter first: it usually says what was missing, and that tells you what to fix in the next filing. If the Board for Correction of Military Records denies you, a federal court can review whether the board followed its own process, but that step needs an attorney.

Step 7: Treat the VA benefits question as its own step

An other than honorable discharge blocks most VA benefits while it stands, which is why an upgrade matters. But an upgrade does not automatically restore benefits: the VA makes its own character-of-discharge determination for benefit eligibility, separate from the military review boards, and some upgraded characterizations still need a VA decision. After an upgrade, ask the VA or an accredited representative how your new characterization affects the specific benefit you need, rather than assuming the upgrade settled it.

Step 8: Get free help before you file

Accredited VSO representatives from the American Legion, VFW, DAV, and your state veterans agency help veterans file these applications for free. They have filed hundreds of them, they know what each board wants to see, and they will review your packet before you send it. An attorney is worth considering when the case is complicated, such as a court-martial discharge or a case that was already denied once. Bring your helper a finished draft, not a blank form: the review goes faster, and the packet comes back better.

Frequently asked questions

What is a military discharge upgrade?

A discharge upgrade is a change to the characterization of your discharge on your DD-214, for example from other than honorable to general under honorable conditions. It is decided by a Discharge Review Board on a DD-293 application, or by a Board for Correction of Military Records on a DD-149 application. The board looks at whether the discharge was inequitable or improper, or whether clemency is warranted, based on the records and the evidence you submit. This is general information, not legal advice.

DD-293 vs DD-149: which form do I use for a discharge upgrade?

Use DD-293 if you separated less than 15 years ago: it goes to your service's Discharge Review Board. Use DD-149 if more than 15 years have passed since your separation, or if your discharge came from a general court-martial: it goes to your service's Board for Correction of Military Records. The 15-year line runs from your separation date. Confirm the right form with the board or an accredited veterans service organization before you file.

What are the grounds for a discharge upgrade?

There are three grounds. Inequity means the discharge was unfair given the circumstances, such as a single incident ending an otherwise good record. Impropriety means an error was made or a rule was not followed in the discharge process. Clemency means what has changed since service, such as sobriety, treatment, education, or steady employment, warrants a second look. Most strong packets argue one ground clearly and prove it with records, rather than arguing all three weakly.

Can I upgrade a dishonorable discharge?

A dishonorable discharge from a general court-martial cannot be reviewed by a Discharge Review Board, so a DD-293 is not the route. Those cases go to the Board for Correction of Military Records on a DD-149. Other characterizations, including other than honorable and bad conduct from a special court-martial, can be reviewed by a Discharge Review Board within 15 years of separation. Whether any discharge can be upgraded depends on the facts and the record, and no tool can predict the outcome. An accredited veterans service organization or an attorney can review your DD-214 and tell you which route fits.

How long does a discharge upgrade take?

Discharge review cases commonly take many months, and a year or more is not unusual, because the board has to pull your full service record and work through a large caseload. Some boards offer a records review on the papers only, and some offer a personal appearance hearing, which can add time. File as soon as your packet is ready rather than waiting for a perfect packet, and keep copies of everything you send. This is general information, not legal advice.

What evidence helps a discharge upgrade application?

The strongest evidence is paper that shows what happened and who you are now: your DD-214, service treatment records, performance evaluations, awards, counseling statements, the separation paperwork, and any investigation or court-martial records. For clemency, add proof of what changed since service: steady employment, education, treatment records, sobriety or counseling records, community service, and character letters from employers, family, or community members. Buddy statements from people who served with you can fill gaps the record leaves.

Does a discharge upgrade restore VA benefits?

An upgrade can help, but it does not automatically restore benefits. The VA makes its own character-of-discharge determination for benefit eligibility, separate from the military review boards, and some upgraded characterizations still need a VA decision. An other than honorable discharge blocks most VA benefits while it stands, which is why an upgrade matters, but treat the VA question as its own step: ask the VA or an accredited representative how your new characterization affects the specific benefit you need. This is general information, not legal advice.

What happens if my discharge upgrade is denied?

A denial is not necessarily the end. If a Discharge Review Board denies your DD-293, you can apply to the Board for Correction of Military Records on a DD-149, which can review the same discharge and can also consider new evidence. If the Board for Correction of Military Records denies you, a federal court can review the board's decision, but the court looks at whether the board followed its own process rather than re-deciding the facts, so that step needs an attorney. Before you appeal anything, read the denial letter: it usually says what was missing, and that tells you what to fix in the next filing.

Do I need a lawyer for a discharge upgrade?

No. Many veterans file with help from an accredited veterans service organization representative, such as the American Legion, VFW, DAV, or your state veterans agency, and that help is free. A representative who has filed many of these cases knows what each board wants to see and can review your packet before you send it. An attorney is worth considering when the case is complicated, such as a court-martial discharge or a case that was already denied once. This is general information, not legal advice.
