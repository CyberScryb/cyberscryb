How to Write a Goodwill Letter to Remove a Late Payment

A goodwill letter asks your creditor, not the credit bureaus, to remove an accurate late payment as a courtesy. It works best for one isolated slip-up on an otherwise spotless account. Here is when to send one, the exact anatomy of the letter, what to leave out, and a copy-paste template.

By CyberScryb 📅 Updated November 3, 2026 ⏱ 8 min read
⚡ Key Takeaways (TL;DR)

A goodwill letter is a short, polite ask to the creditor that reported a late payment: remove it anyway, as a favor. It is not a dispute. Disputes only fix errors, and this letter admits the late payment was accurate. Your odds are best when it is your only late payment, the account is current, you have years of on-time history, and you can name a specific one-time reason that is now behind you. Keep it under 300 words, ask for removal from all three bureaus by name, and mail it to the creditor's customer service or credit reporting department. Expect an answer in 30 to 60 days. Some large lenders (Chase, Bank of America) publicly refuse goodwill requests; most others decide case by case.

Step 1: Confirm the Late Payment Is Actually Accurate

Before you write a goodwill letter, pull your free credit reports and look at the late payment with your own eyes. The whole premise of a goodwill letter is that the creditor reported correctly and you are asking for mercy anyway. If the late payment is wrong, in the wrong month, belongs to a different account, or was never actually late, you do not want goodwill. You want a formal dispute, which carries real legal rights under the Fair Credit Reporting Act and a much better success rate than a favor.

This distinction is the one most people get backwards. A dispute says the entry is wrong. A goodwill letter says the entry is right and asks for it to be removed anyway, and only the lender that reported it can grant that. Sending a dispute to the bureaus about an accurate late payment goes nowhere; sending a goodwill letter to the bureaus goes nowhere too, because they cannot grant goodwill. Creditor gets the goodwill letter. Bureaus get disputes. Never the reverse.

Step 2: Know When Not to Send One

Goodwill letters have a narrow window where they work. Save your stamp if any of these are true:

  • It is a pattern, not a slip. Several late payments across months or years have very low success rates. Creditors grant goodwill for isolated incidents surrounded by good behavior, not for chronic lateness.
  • The account is still past due. A creditor will not do a favor for an account that is behind right now. Get current first; that is step 3.
  • The delinquency was severe. A single 30-day late has the best odds. 90-plus-day lates, charge-offs, and collections are rarely removed on goodwill.
  • The lender publicly refuses. Chase and Bank of America have stated publicly that they do not honor goodwill requests, citing their duty under the Fair Credit Reporting Act to report accurately. Freedom Mortgage has been cited for the same policy. If your late payment is with one of these, the letter is almost certainly dead on arrival.
  • You cannot name a reason. "I just forgot" is honest, but a specific one-time circumstance that is now resolved (a hospitalization, a job loss, an autopay that broke when you switched banks) gives the reviewer something to hang the yes on.

Step 3: Get the Account Current First

No creditor grants a goodwill removal on an account that is still delinquent. Before the letter goes anywhere, bring the account to a zero past-due balance and make sure the next payment is on schedule. If you are juggling several accounts, pay this one current first and build a plan for the rest; a reviewer who sees a current account with a clean stretch of payments after the incident is looking at exactly the profile goodwill is for.

Turn on autopay while you are at it. It costs nothing, it prevents the next late, and mentioning that you did so in the letter tells the reviewer the problem is structurally fixed, not just verbally regretted.

Step 4: The Letter Anatomy

Keep the letter under 300 words. Sincere, specific, and short beats long and dramatic. Include these parts, in this order:

  • Your header and theirs. Your name, address, the date, and the creditor's name and address. Put the account number and a subject line like "Re: Account ending in 4417 — Goodwill Adjustment Request" so it lands with the right team.
  • The exact late payment. Name the month and year and how late it was (30-day, 60-day). Specifics show you did your homework and make the removal easy to execute.
  • One sentence of ownership. Take full responsibility. Do not blame the creditor, the mail, or the system. "This was my error" is the whole sentence.
  • One sentence of cause. Briefly state what happened, without arguing or oversharing. A hospital stay, a layoff, a broken autopay. Then note that the circumstance is resolved.
  • Your payment history as evidence. How many years you have been a customer, your on-time record before and after the incident, and that the account is current now. This is the paragraph that does the persuading.
  • The specific ask. Ask plainly for removal of the late payment from your credit reports with all three bureaus: Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion. Name them; a vague "please fix my credit" is easy to file and forget.
  • Contact information. Phone and email so the reviewer can reach you without friction.

Two things to leave out on purpose. First, do not cite FCRA dispute statutes. Citing the dispute law signals that you believe there is an inaccuracy, which converts the letter into a dispute about an accurate item, and it fails. Second, no legal threats, no ultimatums, no talk of closing the account in protest. You are asking for a favor. Write like it.

Step 5: The Complete Example Goodwill Letter

Here is a full example for a fictional account. Copy the structure and swap in your own facts. Note how short it is; every sentence earns its place:

Goodwill Letter Template

[Your Full Name]
[Your Address]
[City, State, ZIP]
[Date]

[Creditor Name] — Customer Service / Credit Reporting Department
[Creditor Address]
[City, State, ZIP]

Re: Account ending in [XXXX] — Goodwill Adjustment Request

Dear Customer Service Team,

I am writing to request a goodwill adjustment to remove the 30-day late payment reported on my account for [Month, Year]. I take full responsibility for this late payment. At the time, I was [briefly explain the circumstance, for example: "in the hospital for two weeks" or "between jobs after a layoff"], and the payment slipped through the cracks. That situation is fully resolved.

I have been your customer since [Year], with an otherwise perfect payment record before and since this incident. The account is current, I have enabled autopay so it will not happen again, and I have continued to make every payment on time.

I understand you are under no obligation to make this adjustment. I am asking, respectfully, that you remove this late payment from my credit reports with Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion as a one-time courtesy.

Thank you for your time and consideration. I value my relationship with [Creditor Name] and plan to remain a customer for years to come.

Sincerely,
[Your Printed Name]
[Phone Number]
[Email Address]

Notice what the letter never does: it never argues the report was wrong, never cites a statute, and never threatens. It admits the facts, supplies the evidence of good behavior, and makes one clear ask. That is the entire strategy.

Step 6: Send It Right, Then Follow Up

Where the letter goes matters almost as much as what it says. Mail it to the creditor's credit reporting department or customer relations office, and consider the executive office for larger lenders; that is where someone with authority to approve an exception actually sits. Send it by certified mail with a return receipt so you have proof it arrived, and keep a copy of everything.

  • Wait 30 to 60 days. That is the typical window for a response from creditors that engage with goodwill requests.
  • One polite follow-up is fine. If you hear nothing after 60 days, a single follow-up call or letter is reasonable. Do not barrage them; the ask is discretionary and pestering does not help.
  • Verify the removal on all three reports. If approved, the removal can take another 30 to 45 days to appear. Pull your free reports and confirm the late payment is gone from every bureau that listed it.
  • A no costs you a stamp. If the answer is no, you have lost nothing. A yes removes a mark that can linger for up to seven years.

FAQ

Do goodwill letters actually work?

Sometimes. Creditors have no legal obligation to grant a goodwill request, but many do for a single isolated late payment on an account that is otherwise in excellent standing and currently paid up. Your odds are best with a long on-time history, a specific one-time reason that is now behind you, and a polite, short letter. It is a long shot, but a no costs you a stamp.

Should I send the goodwill letter to the credit bureaus or the creditor?

Send it to the creditor, never the credit bureaus. The bureaus can only correct inaccurate information; they cannot grant goodwill removals. Only the lender that reported the late payment can ask the bureaus to take it off. Send the letter to the creditor's customer service or credit reporting department.

What if the late payment on my report is wrong?

Then you do not want a goodwill letter at all. An inaccurate late payment is handled with a formal dispute under the Fair Credit Reporting Act, which gives you actual legal rights. A goodwill letter admits the late payment was accurate and asks for mercy anyway. Check your free credit reports first; if the entry is wrong, dispute it. Do not cite dispute statutes inside a goodwill letter, since that signals you believe there is an inaccuracy and converts the letter into a failed dispute.

Which creditors refuse goodwill requests?

Chase and Bank of America have publicly stated they do not honor goodwill requests, citing their obligation under the Fair Credit Reporting Act to report accurately. Freedom Mortgage has been cited for the same policy. Most other creditors decide case by case, which is why the letter is worth sending even though the outcome is never guaranteed.

How long does a goodwill letter take to work?

Creditors that agree to a goodwill removal typically respond within 30 to 60 days. If the request is approved, the removal can take another 30 to 45 days to appear on your credit reports. Pull your free credit reports afterward and confirm the late payment is gone from every bureau that listed it.

Can I send a goodwill letter for more than one late payment?

You can address each incident separately, but patterns of late payments have very low success rates. Goodwill letters work best for a single isolated slip-up surrounded by years of on-time payments. If lates are a pattern, fix the underlying payment problem first; the guide covers getting accounts current before you ask.

Will removing a late payment raise my credit score?

It can, because payment history is the largest factor in credit scoring. How much it helps depends on the rest of your profile: removing one recent 30-day late payment from an otherwise clean file moves the needle more than removing an old, minor mark from a file with bigger problems. Either way, removing an item that can linger for seven years is worth the stamp.
