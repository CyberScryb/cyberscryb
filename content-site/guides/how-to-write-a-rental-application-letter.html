How to Write a Rental Application Letter

What a rental cover letter is, when to include one, the exact structure that answers a landlord's three questions, and the mistakes that get applications ignored.

What a Rental Application Cover Letter Is (and When It Matters)

A rental application letter is a short cover letter you attach to your rental application. It introduces you as a person, summarizes why you are a reliable tenant, and gets ahead of any weak spots on your screening report. Landlords see a pile of near-identical applications; the letter is the one piece that has your voice in it.

It matters most when a human is making the decision: private landlords, small management companies, and mom-and-pop duplexes. When several applicants have similar credit and income, the letter is often the tiebreaker. Include one when:

  • The market is competitive. If listings get multiple applications in the first week, a letter puts a face on the paperwork.
  • You are applying to a private landlord. Individual owners rent to people, not scores. They read letters.
  • Your application has a weak spot. Thin credit, a job change, a gap in rental history, or pets all deserve a short, honest explanation before the landlord finds them on a report.
  • You are new to renting. First-time renters have no rental history. The letter is how you substitute character and documentation for a track record.

It is optional at large corporate complexes that run everything through automated scoring, but it rarely hurts to include one. If the landlord offers a "tell us about yourself" field in the application, that is the letter in disguise; treat it the same way.

The Structure That Works

Every effective rental letter follows the same six-part skeleton. Keep it to one page, three to five short paragraphs.

  1. Header: your contact details. Full name, phone number, and email at the top, plus the date. Add the property address you are applying for so the landlord knows exactly which unit the letter belongs to.
  2. Opening: name the property and your timeline. One or two sentences: "I am writing to apply for the two-bedroom unit at [address], with a preferred move-in date of [date]." Say who will be living there (you, a partner, a roommate) and why you want this specific place. One concrete detail, such as the commute or the neighborhood, shows you are serious about this unit, not blasting applications everywhere.
  3. Tenant pitch: why you are a safe bet. Two or three sentences on what makes you a good tenant. Reliable, quiet, tidy, handy around the house. Tie each claim to something verifiable: years at your current address, on-time rent, a landlord reference. Claims without evidence are noise.
  4. Rental history: where you have lived. Name your recent addresses, how long you stayed at each, and why you left. Long stays signal stability. If you are moving for a job, a growing family, or a shorter commute, say so. If there is a blemish, address it here briefly rather than hoping it goes unnoticed.
  5. Employment and income stability: proof you can pay. Your job title, employer, how long you have been there, and your income relative to the rent. Many landlords look for income around three times the monthly rent as a general norm. Mention that you can provide pay stubs, an offer letter, or bank statements on request.
  6. Closing: polite, verifiable, and brief. Thank the landlord for their consideration, invite them to contact your references or employer, and sign with your full name. Include your phone number again so it is right there when they decide to call.

What to Include vs What to Never Include

The line is simple: include facts that answer whether you can pay, will pay on time, and will care for the property. Everything else is risk.

Include: your name, phone, and email; the property address and desired move-in date; who will live in the unit; your job, employer, and tenure; income stated as a multiple of rent; rental history with durations; landlord references with phone numbers; a short honest explanation of any weak spot with what has changed; pets with species, breed, size, and temperament; any offer to strengthen the application, such as a larger deposit, guarantor, or renter's insurance.

Never include: your Social Security number or banking details (those belong only on the official application, never in a letter); sob stories or medical details; your race, religion, national origin, family plans, or any other detail a landlord may not legally consider under fair housing law (volunteering it can put both of you in an awkward spot); disparaging remarks about a previous landlord; promises you cannot keep, like "I will never be late" or offers to pay under the table; and anything untrue. Landlords verify. A single caught lie ends the application.

A note for first-time renters: no rental history is not a disqualifier if you substitute proof of responsibility. Employer letters, proof of savings, a guarantor, or an offer to prepay part of the rent all work. Say so plainly.

Sample Outline: Fill In Your Own Details

Copy this skeleton and replace every bracketed placeholder. Keep the tone warm, professional, and factual.

[Your Full Name]
[Your Phone Number] | [Your Email Address]
[Date]

Dear [Landlord Name or "Property Manager"],

I am writing to apply for the [unit description] at [Property Address], with a preferred move-in date of [Date]. The unit would be occupied by [you / you and your partner / you and one roommate]. I am drawn to this property because [one concrete reason: the commute, the neighborhood, the layout].

I am a [job title] at [Employer], where I have worked for [duration]. My gross monthly income is [amount], which is [multiple, e.g., 3.2x] the monthly rent, and I can provide pay stubs and bank statements on request.

I have lived at [Current Address] for [duration], where my rent is [amount] per month, paid on time. [Before that, I lived at / I am a first-time renter; if the latter, substitute: My employer and [reference name] can speak to my reliability.] [If a weak spot exists: one honest sentence of context, e.g., "My credit file is thin because I have only had a credit card for two years; my rent and utilities have been paid on time throughout."] [If pets: "I have a [age]-year-old [breed], [weight], who is [trained / quiet / house-trained]; my current landlord can confirm there have been no pet issues."]

[Previous landlord's Name] ([phone]) can confirm my tenancy and payment history. Thank you for your consideration; please feel free to contact me or any reference above with questions.

Sincerely,
[Signature]
[Your Printed Full Name]
[Phone Number]

Mistakes That Get Applications Ignored

  • Writing a novel. A two-page life story signals you do not respect the reader's time. Half a page to one page, max. Landlords skim long letters; the key details have to land fast.
  • Generic flattery with no facts. "I am a responsible person who loves your beautiful property" says nothing. Every claim needs a fact behind it: years at an address, income multiple, a reference's name and number.
  • Hiding the weak spot. Landlords run screening reports. The eviction, the thin credit file, the pet will come out. A letter that pretends everything is perfect loses all credibility when the report says otherwise. One honest sentence of context beats silence.
  • Leaving out the basics. No phone number, no property address, no move-in date. The landlord wants to know exactly which unit and exactly how to reach you. Missing basics make the letter useless as a document.
  • Tone problems: begging or demanding. Desperate language ("I am begging you to consider me") and entitled language ("I deserve this apartment") both backfire. Professional, confident, and brief is the register.
  • Sensitive details you were never asked for. Medical history, family planning, religion, politics. Some of these a landlord legally cannot consider, and volunteering them creates discomfort or liability for everyone involved.
  • Copy-paste form letters with the wrong address. Nothing ends a letter faster than addressing the wrong property. Proofread the property name, address, and landlord's name on every application.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do landlords actually read rental cover letters?

Private landlords and small property managers usually do, especially when choosing between similar applications. Large corporate leasing offices may skim or skip them in favor of screening scores. Either way, a strong letter costs you nothing and turns a stack of forms into a person with a story.

When should I include a rental application letter?

Include one whenever you are competing with other applicants, applying to a private landlord, have something on your screening report that needs context (a thin credit file, an old eviction, a job change), or own pets. It is optional for large complexes with strict scoring systems, but still rarely hurts.

What should a rental application letter include?

Your contact details, the property you are applying for and your desired move-in date, who will live there, a brief pitch on why you are a reliable tenant, your rental history, your employment and income stability, any context for weak spots on your application, and a polite close with an invitation to verify everything.

How long should a rental cover letter be?

One page at most, and half a page is usually enough. Landlords screen many applications; a tight letter with three or four short paragraphs gets read, while a long autobiography gets skipped. Stick to the facts that answer a landlord's three questions: can you pay, will you pay on time, and will you take care of the place.

Should I mention pets in my rental application letter?

Yes, mention them honestly. Landlords will find out anyway, and hiding a pet is one of the fastest ways to lose a lease or get evicted later. State the pet's species, breed, size, age, and temperament, and note any references from previous landlords about the pet. Offering a pet deposit or renter's insurance that covers pet liability helps.

Can a rental cover letter make up for bad credit?

It can provide context, but it cannot replace the numbers. A short, honest explanation of what caused the problem, what changed, and what you are offering now (a larger deposit, a guarantor, proof of recent on-time payments) gives a landlord a reason to take a second look. What it cannot do is override a landlord's hard minimum credit cutoff.
