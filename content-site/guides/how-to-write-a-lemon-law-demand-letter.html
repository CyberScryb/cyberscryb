How to Write a Lemon Law Demand Letter

A car that keeps breaking down is not just bad luck. A lemon law demand letter turns a stack of repair visits into a written claim the manufacturer has to answer. Here is the repair log that wins cases, the exact structure, what to demand, and a full sample letter.

By CyberScryb 📅 Updated November 8, 2026 ⏱ 9 min read
⚡ Key Takeaways (TL;DR)

A lemon law demand letter is the written claim you send the manufacturer when a new car has a serious defect the dealer cannot fix after a reasonable number of attempts while it is under warranty. The letter works only if the repair log behind it is solid: every visit dated, every repair order kept, every complaint written down the same way you told the dealer. Name one remedy, a repurchase, a replacement, or a refund, and give a specific response deadline, usually 30 days. Send it by certified mail with return receipt requested so you can prove when it arrived. Lemon laws vary by state, so check your state's thresholds and deadlines before you send anything. What a lemon law demand letter is

A lemon law demand letter is a formal written claim sent to the vehicle's manufacturer, not just the dealer, stating that the vehicle has a defect the dealer could not fix after a reasonable number of attempts while it was under warranty, and demanding a specific remedy: a repurchase, a replacement vehicle, or a refund. It is the step that turns months of repair visits into a claim with a deadline. The dealer is the one that tried to fix the car; the manufacturer is the one that owes you the remedy, which is why the letter goes to the manufacturer.

Most states require the manufacturer to get a final chance to fix the defect before a buyback or refund is on the table, and some require you to go through the manufacturer's arbitration program first. The letter is what starts that clock. A phone call does not. An email to the dealer does not. A dated letter sent by certified mail, with the repair log attached, is the record that the claim exists and when it was made.

Do you have a claim?

Before you write anything, check the four things that decide whether a lemon law claim exists at all. If any one of them is missing, the letter will not help, and you need a different approach.

  • The vehicle is covered. Most state lemon laws cover new vehicles, and some cover leased vehicles and demonstrators. Used cars are covered in only a few states, and usually under different rules. Check whether your state's law covers your purchase before you write.
  • The defect is substantial. A rattle in the door panel is not a lemon law defect. A defect that affects the use, value, or safety of the vehicle is: an engine that stalls, brakes that fail, a transmission that slips, an electrical fault that kills the car. The letter has to say, in plain terms, how the defect affects driving the car.
  • The dealer had a reasonable number of attempts. The dealer tried to fix the same defect more than once and could not, or the car spent a long stretch out of service. Your state sets the numbers; common patterns are three or four attempts for the same problem, or 30 days out of service, within a set period after delivery.
  • The defect appeared under warranty. The problem has to have shown up while the manufacturer's warranty was in force, and usually within a set time or mileage after delivery. A defect that first appears years later, after the warranty expired, is usually not a lemon law claim even if the car is genuinely unreliable.

The repair log: the part that wins cases

The letter is only as strong as the repair log behind it. Manufacturers and arbitrators decide lemon law claims on documents, not on how frustrated you are. Build the log before you write the letter, and attach it to the letter when you send it.

  • Every visit gets a repair order. Take the car in every time the problem happens, even if the dealer cannot reproduce it, and get a repair order for every visit. A visit with no fix still counts as a documented attempt in most states. If the dealer will not write one up, write your own dated note of the visit and keep it with the rest.
  • Record the same four facts every time. The date, the mileage, the problem exactly as you described it, and what the dealer wrote on the repair order. If the order says "could not duplicate" while you can reproduce the problem on demand, write that down too, with the conditions that trigger it.
  • Keep the paper. Every repair order, every invoice, every letter from the dealer or the manufacturer, every text or email about the problem. Photograph or scan the paper the day you get it; repair orders fade, and dealers lose files.
  • Count the days out of service. Add up every day the car was at the dealer or undriveable because of the defect, including days waiting for parts. Days out of service are a separate threshold in many states, and a car that spent a month in the shop is a stronger claim than one that went in four times for an hour.
  • Capture the problem if you can do it safely. A short video of the stall, the warning light, or the noise, with the date visible, is worth more than a paragraph of description. Do not film while driving.

The structure of the letter

A lemon law demand letter has seven parts. Keep it to two pages. The manufacturer reads hundreds of these; the ones that get answered are the ones that state the facts, name the remedy, and give a deadline.

  1. Header and identification. Your name and address, the date, the manufacturer's name and address, and the vehicle: year, make, model, VIN, purchase date, and current mileage. The VIN matters more than anything else in the header; it is how the manufacturer finds your file.
  2. The defect, stated plainly. One paragraph: what is wrong, when it first appeared, and how it affects the use, value, or safety of the vehicle. No adjectives about the dealer, no history of how angry you are. The defect is the subject, not the dealership.
  3. The repair history, dated. A table or a numbered list: each visit, the date, the mileage, what you reported, and what the dealer did. This is the core of the letter. If the same defect was reported four times and fixed zero times, the list says that without you having to argue it.
  4. The law, in general terms. One sentence naming your state's lemon law and stating that the vehicle meets its thresholds for repair attempts or days out of service. Do not cite statute numbers you have not verified, and do not state a legal conclusion the manufacturer has to accept. State the facts and let the numbers speak.
  5. The demand. One remedy, stated in one sentence: repurchase the vehicle, replace it with a comparable vehicle, or refund the purchase price. Say which one you want. A letter that asks for everything reads like it does not know what it wants.
  6. The deadline. A specific response date, usually 30 days from receipt. "Please respond within 30 days of receipt of this letter" is enough. A letter with no deadline gets filed; a letter with a deadline gets a calendar entry.
  7. Enclosures and signature. List the enclosed documents: the repair log, copies of the repair orders, the purchase agreement, the warranty. Sign it, date it, and keep a copy of the signed letter with everything else.

What to demand, and what a refund actually means

Most states reduce a repurchase or refund by a mileage offset for the miles you drove before the first repair attempt, so expect that deduction and do not treat the full purchase price as the number. The exact calculation varies by state and is set by your state's law, not by the dealer. Ask for the full purchase price including taxes, title, and registration fees in the letter, and expect the manufacturer to apply the offset in its response. If the manufacturer offers a replacement vehicle instead of a buyback, compare the replacement's value against the refund figure before you accept; a replacement is only a good deal if it is genuinely comparable.

Some states also require the manufacturer to pay your reasonable costs, such as towing or a rental car while the vehicle was out of service. List those costs in the letter with dates and receipts, as a separate line, not folded into the purchase price. Keep every receipt; a cost without a receipt is a cost you will not recover.

Sample lemon law demand letter

Adapt this to your facts. Replace every bracketed item with your own information, and delete any line that does not apply to your case. Do not send it with brackets still in it.

Sample letter

[Your full name]
[Your address]
[Your phone number]
[Your email]

[Date]

[Manufacturer name]
Customer Relations
[Manufacturer address]

Re: Demand under [your state] lemon law — [year, make, model], VIN [VIN number], purchased [purchase date]

Dear Sir or Madam:

I purchased the vehicle above on [purchase date] from [dealer name]. The vehicle has a defect that substantially impairs its use, value, and safety: [describe the defect in one or two sentences, for example, the engine stalls without warning at highway speed].

The defect first appeared on [date] at [mileage] miles. I have taken the vehicle to [dealer name] for repair on the following dates:

[Date, mileage] — reported [problem]; dealer [what they did].
[Date, mileage] — reported [same problem]; dealer [what they did].
[Date, mileage] — reported [same problem]; dealer [what they did].
[Date, mileage] — reported [same problem]; dealer [what they did].

The vehicle has been out of service for [number] days in total. The defect has not been repaired despite these attempts. Copies of the repair orders are enclosed.

Under the lemon law of [your state], I am requesting that you repurchase the vehicle for the full purchase price, including taxes, title, and registration fees, less any mileage offset required by law. Please respond within 30 days of receipt of this letter.

Enclosed: repair log, copies of repair orders dated [dates], purchase agreement, warranty documents.

Sincerely,
[Your full name]

How to send it

Send the letter by certified mail with return receipt requested, addressed to the manufacturer's customer relations or lemon law department, not to the dealer. The certified mail receipt and the green return card are your proof of when the manufacturer received the claim, and the 30-day deadline runs from receipt. Keep the receipt, the tracking number, and a copy of the signed letter together. If the manufacturer has an online claim portal, you can also file there, but the certified letter is the record that counts if there is ever a dispute about dates.

Do not send the originals of anything. Send copies of the repair orders and the purchase agreement, and keep the originals. If the manufacturer asks for the originals later, send them by certified mail too, and keep copies of what you sent.

What happens after you send it

Three things usually happen next, in this order. First, the manufacturer acknowledges the letter and asks for the repair records, or offers an inspection. Cooperate with the inspection and keep a record of it. Second, the manufacturer makes an offer, denies the claim, or refers you to its arbitration program. Read any offer against the refund figure in your letter before you accept it, and do not sign a release that gives up more than the claim is worth. Third, if the offer is too low or the claim is denied, the next step is usually arbitration under the manufacturer's program or a lemon law attorney, not a second letter. Many lemon law attorneys take these cases with fees paid by the manufacturer if you win, under fee-shifting rules that vary by state, so ask about fees before you sign anything.

Mistakes that backfire

  • Waiting too long. Lemon laws have deadlines measured from delivery, not from your last repair visit. A claim filed after the statutory window closes is a claim that does not exist, no matter how bad the defect is. Check your state's window before you do anything else.
  • Not taking the car in. A defect you never reported to the dealer is a defect with no repair attempts behind it. Take the car in every time, even when the dealer says there is nothing wrong, and get the repair order.
  • Threatening a lawsuit in the first letter. It turns a claim the manufacturer might settle into an adversarial one, and the letter stops being read by the people who can approve a buyback. State the facts, name the remedy, give a deadline. If you need a lawyer, get one, but do not use the threat as leverage in the letter itself.
  • Accepting a trade-in offer without reading it. A dealer may offer to take the car back as a trade toward another vehicle. That offer can include a release of your lemon law claim, and it usually pays far less than a buyback. Read the whole document before you sign, and compare the numbers against the refund figure first.
  • Modifying the car before the claim is resolved. Aftermarket parts, tuning, or repairs by an independent shop give the manufacturer an argument that the defect was caused by the modification. Keep the car as it is until the claim is resolved.
  • Sending the letter to the dealer only. The dealer cannot approve a buyback. The letter goes to the manufacturer, with a copy to the dealer if you want one. A letter the manufacturer never receives is a claim that was never made.

FAQ

What is a lemon law?

A lemon law is a state law that gives you a remedy when a new vehicle has a serious defect the dealer cannot fix after a reasonable number of attempts while it is under warranty. The usual remedies are a repurchase, a replacement vehicle, or a refund. Every state has its own version with its own thresholds, deadlines, and procedures, and the details vary widely. This guide is general information, not legal advice.

How many repair attempts before a car is a lemon?

There is no single number that applies everywhere. Many states use a presumption based on the number of repair attempts for the same defect, or the number of days the vehicle was out of service, within a set period after delivery. Common patterns are three or four attempts for the same problem, or 30 days out of service, but your state's law sets its own numbers and its own time window. Check your state's lemon law or your state attorney general's consumer page for the exact threshold that applies to you.

What if the dealer cannot reproduce the problem?

Take the car in every time the problem happens, even if the dealer cannot reproduce it, and get a repair order for every visit. A visit with no fix still counts as a documented attempt in most states, and a stack of repair orders showing the same complaint is the record that matters. Write down the date, the mileage, what you told them, and what they wrote on the order, and keep a video or photo of the problem happening if you can capture it safely.

Do I need a lawyer for a lemon law claim?

Not always. Many people start with a demand letter and the manufacturer's arbitration process, and a well-documented letter resolves some cases. Many lemon law attorneys take these cases with fees paid by the manufacturer if you win, under fee-shifting rules that vary by state, so ask about fees before you sign anything. If the manufacturer denies the claim, offers far less than a buyback, or the case involves a serious safety defect, talk to a lemon law attorney in your state before you accept anything or sign a release.

What if my car is out of warranty?

Most state lemon laws only cover vehicles still under the manufacturer's warranty, and the defect usually has to appear within a set time or mileage after delivery. If the warranty has expired, the lemon law may no longer apply, but other options can still exist: the federal Magnuson-Moss Warranty Act, a state deceptive-practices claim if the dealer misrepresented the car, or a claim under the warranty that was in force when the problem first appeared. A lemon law attorney in your state can tell you which of those fits your dates.

What should a lemon law demand letter ask for?

Name one specific remedy: a repurchase, a replacement vehicle, or a refund of the purchase price, and say which one you want. Most states reduce a repurchase or refund by a mileage offset for the miles you drove before the first repair attempt, so expect that deduction and do not treat the full purchase price as the number. State the facts, list the repair attempts with dates, and give the manufacturer a clear deadline to respond. Do not ask for several remedies at once; a letter that asks for everything reads like it does not know what it wants.

How long does the manufacturer have to respond?

It depends on your state's law and on the manufacturer's own process. Give a specific response deadline in the letter, usually 30 days from receipt, and send the letter by certified mail with return receipt requested so you can prove when it arrived. If there is no response by the deadline, the next step is usually the manufacturer's arbitration program or a lemon law attorney, not a second letter. Keep the certified mail receipt and the tracking number with the letter.

Is this legal advice?

No. This guide is general information about how lemon law demand letters work. Lemon laws vary by state, and the thresholds, deadlines, and remedies that apply to you depend on your state's law and your purchase documents. For anything that could affect your claim, review with a lemon law attorney in your state before you send the letter or accept an offer.
