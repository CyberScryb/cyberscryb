How to Answer Interview Questions

The STAR framework, the five questions you will definitely face, and the delivery details that separate offers from rejections.

By CyberScryb 📅 Updated October 7, 2026 ⏱ 9 min read
⚡ Key Takeaways (TL;DR)

Structure wins interviews: use STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result) for behavioral questions, keep answers to 60-90 seconds, and always close with a quantified result. The STAR Method, Explained

Situation: set the scene in one or two sentences — where, when, what was at stake. Task: what was your responsibility. Action: what you specifically did — this is 60% of your answer, and it must be "I," not "we." Result: the outcome, quantified whenever possible ("cut onboarding time 30%," "retained the $2M account").

Why it works: hiring managers are trained to score answers on exactly this shape. A STAR answer sounds confident because it is organized — and it prevents the rambling that kills most interviews.

The Five Questions You Will Definitely Face

1. "Tell me about yourself." Not your life story — a 60-second professional narrative: present role, relevant background, why this role now. End looking forward, not backward.

2. "Tell me about a time you failed." Pick a real failure, own it fast, spend most of the answer on what you changed. The lesson is the answer — nobody hires someone who has never failed, they hire someone who learns.

3. "Why do you want to work here?" Generic flattery fails. Name something specific — their product, a recent launch, the team’s reputation — and connect it to your skills. Research is the answer.

4. "What is your greatest weakness?" Name a real, non-fatal weakness and show active management: "I used to over-commit, so now I time-block and say no early." Never disguise a strength as a weakness — interviewers see through it instantly.

5. "Where do you see yourself in five years?" Show ambition inside their walls: growing toward the next level of this career path, not fleeing to another one.

Timing: The 90-Second Rule

Most answers should run 60 to 90 seconds — long enough for STAR, short enough to hold attention. Complex behavioral questions can stretch to two minutes. Practice with a timer: if you consistently run long, your Situation is too detailed. Cut setup ruthlessly; the Action is what they are scoring.

Delivery Details That Decide Offers

Slow down — nerves speed everyone up, and fast talk reads as anxious. Make eye contact (or look at the camera for video calls). Pause before answering hard questions; a two-second pause looks thoughtful, not lost. Ask clarifying questions when a question is ambiguous — it shows judgment. And always prepare two or three questions to ask them; "no questions" reads as disinterest.

Prepare Stories, Not Scripts

Build a bank of 6-8 STAR stories from your career: a conflict, a failure, a tight deadline, a difficult stakeholder, a proud win, a time you led without authority. Most behavioral questions map to one of these. Rehearse the beats — situation, your actions, the number — until you can tell each story naturally three different ways. Memorized scripts collapse under follow-up questions; internalized stories do not.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the STAR interview method?

Situation, Task, Action, Result — a framework for answering behavioral questions. Briefly set the scene, state your responsibility, detail your actions (the bulk of the answer), and close with a quantified outcome.

How should I answer "tell me about yourself"?

As a 60-second professional story: where you are now, the relevant path that got you here, and why this role is the logical next step. Present, past, future — in that order.

What questions should I ask the interviewer?

Ask about the team’s current challenges, what success looks like in 90 days, and why the role is open. Avoid questions answered on the careers page — they signal you did not research.

How do I handle a question I do not know the answer to?

Say so honestly, then think aloud: "I have not faced that exact situation, but here is how I would approach it..." Interviewers score reasoning under uncertainty, not omniscience.

Should I bring notes to an interview?

A one-page cheat sheet of your STAR story beats and questions to ask is fine — glancing at notes looks prepared, reading from them does not. For video interviews, keep them just off-camera.
