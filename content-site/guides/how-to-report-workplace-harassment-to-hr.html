How to Report Workplace Harassment to HR

Direct answer: put it in writing, stick to dated facts, and keep your own copy outside work systems. A verbal conversation with HR is a start, not a record. The letter that gets taken seriously is not the longest or the angriest one. It is the one that lets a stranger reconstruct what happened, when, where, who saw it, and what you are asking to happen next, without having to guess at anything.

One safety note first, stated plainly: if you feel unsafe right now, prioritize your immediate safety over perfect paperwork. You can document and write the formal complaint once you are in a safe place. This guide covers the written complaint itself, not emergency response.

Step 1: Decide whether a written complaint is the right move now

Not every unpleasant workplace interaction is harassment, and not every problem needs a formal complaint on day one. A written complaint is the right move when one or more of these are true:

  • The conduct is repeated, or a single incident was serious. A pattern matters, and so does severity. Either one is a reason to create a record, even if you are unsure how HR will classify it.
  • Informal attempts have not worked. If you asked for the behavior to stop, spoke to a supervisor, or raised it once and nothing changed, the next step is a paper trail, not a third informal chat.
  • It is affecting your work, health, or safety. Missed shifts, avoided meetings, changed duties to dodge someone, or dread about coming to work are all relevant impact, and they belong in the letter.
  • You may need the record later. If the situation could lead to a transfer, a disciplinary process, a leave request, or an outside complaint, a dated written report made at the time is far stronger than a reconstruction written months later.

You do not need to prove a legal label before writing. You need to describe what happened accurately and let HR, or a professional you consult, assess how it is classified. Do not hold back a factual complaint because you are unsure whether the conduct meets a particular legal definition. That uncertainty is normal, and it is not your job to resolve it in the letter.

Step 2: Build the incident record before you write the letter

Write the letter from notes, not from memory in the moment. For each incident, record these six items as soon as you safely can:

  • Date and time. As exact as you can make it.
  • Location. Room, floor, channel, or meeting name.
  • Who was involved. Full names and job titles where you know them.
  • What happened, in factual terms. What was said or done, quoted as closely as you remember it. Mark quotes you are sure of and paraphrase the rest; do not present a guess as an exact quote.
  • Witnesses. Who else was present or could confirm the incident, including people who saw messages or heard remarks secondhand only if you label that clearly.
  • Evidence. Emails, messages, schedules, photos, or documents, and where each one is stored.

Keep this record and a copy of every letter outside company systems: a personal email address, a personal device, or printed copies kept at home. Work email, work chat, and work devices can be cut off when employment changes, and a complaint that only exists inside the system you are complaining about is a complaint you may not be able to prove you made. See how to organize a paper trail for a fuller method. Do not take or copy material you are not entitled to hold, such as other employees' private records; your own notes, your own messages, and documents addressed to you are the core of the record.

Step 3: Structure the letter so HR can act on it

Use a standard business letter format and keep the whole letter to what HR needs to open an investigation. A workable structure:

  • Header and subject. Your name, job title, and department; the date; the recipient's name and title; and a subject line such as "Formal Complaint Regarding Workplace Harassment." If the complaint is about your supervisor or someone in HR, address it to the next level up or to the alternate reporting channel your employer provides, never to the person named in the complaint.
  • Opening paragraph. One or two sentences stating that you are filing a formal complaint, naming the person or people involved, and naming the general nature of the conduct. No backstory yet.
  • Incidents in date order. One short paragraph or bullet block per incident, each with its date, location, what happened, and any witnesses. Chronological order is easier to investigate than thematic order, and it shows a pattern without you having to argue that one exists.
  • Prior attempts to resolve it. Any earlier conversation, report, or request to stop, with dates. If you never raised it before, say so plainly and briefly explain why, for example that the person is your supervisor. Do not apologize for reporting late.
  • Impact on your work. Two or three factual sentences: duties avoided, meetings missed, schedule changes, or performance effects. Keep it tied to work, not to a diagnosis or a medical claim you are not making.
  • Evidence list. What you hold and what you are attaching or can provide on request. Do not attach originals you cannot replace; send copies and keep the originals.
  • What you are asking for. See Step 4. Be specific.
  • Closing. A request for written acknowledgement, a named contact, and a timeline for next steps, plus your contact details and a line stating that you expect no retaliation for reporting.

Example opening, adapted to your facts: "I am writing to formally report repeated harassing conduct by [name, job title] in [department]. The incidents described below occurred on [dates] at [locations], and I am requesting a formal investigation." Example impact line: "Since [date], I have avoided [meeting or area] and asked to change [duty or shift] to limit contact, which has affected [specific work outcome]." Short, dated, and checkable beats long and general every time.

Step 4: Ask for specific action, not just attention

A letter that only says "please look into this" gives HR nothing to answer to. Ask for these items by name, and adjust them to your situation:

  • Written acknowledgement of receipt with the date the complaint was received.
  • A named contact for the process and an expected timeline for the next step, even if that step is only an initial meeting.
  • A formal investigation or, if the employer follows a different process, a written explanation of what that process is.
  • Interim measures if ongoing contact is part of the problem: a reporting-line change, a schedule change, or a seating or assignment change while the matter is reviewed. Name the measure that would actually help, rather than asking vaguely for protection.
  • Confidentiality handling. Ask how the complaint will be shared and who will see it. Do not promise yourself confidentiality HR cannot give: an investigation usually requires speaking to the people involved, so ask for the sharing to be limited to what the process requires.
  • No retaliation. State clearly that you are reporting in good faith and expect no adverse action for doing so. Retaliation protections vary by situation and jurisdiction, so this line is a record of your expectation, not a legal conclusion.

Keep the requested outcome reasonable and tied to the workplace: an investigation, corrective action, training, or a change in working arrangements. Asking for a specific person's dismissal in the first letter usually weakens the complaint rather than strengthening it, because it turns a factual report into a demand about someone else's employment outcome.

Step 5: Send it through a channel you can prove, and keep the proof

  • Use the channel your policy names, if one exists. Check the handbook or intranet for the reporting route, and follow it. If the named route leads to the person you are reporting, use the alternate route and note in the letter why you did so.
  • Prefer a method with a receipt. Email to a named HR address, a submitted form with a confirmation number, or hand delivery with a signed and dated copy for yourself. If you hand it over in person, ask for a stamped or signed receipt on your copy on the spot.
  • Send it to one responsible recipient, not to the whole company. Copying a wide audience feels powerful and usually backfires: it spreads the complaint beyond the people who can act on it and can complicate the process for everyone, including you.
  • Keep the sent copy, the receipt, and any reply together with your incident notes, outside work systems. That bundle is your record if the matter is later disputed, escalated, or reviewed.

Step 6: After you file, follow up in writing and watch the clock

If you receive no acknowledgement within the timeline HR gave you, or within about a week if no timeline was given, follow up in writing. Reference the original complaint by date and subject, ask for the status and the next step, and keep a copy. One polite written follow-up is stronger than several unrecorded verbal check-ins, because it shows a gap in the process rather than a difference in memory about a conversation.

If the response is a genuine investigation, cooperate with it: answer questions factually, provide the evidence you listed, and keep notes of meetings, dates, and who was present. If the response is dismissal without explanation, a long silence, or a change in your duties, shifts, or treatment after you reported, write down what changed and when, and get professional advice promptly. That pattern is exactly the kind of record that matters later, and it is easy to lose if you only notice it in retrospect.

One timing warning, stated generally because the details vary: outside complaints to government agencies and other bodies carry deadlines that depend on the agency and where you live. A slow internal process does not pause those deadlines automatically. Do not assume you have unlimited time while waiting for an internal answer. If the internal process drags, check what outside deadlines apply to you and get advice before they pass.

Step 7: Mistakes that weaken an otherwise valid complaint

  • Vague dates and sweeping claims. "He is always hostile" cannot be investigated. Dated incidents can.
  • Guessing at motives. Report what was said and done. Let the investigation assess intent; your letter loses credibility when it asserts what someone was thinking.
  • Threats and ultimatums. Do not threaten lawsuits, media contact, or resignation to force action unless you genuinely intend that step and have advice behind it. Empty threats harden the other side and distract from the facts.
  • Posting about it publicly first. Social posts can breach policy, spread unproven claims, and complicate an investigation. Keep the complaint in the formal channel until you have advice to do otherwise.
  • Deleting evidence or editing messages. Keep originals intact, even messages that are unflattering to you. Selective editing, once discovered, damages the whole complaint.
  • Signing something under pressure to close the matter. Do not sign a new agreement, a retraction, or a settlement in the same meeting where it is presented. Take it away, read it, and get advice, just as with any binding document.

FAQ

Should I report workplace harassment to HR in writing?

Yes, put it in writing even if you also speak to someone in person. A written complaint creates a dated record of what you reported, when, and to whom. Verbal reports are easy to misremember, minimize, or lose. Keep a copy outside company systems, since work email and devices are not yours to rely on later.

What should a harassment complaint letter to HR include?

The essentials are: who was involved, what happened in factual terms, and when and where each incident occurred, in date order. Add witnesses if any, evidence you hold, how the conduct affected your work, any earlier attempts to resolve it, and the specific action you are asking for, such as a formal investigation and a written acknowledgement. Stick to what you observed rather than guessing at motives.

What if the person I need to report is my supervisor or in HR?

Do not send the complaint to the person named in it. Send it to the next level up, another HR contact, or whatever alternate reporting channel your employer provides, such as an ethics line. If no usable internal channel exists, that fact itself belongs in your record, and it is a reason to get outside professional advice rather than to stay silent.

Can I ask HR to keep my harassment complaint confidential?

You can ask, and you should, but do not assume confidentiality can be guaranteed. An employer that receives a complaint usually needs to look into it, and that often requires speaking to the people involved. Ask how the information will be shared, who will see it, and what interim steps are possible, and get those answers in writing where you can.

What should I ask HR to do after I report harassment?

Ask for four things in plain terms: written acknowledgement of receipt, a named contact and an expected timeline for next steps, a formal investigation or a written explanation of the process that will be followed, and interim measures if contact with the other person is part of the problem, such as a schedule or reporting change. Also state clearly that you expect no retaliation for reporting.

What do I do if HR does not respond to my harassment complaint?

Follow up in writing after a reasonable interval, referencing your original complaint and its date, and keep a copy of the follow-up. If there is still no meaningful response, escalate through the next internal channel and get professional advice about outside options. Deadlines for outside complaints vary by agency and location, so do not let a slow internal process run out the clock without checking what applies to you.

Should I include every detail and emotion in the complaint?

No. Include every relevant fact and leave out insults, threats, and speculation about why someone acted. A short factual account with dates, quotes where you remember them accurately, and named witnesses is easier for HR to investigate than a long emotional letter. You can state the impact on your work plainly without turning the letter into an argument.
