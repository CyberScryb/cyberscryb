How to Write an Invitation Letter for a Visitor Visa

What the letter must contain, a fill-in template outline, and the mistakes that make consular officers stop reading. One strong letter supports the whole application.

By CyberScryb 📅 Updated October 16, 2026 ⏱ 7 min read
⚡ Key Takeaways (TL;DR)

A visitor visa invitation letter must include the host's full contact details and legal status, the visitor's full name, date of birth, and passport number exactly as on the passport, the real relationship between you, the purpose and specific dates of the visit, where the visitor will stay, who pays for what, and a signature with the date. Specific details beat warm prose: vague letters get ignored, precise ones get trusted. The letter is supporting evidence, not a guarantee of approval, and this guide is general information, not immigration advice.

What the Letter Is and What It Is Not

An invitation letter is a written statement from you, the host, confirming that you are inviting a specific person to visit you in your country. It goes into the visitor's visa application file as supporting evidence. Its real job is to answer the officer's questions before they ask them: who is this person, why are they coming, where will they stay, who is paying, and when are they leaving.

What it is not: a legal guarantee, a sponsorship contract, or an approval. No letter obligates a consulate to issue a visa. The officer still weighs the applicant's own ties to home, finances, and travel history. Write the letter as evidence, not as a demand.

What the Letter Must Contain

  1. Your details as the host. Full legal name, home address, phone number, and email address. Add your legal status in the destination country (citizen, permanent resident) and your occupation. This is what makes you a verifiable, reachable person.
  2. The visitor's details. Full name, date of birth, and passport number, copied exactly from the passport. Even a small mismatch between the letter and the passport invites suspicion.
  3. The relationship. State plainly how you know the visitor and for how long: parent, sibling, friend since a specific year, former colleague. One or two sentences of real history beat a paragraph of adjectives.
  4. Purpose of the visit. Be concrete: attending a wedding, meeting a new grandchild, sightseeing together, a family reunion. "Tourism" alone is thin; give it a reason.
  5. Specific travel dates. Planned arrival and departure dates. Officers are specifically looking for evidence the visitor intends to leave, so firm dates matter more than any promise.
  6. Accommodation and daily plans. Where the visitor will stay (your home, with the address, or a named hotel) and a rough outline of the visit.
  7. Financial responsibility. State clearly who covers what: you will cover lodging and meals, the visitor covers their own flights, or whatever the real arrangement is. If you are covering costs, say so and be prepared to back it with your own financial documents.
  8. Date and signature. The date the letter was written and your signature. A signed, dated letter is a document; an unsigned one is a draft.

Template Outline: Fill In Your Own Details

Copy this skeleton and replace every bracketed placeholder with your real information. Keep it to one page.

[Your Full Name]
[Your Street Address]
[Your Phone Number]
[Your Email Address]
[Date]

To the Consular Officer,
Re: Invitation Letter for [Visitor's Full Name], Passport No. [Passport Number]

I am writing to invite [Visitor's Full Name], born [Date of Birth], a citizen of [Country], to visit me in [Destination Country] from [Arrival Date] to [Departure Date].

[Visitor's First Name] is my [relationship, e.g., mother / friend of 12 years]. [One or two sentences on how you know each other.] The purpose of this visit is [specific purpose, e.g., to attend my daughter's wedding on (date) / to meet their new grandchild].

During the visit, [Visitor's First Name] will stay with me at [your address or hotel name and address]. [He/She/They] will return to [Country] on [Departure Date], as [he/she/they] [tie to home, e.g., must return to work / has family and property there].

Regarding expenses: [I will cover / the visitor will cover] [accommodation, meals, local travel, flights - state the real split]. I have attached [proof of my status: passport copy / permanent resident card] and [bank statements / employment letter] in support.

I confirm that all information in this letter is true and accurate. I can be reached at the contact details above if any verification is needed.

Sincerely,
[Signature]
[Your Printed Full Name]

A note on attachments: the letter carries more weight with proof behind it. Common enclosures are a copy of the host's passport or status document, proof of address, and recent bank statements or an employment letter if the host is covering costs.

Mistakes That Weaken the Letter

  • Vague purpose and dates. "They will visit sometime next year for a while" tells the officer nothing. Specific dates and a concrete reason are the entire point of the letter.
  • Details that do not match the passport. A misspelled name or wrong passport number turns one document into two stories. Copy details character for character from the passport.
  • Promising the visa will be granted. Never write that the visitor "will be granted a visa" or that you "guarantee approval." You cannot promise it, and the claim undermines your credibility.
  • Saying you guarantee the visitor will return. You can state their ties to home and their planned departure date. You cannot guarantee another adult's behavior, and officers know it.
  • Financial claims with no backing. If you say you will cover all expenses, attach evidence you can afford to. A promise of support with no proof reads worse than an honest split of costs.
  • No contact details, no signature, no date. An unsigned, undated letter with no phone number cannot be verified. Officers treat unverifiable letters as absent letters.
  • Over-explaining or begging. Long emotional appeals and legal-sounding language both backfire. Formal, factual, and short wins.

Logistics People Always Ask About

Notarization: usually not required, but check the destination consulate. Some, such as certain Canadian consulates, specifically prefer a notarized or commissioned letter, and it carries more weight there. When in doubt, a notarized letter never hurts.

Original vs. copy: most consulates accept a clear scanned or printed copy of the signed letter. The host can email it and the visitor prints it for the application file. Keep the original signed copy in case the officer asks.

Who can write one: anyone with a real relationship to the visitor and legal status in the destination country. Family is most common, but a genuine friend works the same way as long as the relationship is explained honestly.

Addressing: addressing the letter to the consular or visa officer in a formal tone is the safest choice. Letters written directly to the guest work too, but they must still contain every factual detail since the officer reads them either way.

One letter per visitor: if you are inviting a couple or a family, either write one letter naming everyone with each person's passport details, or write one per person. Do not leave a traveling companion unnamed.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is an invitation letter for a visitor visa?

A written statement from a host in the destination country inviting a foreign visitor to stay with them or visit them, submitted as supporting evidence with the visitor's visa application. It explains the relationship, the purpose and dates of the visit, where the visitor will stay, and who covers the costs.

What must an invitation letter for a visitor visa include?

The host's full name, address, phone, and email; the host's legal status in the destination country; the visitor's full name, date of birth, and passport number exactly as on the passport; the relationship between host and visitor; the purpose of the visit; specific travel dates; where the visitor will stay; who pays for what; the date the letter was written; and the host's signature.

Does an invitation letter guarantee the visa will be approved?

No. The letter is supporting evidence, not an approval. The consular officer still evaluates the applicant's own ties to their home country, financial situation, and travel history. A strong letter helps, but it cannot override a weak application, and you should never promise in the letter that the visa will be granted.

Does a visa invitation letter need to be notarized?

It depends on the country and consulate. Most countries, including the United States, do not require notarization. Some consulates, such as certain Canadian ones, specifically request or prefer a notarized or commissioned letter, and it carries more weight there. Check the destination consulate's requirements before deciding.

Can I send a scanned or emailed copy, or does it need to be an original?

Most consulates accept a clear scanned or printed copy of a signed letter, so the host can email or message it to the visitor. An original with a wet signature is rarely required, but the signature and the host's contact details should be real and verifiable.

Can a friend write an invitation letter, or does it have to be a family member?

A friend can write one. What matters is that the relationship is real and believable, so explain how you know each other and for how long. Family invitations are common, especially for parents, but a genuine friendship with some history behind it works the same way.

Should the letter be addressed to the visitor or to the visa officer?

Either works, but addressing it to the consular or visa officer in a formal tone is the safer choice when the letter will be read as part of the application file. If it is written directly to the guest, keep it warm but still include every factual detail, because the officer reads it either way.

Is an invitation letter required for a US visitor visa?

No, the United States does not officially require an invitation letter for a B-1/B-2 visitor visa. It is optional supporting documentation. Many applicants include one anyway because it documents the purpose of the trip, the planned stay, and the ties pulling the visitor back home.
