The uncomfortable truth about denials

Here's the uncomfortable truth: your insurance didn't deny you because the treatment isn't needed. They denied you because denials are profitable. A huge percentage of prior auth denials get overturned on appeal, which tells you everything: the first "no" is a filter, not a medical opinion. Most people accept it and walk away. Don't be most people.

Appeals aren't won with emotion or outrage. They're won with clinical facts stacked so neatly the reviewer can't say no twice. You're not arguing. You're documenting.

The packet that wins

Build one packet, in this order. The denial letter itself, with the reason code highlighted. Your clinical notes showing the diagnosis and history. Relevant labs and imaging. A letter of medical necessity from your provider, which is often the single decisive piece. And the specific policy language you're appealing under, quoted, not paraphrased. Five pieces. If any one is missing, the reviewer has an excuse. Don't give them one.

Deadlines (don't blow these)

Internal appeals usually have 30 to 180 day windows, and the clock starts the day the denial arrives, not the day you feel like dealing with it. Put the deadline on your calendar immediately. Miss it and you're done, no matter how good your case is. If waiting risks your health, say so explicitly and ask for expedited review. That's a real process with real teeth. Use it.

When your doctor should write it

Your member appeal introduces the case. Your clinician's letter of medical necessity carries the medical argument. If your doctor is willing to write one, which most are when you ask directly, that letter does more work than everything else combined. Reviewers are clinicians too. They listen to clinicians, not to patients' frustration, however justified.

FAQ

Should my doctor write the appeal?

A clinician letter of medical necessity is often decisive. Your member appeal can introduce the case; the clinical letter carries the medical argument. Ask directly, most doctors will do it.

What if internal appeal fails?

Many plans allow external review by an independent third party, and those reviewers overturn denials at a decent clip. Keep every letter and timeline. Consumer assistance programs in your state can help navigate next steps free.

How long does an appeal take?

Standard internal appeals usually get decided within 30 days. Expedited ones, when delay risks your health, can come back in 72 hours or less. Ask for expedited in writing and say exactly why waiting is dangerous.

Can I appeal more than once?

Yes. Most plans have multiple internal levels, then external review after that. Each denial is just the next round, not the end, as long as you're inside the deadlines. The people who win are usually the ones who didn't stop at the first no.
