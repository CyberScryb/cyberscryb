How to Write a Poem

The one rule that fixes most bad poems — plus how to pick a form, kill cliches, and read your draft aloud.

By CyberScryb 📅 Updated October 10, 2026 ⏱ 6 min read
⚡ Key Takeaways (TL;DR)

Good poems run on concrete images, not abstractions. Start with one image, write ten raw lines, keep the three strongest. Pick a form that fits the occasion — haiku for quiet moments, ballad for stories, ode for praise — then read every draft aloud and cut what repeats.

The One Rule: Concrete Over Abstract

Nearly every bad poem fails the same way — it stays at the level of the idea. "Love is beautiful," "grief is heavy," "time passes." The reader nods and feels nothing. A poem works when it goes one level down: the coffee cup still warm on the counter, the porch swing chain creaking at dusk, the exact sound of the screen door. The image carries the feeling without naming it. Give the reader the image; let them do the feeling.

Pick a Form That Fits the Job

Form is not decoration — it is a set of decisions someone already made for you, which is exactly what you want when you are stuck. A haiku (three lines, 5-7-5) suits one clear image and nothing else. A limerick suits jokes. A ballad suits a story with a beginning and an end. An ode suits the person or thing you cannot stop praising. Free verse suits everything else — but "free" does not mean shapeless; the line breaks are still doing the work of emphasis and pause. When in doubt, start with a haiku: the tight frame forces specificity, which is the skill everything else rests on.

Write Ugly, Then Cut Ruthlessly

The first draft of a poem is not a poem — it is raw material. Write ten lines fast, without judging. Then become the editor: circle the three strongest lines, keep only what serves them, and cut everything that repeats, explains, or decorates. Cliches go first — "heart of gold," "dancing like nobody's watching," "a rollercoaster of emotions" have all been said ten thousand times, which means they say nothing now. If a line sounds like a greeting card, it is not finished.

Read It Aloud or It Is Not Done

A poem is built for the ear as much as the eye. Read every draft out loud and listen for three things: lines you stumble over (rephrase them), rhythms that go flat (break the line somewhere else), and rhymes that feel forced (drop the rhyme — forced rhyme is worse than no rhyme). The ear catches what the eye forgives. If you are reading it at a wedding, a memorial, or anywhere people are listening — rehearse it standing up, twice.

What to Avoid

  • Abstractions with no image underneath — "beauty," "sorrow," "forever"
  • Cliches that have been said ten thousand times
  • Rhymes that twist the sentence to serve the sound
  • Explaining the image after showing it — trust the reader
  • Ending on a moral — let the last image do the work

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I start writing a poem?

Start with one concrete image instead of a topic. Write ten raw lines about it without judging them, then pick the three strongest and build from there.

What is the easiest poem form for beginners?

Haiku. Three lines, 5-7-5 syllables, one clear image. The tight frame forces you to be specific.

Do poems have to rhyme?

No. Most modern poetry does not rhyme. Rhyme is a tool for specific forms and effects — forced rhyme makes poems sound stiff.

How long should a poem be?

As long as the image holds. Cut anything that repeats what an earlier line already said.
