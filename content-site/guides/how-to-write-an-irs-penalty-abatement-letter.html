How to Write an IRS Penalty Abatement Letter

Direct answer: check the new automatic abatement first. For 2025 and later returns the IRS now waives first-time penalties on its own if you have a clean three-year record, so many people do not need a letter at all. If your penalty is for an older tax year, or you are claiming reasonable cause, write a one-to-two-page letter that names the exact penalty and tax year, tells what happened in chronological order, ties each fact to a reason the IRS recognizes, and attaches proof. File it with Form 843 by certified mail. Expect an answer in 6 to 12 weeks.

First, the 2026 change: automatic abatement for 2025 and later returns

Starting with the 2026 filing season, the IRS replaced the old request-based first-time abatement with an Automatic Exemption from Penalty (AEP). If you qualify, the IRS simply does not assess the penalty. You do not call, write, or file anything.

  • What it covers: failure-to-file, failure-to-pay, and failure-to-deposit penalties on 2025 annual returns and later, and 2026 quarterly returns and later.
  • Who qualifies: you filed the same type of return on time and paid for the prior three years (or 12 consecutive quarters), with no penalties other than an estimated tax penalty in that period.
  • What it does not cover: estimated tax penalties, accuracy-related penalties, and returns for tax years before 2025. Estate and gift returns are excluded.

The rollout is phased, so some qualifying taxpayers still get a penalty notice by mistake during the transition. If that happens to you, call the number on the notice and ask for first-time abatement. The IRS can fix it on the phone. Only write the letter if the phone request fails, your penalty is for a pre-2025 tax year, or you are claiming reasonable cause.

The two doors: first-time abatement vs reasonable cause

The IRS removes penalties through one of two doors. Pick the right one before you write a word, because the letter you write is completely different for each.

  • First-time abatement (FTA): an administrative waiver for taxpayers with a clean three-year record. No story, no evidence, no hardship to prove. For 2025 and later returns it is automatic. For 2024 and earlier, request it by phone, letter, or Form 843. You do not even have to name it: if you call asking for reasonable cause and you qualify for FTA, the IRS applies FTA.
  • Reasonable cause: for everyone else. You must show you exercised ordinary business care and prudence but still could not comply because of circumstances beyond your control. This is the door that needs a real letter, with dates, facts, and documents. The rest of this guide is about this door.

Which penalties can be abated, and how big they are

Know the penalty you are fighting before you ask for it to be removed. The three common ones:

  • Failure to file: 5% of the unpaid tax for each month or part of a month the return is late, capped at 25%. If the return is more than 60 days late, a minimum penalty applies (currently $510 for returns due in 2025).
  • Failure to pay: 0.5% of the unpaid tax per month, capped at 25%. When both penalties apply at once, the failure-to-file penalty is reduced to 4.5% so the combined rate stays at 5% per month. Under an IRS installment agreement, the failure-to-pay rate drops to 0.25% per month.
  • Accuracy-related: 20% of the underpayment. Harder to abate: you must show reasonable cause and good faith, not just one or the other.

Abatement removes the penalty, not the interest on the tax you owe. Interest keeps accruing while your request is reviewed, so pay the underlying tax as soon as you can even while the letter is pending. The interest charged on an abated penalty goes away with the penalty itself.

What counts as reasonable cause (and what does not)

The IRS decides reasonable cause case by case, but the accepted categories are consistent. Match your facts to one of these, with documents to prove it:

  • Death or serious illness in your immediate family.
  • Records destroyed by fire, flood, or another natural disaster.
  • A documented IRS error, such as wrong written advice or a misapplied payment.
  • Incorrect advice from a competent tax professional that you relied on in good faith.
  • Unavoidable absence, such as hospitalization, rehab, or incarceration.
  • Unable to obtain records despite reasonable effort, for example a business partner or employer withholding documents.

These usually do not qualify on their own:

  • Lack of funds. Being unable to pay is not, by itself, reasonable cause for filing or paying late.
  • Forgetting the deadline or not knowing the rule.
  • A simple mistake with no circumstance behind it.
  • Relying on someone else to file for you, without checking that they did.

The IRS also looks at what you did once the problem passed. If you waited months after the illness ended or the records were recovered before filing, the delay after the hardship ended works against you. File or pay promptly once you are able, then write the letter.

Step 1: pull your notice and identify the penalty

Everything in your letter traces back to the notice. Before writing, pull these facts from it:

  1. The notice number (CP14, CP161, CP22, and similar) and its date.
  2. The tax year and the exact penalty type and amount.
  3. The tax period, usually shown as a month and year (for example, December 2024).
  4. Whether the tax itself has been paid, or whether you are on a payment plan.

If you lost the notice, request a transcript through your IRS online account or call 800-829-1040. Do not guess at the penalty amount or the tax year: a letter that names the wrong penalty gets routed to the wrong unit and sits.

Step 2: check first-time abatement before writing anything

If your penalty is for tax year 2024 or earlier and you had no penalties in the three prior tax years, filed all required returns, and have paid or arranged to pay the tax, you probably qualify for first-time abatement. Call the number on your notice and ask for it. Many FTA requests are granted on the phone in minutes.

Only write the reasonable-cause letter if FTA does not apply: you had a penalty in the last three years, you have unfiled returns, or the penalty is accuracy-related. Spending weeks drafting an elaborate reasonable-cause letter when a phone call would have cleared the penalty is the most common waste of effort in this process.

Step 3: gather evidence that matches your dates

Evidence is what makes or breaks a reasonable-cause request. The reviewer checks three things: the dates and duration of the hardship, how it prevented compliance, and whether you acted promptly once it passed. Collect documents that prove each one:

  • Medical records, hospital admission and discharge dates, or a death certificate.
  • Insurance claims, fire department reports, or FEMA documentation for a disaster.
  • Written IRS correspondence showing the error, or the letter from your tax professional with the wrong advice.
  • Proof you filed or paid as soon as you could: the filing confirmation, payment receipts, or your installment agreement.

Attach copies, never originals. Reference each attachment by number in the letter so the reviewer can match document to claim without hunting.

Step 4: write the letter

Keep it to one or two pages. The structure that works:

  1. Identify yourself and the penalty. Full name, Social Security number, address, tax year, tax period, penalty type and amount, notice number. Put this in the first paragraph so the letter can be routed correctly even if the cover sheet is lost.
  2. State the request plainly. "I am requesting abatement of the failure-to-pay penalty for tax year 2024 under reasonable cause, Internal Revenue Code section 6651(a)(2), and Internal Revenue Manual 20.1.1.3.2." One sentence. No throat clearing.
  3. Tell what happened in chronological order. Dates first, then facts. "On March 10, 2025, I was admitted to the hospital. I was discharged April 2. My return was due April 15." Tie each fact to the deadline it affected.
  4. Connect the facts to the rule. Say which reasonable-cause category applies and why: "This was a serious illness beyond my control. I exercised ordinary business care by [what you did]. I filed within two weeks of discharge."
  5. List your attachments. Number them and name them: "Attachment 1: hospital discharge summary, March 10 to April 2, 2025."
  6. Close with the ask and your contact info. "Please abate the penalty of $X for tax year 2024. I can be reached at [phone] and [address]." Sign it.

Do not pad the letter with background the reviewer does not need, do not blame the IRS unless you can document an IRS error, and do not claim a hardship you cannot prove. A short letter with attached proof beats a long letter with none.

Step 5: file Form 843 and send it by certified mail

A letter alone can work, but the formal vehicle is Form 843, Claim for Refund and Request for Abatement. Attach your letter to it:

  • Line 1: the tax period (month and year).
  • Line 3: the type of tax and the specific penalty.
  • Line 4: the code section under which the penalty was assessed (commonly IRC 6651 for failure-to-file and failure-to-pay penalties).
  • Line 5a: the reason allowed under the law, and Line 7 for your explanation, or write "see attached letter" and attach the letter.
  • Sign and date it. An unsigned Form 843 is rejected without review.

Mail the form, the letter, and copies of your evidence to the address on your penalty notice, or to the IRS service center address in the Form 843 instructions for your region. Send it by USPS Certified Mail, return receipt requested, and keep the receipt and copies of everything. The IRS has a history of losing written requests, and the certified receipt is your proof of the filing date.

What happens after you send it

Expect an initial answer in 6 to 12 weeks on a written request. The IRS sends one of three outcomes: full abatement (the penalty is removed and you get a notice showing the adjusted balance), partial abatement, or denial with the reason stated.

Watch the clock while you wait. You generally have 3 years from the date you filed the return, or 2 years from the date you paid the tax, whichever is later, to request a refund of penalties already paid. If the request is denied, you can appeal within 30 days with Form 12203, Request for Appeals Review (for penalties of $25,000 or less), or re-request with new evidence that was not in the first submission. Keep a log of every call and letter: date, time, the representative's ID number, and what was said.

Mistakes that get letters denied

  • Claiming lack of funds and nothing else. Not being able to pay does not excuse filing or paying late. You need a circumstance beyond your control.
  • No evidence attached. A hardship claim with no documents is a story, not a case. The reviewer cannot approve what you cannot prove.
  • Dates that do not line up. If the hardship ended in March and the return was due in October, the letter fails on its own facts.
  • Skipping the FTA check. Writing a reasonable-cause letter when a five-minute phone call would have granted first-time abatement wastes weeks.
  • Waiting too long after the hardship ends. The IRS measures how fast you acted once you were able. File first, then write.
  • Sending it regular mail with no copies. If the IRS loses it, you have no proof you sent it and no copy to resend.

FAQ

Do I still need to write a penalty abatement letter now that the IRS applies first-time abatement automatically?

Not always. Starting with the 2026 filing season, the IRS automatically waives failure-to-file, failure-to-pay, and failure-to-deposit penalties for qualifying taxpayers on 2025 and later returns, with no request needed. You still need a letter if your penalty is for a tax year before 2025, if you do not have a clean three-year compliance history, or if you are claiming reasonable cause rather than first-time abatement.

What counts as reasonable cause for IRS penalty abatement?

Reasonable cause means you exercised ordinary business care and prudence but still could not comply because of circumstances beyond your control. Accepted reasons include a death or serious illness in the immediate family, records destroyed by fire or natural disaster, a documented IRS error, or incorrect advice from a competent tax professional. Lack of funds alone, forgetting the deadline, or not knowing the law usually do not qualify.

How long does an IRS penalty abatement request take?

A phone request for first-time abatement can be approved on the spot. A written reasonable-cause request with Form 843 usually takes 6 to 12 weeks for an initial answer, and complex cases take longer. If the request is denied and you appeal, the appeal can take 6 to 12 months.

Should I use Form 843 or just write a letter?

Use both: file Form 843, Claim for Refund and Request for Abatement, and attach your letter as the explanation. The form gives the IRS the tax period, penalty type, and code section in the format its system expects; the letter tells the story and ties your facts to reasonable cause. For a simple first-time abatement request on an older tax year, a phone call to the number on your notice is often enough.

Where do I send an IRS penalty abatement letter?

Send it to the address shown on your penalty notice, or to the IRS service center address listed in the Form 843 instructions for your region. Send it by USPS Certified Mail with return receipt requested and keep copies of everything, including the mailing receipt.

Can I request penalty abatement by phone instead of writing a letter?

Yes, for first-time abatement on older tax years. Call the toll-free number on your notice, or 800-829-1040 for individuals, and ask for first-time abatement. The IRS checks your three-year compliance history while you are on the phone. Reasonable-cause requests are stronger in writing, with evidence attached, because the reviewer needs documentation.

What happens if the IRS denies my penalty abatement request?

You can appeal. For penalties of $25,000 or less, file Form 12203, Request for Appeals Review, within 30 days of the denial notice. You can also re-request with stronger evidence if you have new facts or documents that were not in the first submission.

Does penalty abatement remove the interest too?

No. Abatement removes the penalty, not the interest on the unpaid tax, and interest keeps accruing while your request is under review. The interest charged on an abated penalty is removed along with the penalty itself, but interest on the underlying tax balance stays. Pay the tax itself as soon as you can.
