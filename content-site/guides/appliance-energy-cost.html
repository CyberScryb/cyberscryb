How Much Does It Cost to Run an Appliance?

One formula answers every appliance on your bill. Here it is, a cost table so you can look things up fast, the big offenders worked out, and five ways to shrink the bill.

By CyberScryb 📅 Updated October 6, 2026 ⏱ 8 min read
⚡ Key Takeaways (TL;DR)

Cost = watts x hours / 1,000 x your rate. That is the whole trick. A 3,500-watt central AC at 8 hours a day costs about $143 a month at $0.17 per kWh. Heating and cooling, the water heater, and the dryer are where your money actually goes. Everything below runs on that one formula, plus a calculator you can use right on this page.

The Direct Answer: The kWh Cost Formula

Your power company bills you in kilowatt-hours (kWh). One kWh is what a 1,000-watt appliance burns in one hour. Your rate is the price per kWh. So the cost of running anything is:

kWh = watts x hours / 1,000

Cost = kWh x your rate per kWh

Worked example. A 1,500-watt space heater, 4 hours a day. kWh = 1,500 x 4 / 1,000 = 6 kWh a day. At $0.17 per kWh that is 6 x 0.17 = $1.02 a day, or about $30.60 a month. Two inputs you look up (wattage is on the appliance label), one you read off your bill (your rate), and you know exactly what the thing costs.

$0.17 per kWh is roughly the US residential average. Your rate may be half that or double. The formula does not care; it works for any rate.

The Big Offenders, Worked Out

Central AC. A 3-ton system pulls roughly 3,500 watts when the compressor is running. At $0.17 per kWh, that is $0.60 an hour. Eight hours of real runtime a day puts you near $143 a month. In hot states with rates above $0.25, the same unit clears $200. This is the single largest line on most summer bills.

Space heater. The classic budget trap. 1,500 watts on full blast is $0.26 an hour, which feels like nothing until it runs all evening for a month: 6 hours a day is $45.90 a month for heating one room. Two heaters running through a cold snap can cost more than the rest of the house combined.

Electric dryer. About 3,000 watts, roughly 45 minutes per load. That is 2.25 kWh per load, or $0.38 a load at $0.17. A family doing a load a day pays around $11 to $12 a month. Hang-drying half your laundry cuts that in half with no lifestyle cost.

Water heater. Electric tank heaters draw about 4,500 watts, but only fire about 3 hours a day in a typical household. That is 13.5 kWh a day, $2.30 a day, roughly $69 a month. It is the quiet heavyweight: nobody thinks about it, and it never stops showing up on the bill.

Refrigerator. Modern full-size fridges use 40 to 60 kWh a month, so $6.80 to $10.20 at average rates. An old second fridge in the garage can double or triple that. If you own one made before 2001, its nameplate math will often justify replacing it on electricity alone.

Gaming PC. Under load, a gaming rig with a midrange GPU draws 300 to 500 watts, not the 750-plus on the power supply label (that is capacity, not draw). Four hours a day at 400 watts is $8.16 a month. Idle, it drops under 100 watts, so use sleep mode; you do not need to shut it down between sessions.

Phantom Load: The 10% You Never See

Phantom load is electricity drawn by devices that look off but are not: TVs in standby, cable boxes, game consoles, printers, chargers, coffee makers with clocks. The US Department of Energy estimates standby power is roughly 10% of residential electricity use. On a $130 monthly bill, that is about $13 for nothing.

The worst offenders are the ones with a hard drive or a tuner: old cable boxes can draw 15 to 20 watts around the clock, which is $2.20 to $2.45 a month for a box nobody is watching. Phone chargers are the opposite extreme, under a watt each; unplugging them saves pennies. The fix that actually pays: put the entertainment center on one power strip and kill it with a single switch. One strip, one click, no more 24-hour TV standby.

How to Read Your Electric Bill

The bill has four parts that matter:

Total kWh used
Your consumption this period. Compare to the same month last year, not to last month; seasons drive everything.
Rate per kWh
Often split into supply (the power) and delivery (the wires). Your real rate is the sum, plus sometimes tiered pricing where kWh above a threshold cost more.
Service charge
A flat fee, usually $5 to $15 a month. Unavoidable; ignore it when hunting for savings.
Taxes and fees
Small per-kWh add-ons. They make your effective rate slightly higher than the headline rate.

The number most people get wrong is the rate. A bill that says "$0.11 per kWh" can have an effective rate of $0.16 or more after delivery charges, tiers, and fees are added. Divide the total bill by total kWh to get your true all-in rate, and use that number in the formula above.

5 Ways to Cut the Bill

1. Set the thermostat like it matters, because it does. Every degree you raise the AC (or lower the heat) saves about 3% on that portion of the bill. Two degrees down in summer and three up in winter is a real line item, not a rounding error.

2. Swap remaining incandescents for LEDs. A 60-watt bulb becomes a 9-watt bulb. Five old bulbs running 6 hours a day cost $9.18 a month; the LED versions cost $1.38. The payback is a few months per bulb.

3. Put entertainment gear on one power strip and switch it off. This kills the standby draw of the TV, console, soundbar, and streaming box in one move. Roughly $5 to $15 a month recovered, zero effort after setup.

4. Drop the water heater to 120°F. Most ship set at 140°F. Lowering it cuts standby and cycling losses for a device that is already one of your top three costs, and 120°F is the recommended safe setting.

5. Move heavy use off-peak if your plan has time-of-use rates. Some utilities charge half the rate late at night. If yours does, run the dryer and dishwasher after 9 or 10 PM and put the same kWh at a cheaper price.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does it cost to run an air conditioner per hour?

A typical 3-ton central AC draws about 3,500 watts, so it costs roughly $0.60 per hour at $0.17 per kWh. Run it 8 hours a day and that is about $143 a month. A window unit (around 1,200 watts) costs about $0.20 per hour, or $49 a month at 8 hours a day.

What uses the most electricity in a home?

Heating and cooling is the largest share of most electric bills, followed by the water heater and the electric dryer. A central AC or a space heater can each cost more per month than everything else in the house combined. Plug loads, lights, and electronics make up a smaller slice than most people expect.

How much electricity does a refrigerator use per month?

A modern full-size refrigerator uses roughly 40 to 60 kWh per month, which costs $6.80 to $10.20 at $0.17 per kWh. An old or inefficient model can use 100 kWh or more, doubling that cost. The fridge runs 24 hours a day but its compressor cycles on and off, so it draws power only about a third of the time.

Why is my electric bill so high?

The usual causes are heating or cooling a space more than necessary, an electric water heater, extra electric dryer loads, old inefficient appliances, phantom load from devices on standby, and a rate that is higher than you think once delivery charges are included. Compare your total kWh this month to the same month last year to spot what changed.

Is it cheaper to leave lights on or turn them off?

Turn them off. That rule was about old fluorescents and the energy cost of warming up; modern LEDs draw 5 to 15 watts and lose nothing from being switched. A 10-watt LED left on 8 hours a day costs about $0.41 a month, so lighting is cheap either way, but off is still cheaper than on.

How do I calculate the kWh of an appliance?

Multiply the appliance's wattage by the hours it runs, then divide by 1,000. A 1,500-watt space heater run for 4 hours uses 1,500 x 4 / 1,000 = 6 kWh. Multiply by your electricity rate (for example $0.17 per kWh) to get the cost: 6 x 0.17 = $1.02.
