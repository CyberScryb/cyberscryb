How to Write a Dating Profile

The anatomy of a high-match profile: what to write, what to show, and the lines that actually start conversations.

The Anatomy of a High-Match Profile

Every strong profile has four parts: a hook (the first line, which is all most people read), specifics (real hobbies and details, not adjectives), warmth (something that shows you are kind or fun to be around), and an invitation (an easy conversation starter). Miss any one and matches drop.

Example of the full anatomy in 60 words: "ER nurse who runs marathons slowly and makes lasagna fast. Currently accepting hiking buddies and hot takes on pizza toppings. Looking for someone kind who laughs easily — bonus points if you have strong opinions about breakfast burritos." Hook, specifics, warmth, invitation. Done.

Lines That Work vs. Lines That Do Not

Works: "Teach me your best pasta recipe and I will teach you mine." (Playful, specific, invites a reply.)
Does not work: "I love to laugh and have fun." (Everyone loves to laugh. This says nothing.)

Works: "Fluent in three languages: English, sarcasm, and ordering for the table." (Shows personality with structure.)
Does not work: "Just ask." (Puts all the work on the reader — they will not.)

Works: "Weekend project: converting my balcony into a jungle. Plant advice welcome." (Vivid, current, hook.)
Does not work: "Live, laugh, love." (A throw pillow is not a personality.)

Prompt Answers (Hinge and Friends)

Prompts are free personality — treat them like mini-essays, not chores. "Two truths and a lie" works when one truth is genuinely surprising. "I am weirdly competitive about..." should name something real (board games, fantasy football, parallel parking). "Together we could..." should paint a picture of a date, not a life plan: "hit every taco truck on 5th Street and rank them" beats "travel the world."

Photo Tips That Matter More Than the Bio

Honest truth: photos do most of the work. Lead with a clear, smiling face photo — no sunglasses, no group shots. Second photo: full body. Then 2-4 photos of you doing things you love: hiking, cooking, playing an instrument, with your dog. One group photo maximum, and never first. Profiles with 4-6 varied photos dramatically outperform single-selfie profiles, and recent photos only — misleading photos poison the first date.

The Five Profile Sins

1. Adjective lists ("fun, loyal, adventurous"). 2. Negativity ("no drama," "don’t waste my time"). 3. All group photos. 4. Sunglasses or hats in every photo. 5. An empty bio. Any one of these tells quality matches to keep swiping.

After the Match: The First Message

Reference something specific from their profile — it proves you read it and gives them something easy to answer. "Your dog looks like he runs the household — what is his name?" beats "hey" by orders of magnitude. Keep it short, ask one question, and suggest a low-pressure plan within a few exchanges. Pen pals do not become partners.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long should my dating profile bio be?

40 to 80 words for Tinder and Bumble; Hinge can run slightly longer since prompts carry weight. If it needs scrolling, cut it.

Should I mention what I am looking for?

Yes, briefly and positively. "Looking for someone kind who wants a real relationship" works. A list of dealbreakers does not — save standards for conversation, not the billboard.

Is it OK to use humor?

If it is natural to you. Wit is the highest-converting tone, but forced jokes read worse than sincerity. Write the way you actually talk.

How often should I update my profile?

Every few months, or whenever your photos or life change materially. Fresh profiles also get a small algorithmic boost on most apps.

Do dating apps punish certain words?

They do not punish words, but profiles with negativity ("no hookups," "don’t bother if...") get fewer right swipes in every study on the topic. Positivity is a strategy.
