How to Check Grammar Online

Most free grammar checkers send your writing to a server. Here is how online grammar checking actually works, what rule-based and AI checkers each catch and miss, the mistakes that show up most often, and how to proofread without giving up your privacy.

The Direct Answer

To check grammar online, you paste text into a web tool, the tool analyzes it, and you get a list of suspected errors with suggested fixes. That is the whole interaction. What differs is where the analysis happens. In most tools, your text travels to a company server, an AI model or rule engine processes it there, and the results travel back. In a fully local tool, the analysis runs as JavaScript in your browser tab and your text never leaves your device.

That distinction is the one that matters. Everything else, accuracy, features, price, is downstream of it. If the text you are checking is a school essay, the distinction barely matters. If it is a legal draft, a manuscript, a medical note, or a confidential email, it matters a great deal. This guide covers both kinds of checkers honestly, so you can pick the right one for the text in front of you.

Why Most Free Grammar Checkers Are Not Private

Nearly every well-known grammar checker is AI-powered, and AI-powered checking has a structural requirement: the text must be sent to a server where the model runs. Your browser cannot run their models, so the paste-and-send round trip is not a design choice, it is how the product works. The result is that your drafts, cover letters, essays, and emails pass through someone else's infrastructure every time you check them.

What happens next is governed by each company's privacy policy, and the honest summary is that practices vary. Some services state they do not use your text for training; others reserve the right to keep documents for product improvement. Policies change, accounts get breached, and most people never read the terms they accepted. None of this means the big checkers are malicious. It means the privacy guarantee you get from them is a promise, while the guarantee you get from a local tool is a mechanism: there is simply no code path that sends your text anywhere.

The business model question is worth asking directly. Running AI inference on millions of pasted documents costs real money, so free tiers are subsidized by paid plans, and your usage data is valuable for improving the product. A rule-based checker that runs in your browser costs the provider nothing per check, which is why it can be free and unlimited without needing anything from you in return. When a tool asks for nothing, there is nothing to monetize.

The practical workflow is to use both in sequence: run the fast, private, rule-based pass first to clear every mechanical error, then send only the text you are comfortable sharing to an AI checker or editor for meaning-level review. Most of what makes writing look unprofessional is mechanical, and the rule-based pass handles all of it without your text going anywhere.

What a Rule-Based Checker Catches

A rule-based checker is a collection of hand-written patterns, each targeting one known error. Ours runs sixteen rule families: common misspellings with suggested corrections, doubled words, extra and missing spaces around punctuation, sentence and line capitalization, the pronoun "i" in lowercase, missing apostrophes in contractions, confusable word pairs flagged for context review, "could of" corrected to "could have", "in regards to" to "in regard to", "alot" to "a lot", basic subject-verb mismatches like "we was", and doubled punctuation.

The underappreciated strength is explainability. When the checker flags "teh", it tells you it is a common misspelling of "the" and offers the fix. When it flags "their", it reminds you of the three meanings and asks you to pick. You learn the rule as you fix the error, which means the tool makes you a better writer instead of a more dependent one. AI suggestions, by contrast, often arrive as silent rewrites that teach nothing.

What It Will Not Catch

Honesty first: a rule-based checker cannot understand meaning, and that sets a hard ceiling. It will miss a wrong word that is spelled correctly if it is not on the confusables list (writing "bare" when you meant "bear"). It will miss tense drifting from past to present across paragraphs. It will not notice run-on sentences, deliberate fragments it cannot distinguish from accidental ones, awkward phrasing, weak structure, tone problems, or anything factually wrong. It also cannot judge style: it will never tell you a sentence is boring.

This is why the tool presents confusable words as "check this" rather than "this is wrong". Flagging every "their" as an error would be dishonest; the engine cannot know which one you meant. The blue highlights are the tool saying it reached the limit of what patterns can decide, and the judgment is yours. Treat any grammar checker, rule-based or AI, the same way: suggestions are advice, never verdicts.

How to Use the Free Grammar Checker

  1. Paste your text. Open the free grammar checker and paste or type your draft. Checking starts automatically as you type; nothing is uploaded anywhere.
  2. Review the red highlights first. Red marks are likely errors with safe automatic fixes: misspellings, doubles, spacing, capitalization. Read each explanation, then apply the fix or use Apply All Fixes.
  3. Judge the blue highlights yourself. Blue marks are confusable words like their/they're/there. The checker cannot know your intent, so read the meanings and pick the right one.
  4. Do a final human pass. Read the text aloud once. Your ear catches the rhythm problems, tense drift, and awkward phrasing that no rule engine can see. Then it is ready to send.

Before You Paste: A Privacy Checklist

For any grammar checker you have not used before, answer these questions first. If you cannot answer one, assume the worst and paste accordingly.

Where does the checking happen? If the page works with no account and no loading spinner on every keystroke, the work may be local. If results arrive after a delay, your text went to a server.

Does it require an account? Accounts exist to store things, including your documents. A tool with no signup has nowhere to keep your text.

What does the privacy policy say about training? Look for explicit language about whether pasted text is used to improve models. Vague wording is an answer too.

When the text is sensitive, a confidential email, a legal draft, a manuscript, an unpublished idea, the safe default is the local tool. You give up nothing: the mechanical errors are the same ones the server tool would have found first anyway.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is there a free grammar checker without signup?

Yes. Our free grammar checker requires no signup or account and has no usage limits. Paste your text and it checks spelling, punctuation, and common grammar mistakes instantly, entirely in your browser.

Do online grammar checkers store my text?

Most do, in practice. AI-powered grammar checkers send your text to their servers for analysis, and their privacy policies govern how long it is kept and whether it is used for product improvement. A checker that runs entirely in your browser never transmits your text, so there is nothing to store.

What is the difference between a rule-based and an AI grammar checker?

A rule-based checker runs hand-written patterns locally and explains every flag. An AI checker sends text to a server where a model suggests changes, catching subtler issues but requiring you to share your writing. Rule-based is private and explainable; AI is broader but server-dependent.

Can a grammar checker be wrong?

Yes. Rule-based checkers flag confusable words for you to verify because they cannot know your intent, and they miss wrong words that are spelled correctly. AI checkers can misread context and suggest changes that alter your meaning. Treat every suggestion as advice, not a verdict.

What are the most common grammar mistakes?

Their/they're/there and your/you're confusion, its vs it's, then vs than, affect vs effect, missing apostrophes in contractions, "could of" instead of "could have", doubled words, and sentences starting with a lowercase letter.

Is a rule-based grammar checker enough for professional writing?

It is enough for a first pass: it reliably catches the mechanical errors that make writing look unprofessional. For meaning-level review, tone, and structure, pair it with a careful human read or an AI editor, and only paste text you are comfortable sharing with that service.

Can I check grammar offline?

With a browser-based rule checker, yes: once the page has loaded, the checking itself needs no internet connection because the rules run as JavaScript on your device.
