How to Respond to an IRS Audit Notice

Direct answer: an audit notice is a mailed letter saying the IRS is examining specific items on a return. It is not a bill, not a verdict, and not proof you did anything wrong. Read it in full, identify exactly what kind of examination it opens, calendar the response deadline, gather only the records it asks for, and reply in writing with copies, never originals. Do not ignore it. The IRS finishes an unanswered audit without you, using only the information it already has.

Step 1: Identify what you actually received

Not every IRS letter is an audit, and the kind of examination changes how you respond. Find the letter or notice number at the top of the page and match it to one of these:

  • Correspondence audit, usually Letter 566. The most common type. The IRS asks for documents by mail or secure upload for specific items. No meeting.
  • Office audit. You meet in person at an IRS office with a tax compliance officer. Less common for individual returns.
  • Field audit, usually Letter 2205. A revenue agent examines the return at your home, your business, or your representative's office. The broadest scope and the highest stakes of the three.
  • CP2000, proposed changes. Not an audit. An automated notice that income reported by third parties does not match your return. It still needs a response by its deadline, but it is a different proceeding.
  • CP75 or CP75A, request for supporting documentation. Not a full audit either. The IRS is asking for proof behind a credit or deduction, often the Earned Income Credit. Answer with the requested documents only.
  • CP14, balance due. Not an audit at all. It says you owe a balance for a tax year. Our CP14 guide covers that one.

If the letter is not one of these, or you cannot tell what it is, call the number printed on it before you send anything. Guessing wrong about the proceeding wastes the response window.

Step 2: Read the notice like a checklist

Before you react, pull these facts off the notice itself:

  • The tax year or years. Only the years named are under examination. Do not volunteer other years.
  • The specific items under review. Each item maps to a line on the return, a form, a deduction, or a credit. List them one by one.
  • The response deadline. Usually 30 days from the notice date. That date comes from the notice, not from a general rule.
  • How to respond. Mail, fax, or the IRS Document Upload Tool, as the notice directs. Use the method it specifies.
  • The contact. The examiner, unit, or phone number printed on the notice. That is the only contact to use.

Keep the notice. Do not throw it away after reading it: it is the reference for every later step, and you will need the notice number and tax year every time you call or write.

Step 3: Calendar the deadline, and ask for more time if you need it

Put the response date on a calendar the day the notice arrives, with a reminder a week before it. Missing it is the single most expensive mistake in an audit: the IRS completes the examination on its own information, disallows the items it questioned, and moves toward a Notice of Deficiency.

If the deadline is too tight to gather your records, ask before it passes. For audits conducted by mail, the IRS can ordinarily grant a one-time automatic 30-day extension if you fax a written request to the number shown on the letter. Extensions are not automatic for every type of notice, so ask early and get the answer in writing. One deadline you cannot extend, ever, is the 90-day Tax Court petition window after a Notice of Deficiency. If that letter arrives, calendar it immediately.

Step 4: Gather only what was asked for

For each item on your list from step 2, pull the filed return for that year and the records that support it:

  • The exact return filed. The return and schedules the IRS has, not a reprinted summary. If you used a preparer, ask for their file copy and workpapers.
  • Proof for each questioned item. Receipts, bank statements, mileage logs, 1099s, or contracts, matched item by item. A receipt that proves one deduction does nothing for a different one.
  • Nothing extra. Do not send records for years or issues the IRS has not opened, and do not answer questions the notice never asked. The two most common self-inflicted errors in an audit are answering unasked questions and producing records for years not under review.

Send copies, never originals. Preserve everything you submit and everything the IRS sends back. Never create, alter, or backdate a record: a fabricated document turns a civil examination into a far worse problem.

Step 5: Reply in writing, item by item

A good mailed or uploaded response has four parts:

  1. A short cover letter. Your name, the tax year, the notice or letter number, and a one-sentence statement of what is enclosed.
  2. A numbered answer for each item. One number per questioned item, stating your position and pointing to the attached proof. Keep it factual and short.
  3. The supporting copies. Labeled to match the numbers in your letter, so the examiner can check each item without hunting.
  4. Proof you sent it. Certified mail with return receipt, fax confirmation, or the upload confirmation from the IRS Document Upload Tool. Keep that proof with the file.

Do not mail a hurried document dump the day the notice arrives. A short, organized response that answers exactly what was asked beats a thick folder of unsorted paper.

Step 6: Decide on representation before you are in too deep

You can represent yourself, and for a simple correspondence audit over one or two items with clean records, many people do. Bring in a CPA, enrolled agent, or tax attorney when the amounts are large, the issues are complex, more than one year is involved, the audit is a field audit, or you are unsure what the IRS is really asking. A representative is authorized with Form 2848, Power of Attorney, and the IRS then deals with them instead of you.

Step 7: What happens after you respond

Three outcomes, in order:

  • No change. The IRS accepts your records and closes the audit. You get a closing letter. Keep it with the file.
  • Agreed changes. The examiner proposes adjustments and you agree. You sign the examination report, and the IRS sends a bill for the additional tax plus interest and any penalties. Pay it in full within 21 days if you can.
  • Disagreed changes. You do not agree. The IRS sends a 30-day letter (Letter 525) with the proposed changes and your appeal rights. You have 30 days to agree and sign, submit more documentation, or request an appeal. If that is not resolved, the IRS issues a Notice of Deficiency (Letter 3219), which gives you 90 days to petition the U.S. Tax Court without paying first. Miss that 90-day window and the tax is assessed, and your remaining options are to pay and file a refund claim or request audit reconsideration.

If a bill is assessed and goes unpaid, collection follows the same path as any other tax debt: further notices, then a lien or levy. Our wage garnishment guide covers what happens if collection reaches your paycheck.

What not to do

  • Do not ignore the notice. The IRS does not need your cooperation to finish the audit. It will decide what you owe from its own records.
  • Do not pay a caller. Real IRS audit contact starts by mail. Calls, texts, or emails demanding immediate payment by gift card, wire transfer, or cryptocurrency are scams.
  • Do not send originals. Copies only. Originals sent to the IRS are rarely returned quickly, and sometimes never.
  • Do not volunteer extra years or issues. Answer what was asked. Extra material can widen the examination.
  • Do not sign a statute extension without understanding it. The IRS may ask you to sign Form 872 to extend the assessment deadline, generally three years from filing. You do not have to agree, but refusing means the auditor decides on the information provided. Get advice before signing.
  • Do not miss new filings. The audit covers a past year. New years still need filed returns on time, or the problem compounds.

Your rights, in one paragraph

The Taxpayer Bill of Rights applies through the whole examination: the right to be informed, to quality service, to pay no more than the correct amount of tax, to challenge the IRS position and be heard, to appeal most decisions in an independent forum, to finality, to privacy, to confidentiality, to retain representation, and to a fair and just tax system. An audit is an administrative review, not a criminal case, and you are allowed to disagree, to ask for a manager conference, to request mediation, and to appeal. If the process itself is causing hardship, the Taxpayer Advocate Service is an independent organization inside the IRS that helps taxpayers resolve problems.

FAQ

What is an IRS audit notice?

An audit notice is a mailed letter saying the IRS is examining one or more items on a tax return. It names the tax year, the items under review, the records requested, and a response deadline. An audit is a review, not a bill and not a finding of wrongdoing: being selected does not mean the return has an error.

How do I know if my IRS audit notice is real?

The IRS starts an audit by mail, never by phone call, text, or email. A real notice is a paper letter with a notice or letter number, a tax year, and a contact number you can verify at IRS.gov. Any call, text, or email demanding immediate payment by gift card, wire transfer, or cryptocurrency is a scam: do not pay and do not send personal information.

What is the difference between an audit notice and a CP2000?

A CP2000 is not an audit. It is an automated notice that income reported by employers, banks, or brokerages does not match what you reported, and it proposes a change. An audit notice, such as Letter 566 for a correspondence audit or Letter 2205 for a field audit, opens an examination of specific items and asks for supporting records. Both need a response by the stated deadline, but they are different proceedings.

How long do I have to respond to an IRS audit notice?

The deadline is printed on the notice, and it is usually 30 days. For audits conducted by mail, the IRS can ordinarily grant a one-time automatic 30-day extension if you fax a written request to the number on the letter before the due date. Do not assume a deadline from a general rule: the date on your notice controls.

What happens if I ignore an IRS audit notice?

The IRS finishes the audit without your input, using only the information it already has. It disallows the items it questioned and sends an audit report with proposed changes, which can lead to a Notice of Deficiency, an assessed tax bill with penalties and interest, and eventually collection action. Ignoring the notice never makes the audit go away.

Do I need a tax attorney for an IRS audit?

Not always. A simple correspondence audit over one or two items with clean records is often manageable on your own. Consider a CPA, enrolled agent, or tax attorney when the amounts are large, the issues are complex, more than one year is involved, or you are unsure what the IRS is really asking. A representative is authorized with Form 2848, and the IRS will then deal with them instead of you.

What documents do I need for an IRS audit?

Only what the notice asks for: the filed return for the year under review, plus the records that support each item questioned, such as receipts, bank statements, mileage logs, or 1099s. Send copies, never originals. Do not send records for years or issues the IRS has not opened, and do not create, alter, or backdate any record.

What if I disagree with the audit results?

You can ask for a conference with an IRS manager, request mediation, or file an appeal. The IRS first sends a 30-day letter with the proposed changes and your appeal rights. If that is not resolved, it issues a Notice of Deficiency, which gives you 90 days to petition the U.S. Tax Court without paying first. That 90-day deadline cannot be extended, so calendar it the day the notice arrives.
