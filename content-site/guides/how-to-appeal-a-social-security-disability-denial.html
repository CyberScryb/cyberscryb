How to Appeal a Social Security Disability Denial

A denial is not the end of your claim. Roughly two-thirds of initial SSDI claims are denied, and many of those denials get reversed on appeal, especially at the hearing stage. Here is the whole path: the 60-day deadline, reconsideration, the ALJ hearing, and the evidence that actually changes the outcome.

By CyberScryb 📅 Updated November 6, 2026 ⏱ 10 min read
⚡ Key Takeaways (TL;DR)

You have 60 days from the date you receive the denial notice to appeal, and SSA assumes you received it 5 days after the date printed on the letter, so your real deadline is about 65 days from that printed date. Do not file a new application; appeal instead, or you lose your original filing date and the back pay that goes with it. There are four appeal levels in order: reconsideration (Form SSA-561), a hearing before an administrative law judge (Form HA-501), Appeals Council review, and federal court. Reconsideration approves only about 10 to 15 percent of claims, but the hearing stage is where most reversals happen. What wins is not a better argument, it is new evidence of what you cannot do: treatment records, a residual functional capacity form from your treating doctor, and a concrete record of your daily limits.

Step 1: Read the Denial Letter and Find the Real Reason

The denial letter is the whole case against you in one document, and your appeal has to answer the specific reason in it. Most denials fall into two buckets, and they need completely different responses.

  • Medical denial. SSA decided your condition does not prevent you from working, or does not meet its duration requirement (expected to last at least 12 months or result in death). This is the common one. The letter usually cites the medical evidence it reviewed and the residual functional capacity, or RFC, it decided you still have: what it thinks you can still do in a workday, like lift 20 pounds or sit for 6 hours. Your appeal has to attack that RFC finding with better evidence.
  • Technical denial. The problem is not your health, it is the paperwork. For SSDI this usually means not enough work credits. For SSI it usually means income or resources above the limit. A technical denial is not fixed by more medical records; it is fixed by correcting the record, for example by proving missing earnings or showing that counted income should not count.

Read the letter twice. Note the date printed on it, the exact reason given, the onset date SSA accepted or rejected, and which doctors and records it says it reviewed. If it lists a doctor you never saw, or misses your main treating doctor entirely, that gap is part of your appeal. If the letter says your condition improved, or that you can do your past work, that tells you exactly which claim to rebut.

Step 2: Calendar the Deadline Before Anything Else

You have 60 days from the date you receive the decision to ask for an appeal, at every level. SSA assumes you received the notice 5 days after the date printed on it, unless you can show you got it later. In practice that gives you about 65 calendar days from the printed date. Miss the window and the denial becomes final.

  • Do the math today. Take the date printed on the denial letter, add 5 days for mailing, then add 60 days. Write that date on a calendar, on your phone, and on the envelope the letter came in.
  • File early, not perfectly. You can file the appeal and add evidence afterward. A thin appeal filed on day 20 beats a perfect appeal filed on day 66.
  • Late appeals need good cause. SSA may give you more time if you have a good reason, such as a serious illness, a death in the family, or never receiving the notice. "I forgot" and "I was busy" are not good cause. If you are already late, file anyway and explain the reason in writing.

Step 3: Appeal, Do Not File a New Application

The most common and most expensive mistake after a denial is starting over with a fresh application. It feels productive and it is usually the wrong move. A new application gets a new filing date, which can cost you months of back pay, and it sends you back to the start of the same queue with the same file. An appeal keeps your original filing date and moves your existing claim up the ladder, where a new decision-maker looks at it.

The one exception: if you missed the 60-day deadline with no good cause, the denial is final and a new application is your only path back in. Even then, file it knowing you have likely lost the back pay from the first claim. If you are inside the deadline, appeal. Every time.

Step 4: File Reconsideration (Form SSA-561)

Reconsideration is the first appeal level. A different reviewer at your state Disability Determination Services office, someone who had no role in the original decision, looks at your entire file plus any new evidence you submit. There is no hearing and no live testimony; the decision is made on the paperwork alone.

  • File Form SSA-561, Request for Reconsideration. You can file online at SSA.gov, by phone, by mail, or in person at a local office. If your appeal is about the medical decision, also complete Form SSA-3441-BK, the Disability Report Appeal, which collects updates on your conditions, new diagnoses, and any new providers you have seen since the last decision.
  • Expect a wait of three to seven months. Reconsideration is slow, and most requests are denied again. Published figures put approval somewhere around 10 to 15 percent. Go in with your eyes open: for most people, reconsideration is a required step on the way to the hearing, not the step where the case is won.
  • Do not skip it. You cannot get a hearing without going through reconsideration first (with a few narrow exceptions in states that run a pilot program). Filing it protects your filing date and keeps the claim alive.
  • Add new evidence now, not later. A reconsideration that submits the same file SSA already denied will get the same answer. New treatment records, a new diagnosis, a new specialist, or a treating doctor's statement that was not in the original file are what give the new reviewer something different to decide on.

Step 5: Build the Evidence That Changes the Outcome

SSA decides disability on function, not diagnosis. Two people with the same diagnosis get different answers because one can still work a desk job and the other cannot sit for an hour. Your evidence has to prove what you cannot do, in concrete terms, day after day. A diagnosis alone almost never wins an appeal.

  • Keep getting treatment. Gaps in treatment are read as proof you got better. If you cannot afford care, say so in writing and keep every free-clinic, ER, or urgent-care visit on the record. A thin file loses; a file that shows you kept seeking help wins credibility.
  • Get an RFC form from your treating doctor. A residual functional capacity form asks your own doctor to state your limits in work terms: how long you can sit, stand, and walk; how much you can lift; how often you need breaks; whether you can follow instructions reliably. SSA gives real weight to a treating doctor who has seen you over time, and an RFC form is the single most useful document in most appeals.
  • Write your own function report honestly. SSA will ask you to describe a typical day. Be specific instead of general: "I can stand for about ten minutes before my back forces me to sit" beats "I have a lot of pain." Describe what happens when you try: the task, how long it takes, what you have to stop doing, and how long recovery takes. Do not exaggerate and do not minimize. Inconsistencies between what you write and what your records show are the fastest way to lose credibility.
  • Collect third-party statements. A short written statement from a spouse, family member, former coworker, or supervisor who has watched your limits adds what medical records cannot: what your day actually looks like. Keep them short and factual, focused on observed limits rather than opinions about whether you are disabled.
  • Log failed work attempts. If you tried to return to work and could not sustain it, document it: dates, hours, duties, what went wrong, and why it ended. A failed work attempt is strong evidence that you cannot sustain full-time work, and SSA specifically considers unsuccessful work attempts in its decision.
  • Keep a simple daily diary. A dated notebook of symptoms, sleep, medication side effects, and what you could and could not do that day turns vague complaints into a dated record. Bring it to medical appointments so the limits get into your treatment notes too.

Step 6: The ALJ Hearing (Form HA-501)

If reconsideration denies you, the next step is a hearing before an administrative law judge, and this is where outcomes change most. You file Form HA-501, Request for Hearing by Administrative Law Judge, within 60 days of the reconsideration denial. A judge who had no part in the earlier decisions hears the case in person, by video, or by phone.

  • This is the stage that reverses denials. Advocates commonly cite approval rates near 45 to 55 percent at the hearing stage, against roughly 10 to 15 percent at reconsideration. The judge sees you, hears your testimony, and weighs your treating doctor's opinion directly, which is why this stage matters so much.
  • Expect a long wait. The average processing time at this stage is roughly 286 days from the date you file the hearing request, and in some regions the wait stretches past a year. Keep your records current while you wait: keep treating, keep the diary, and send updated records in as they come.
  • You get at least 75 days' notice of the hearing date. Use that time to prepare: review your file, line up your treating doctor's RFC form, and prepare to testify about your limits in plain language. You can bring a representative, and representation tends to help most at this stage.
  • All written evidence must reach the judge no later than five business days before the hearing. Anything later may not be considered. Do not save evidence for a dramatic reveal; get it in early.
  • Expect a vocational expert. The judge will usually have a vocational expert testify about whether jobs exist that someone with your limits could do. Your testimony and your RFC form are what push back on that. Be ready to explain, job by job, why past work is no longer possible for you.

Step 7: Appeals Council and Federal Court

If the judge denies you, two levels remain, and both are long shots. The Appeals Council (Form HA-520) reviews the judge's decision for legal or procedural error; it directly approves only about 1 percent of cases, denies or dismisses most, and sends roughly 15 percent back to the hearing level for a new decision. Federal court is the last step: a federal judge reviews whether SSA followed the law, and while about 65 percent of cases are sent back for another look, fewer than 1 percent are directly approved by the court. Both levels still carry the same 60-day filing deadline. If you reach this point, get a representative; the arguments are legal, not medical, and the record is already closed.

Mistakes That Kill Appeals

  • Missing the 60-day deadline. The single most common way a winnable claim dies. File first, perfect later.
  • Submitting no new evidence. The same file gets the same answer. Every level needs something the last reviewer did not have.
  • Stopping treatment. No visits means no records, and no records reads as no problem. Keep going even when it feels pointless.
  • Working above substantial gainful activity. Earning above SSA's threshold while claiming you cannot work is the fastest way to lose. Any work during an appeal needs a careful conversation with a representative first.
  • Exaggerating, or minimizing. Both destroy credibility. Describe your limits the same way to SSA, to your doctors, and in your diary, and let the record match.
  • Applying for a different benefit and abandoning the appeal. Filing a new claim instead of appealing resets the clock and usually forfeits back pay.

The Short Reconsideration Statement

The SSA-561 form asks why you disagree with the decision. Keep it short and tied to the denial letter. Here is a structure that works for a fictional applicant; swap in your own facts and your own denial reason:

Reconsideration Statement Example

I disagree with the determination dated [date] because it states I can perform light work, including standing for most of an 8-hour day. My treating physician, Dr. [Name], has documented that I cannot stand for more than 15 minutes at a time due to [condition], and that I must lie down for 30 minutes after standing or walking. I have attached Dr. [Name]'s residual functional capacity form dated [date], which was not in the file when the first decision was made.

Since the original application, my condition has worsened: [one or two sentences on what changed, for example a new diagnosis, a new medication with side effects, or a failed work attempt with dates]. I have also attached treatment records from [clinic or hospital] covering [dates], and a statement from my former supervisor describing the accommodations I needed and why they could not be sustained.

I am requesting a complete review of my claim with this new evidence. My onset date of [date] is supported by my treatment records beginning [date].

Notice the pattern: it quotes the decision back, names the specific new evidence, and ties both to a date. That is the whole strategy at every level. Disagree with the finding, supply the evidence the finding missed, and point at the record.

FAQ

How long does a Social Security disability appeal take?

Reconsideration usually takes three to seven months. If you go to an ALJ hearing, expect another eight to fourteen months or more after you request it, depending on your hearing office backlog. The full path from denial to a hearing decision commonly runs well over a year, which is why filing the appeal immediately matters more than filing it perfectly.

What are my chances of winning a disability appeal?

Reconsideration approves roughly 10 to 15 percent of claims, so most people who win do so later. At the ALJ hearing stage, advocates commonly cite approval rates near 45 to 55 percent. Your odds depend on your medical evidence, not on how forcefully you argue, so the work that moves the number is new evidence and a clear record of your functional limits.

Do I need a lawyer to appeal an SSDI denial?

No, you can appeal on your own at every level, and many people do. A disability representative or attorney usually works on contingency, taking a percentage of past-due benefits only if you win, with the fee capped by SSA rules, so there is generally no upfront cost. Representation tends to help most at the hearing stage, where cross-examining a vocational expert and framing the medical record matter. If your case is straightforward and your records are strong, self-representation at reconsideration is reasonable.

Can I work while my disability appeal is pending?

Working while you appeal is risky. Earning above substantial gainful activity levels signals to SSA that you can work, which undercuts the claim that you cannot. Short-term or very part-time work below those levels does not automatically kill a claim, but any work activity will be examined. Talk to a representative before taking a job during an appeal, and keep records of any work attempt, including hours, duties, and why it did or did not last.

What happens if I miss the 60-day appeal deadline?

If you miss the 60-day window without a good reason, the denial becomes final and you lose the right to appeal that claim. SSA may accept a late appeal if you can show good cause, such as a serious illness, a death in the family, or never receiving the notice. Without good cause, your only option is filing a brand-new application, which usually means a new filing date and lost back pay for the months in between.

What is the difference between SSDI and SSI when appealing?

SSDI is based on your work history and the Social Security taxes you paid; SSI is needs-based, for people with limited income and resources. The appeal process is the same for both: reconsideration, ALJ hearing, Appeals Council, and federal court, each with a 60-day deadline. The difference shows up in the denial reason. A technical denial for SSDI often means not enough work credits, while for SSI it often means income or resources above the limit. Read your letter to see which kind of denial you got.

What evidence helps most in a disability appeal?

The evidence that wins appeals describes what you cannot do, not just what diagnosis you have. Strongest items: treatment records showing ongoing care, a residual functional capacity (RFC) form completed by your treating doctor, your own function report describing a typical day in concrete terms, statements from people who see your limits daily, and a record of failed work attempts. A new diagnosis alone rarely changes a decision; new proof of functional limits does.
