How to Negotiate a Higher Salary

The first offer is rarely the final one. A professional framework for negotiating salary — what to ask, how to ask it, and when to take the whole package.

By CyberScryb 📅 Updated October 10, 2026 ⏱ 7 min read
⚡ Key Takeaways (TL;DR)

Always negotiate — employers expect it, and a professional counter backed by data almost never hurts the offer. Research the market band, anchor high within it, make one clear ask, and negotiate total compensation (bonus, equity, flexibility) when base pay is fixed. Never bluff a competing offer.

Always Negotiate — They Expect You To

The first number in an offer letter is an opening position, not a ceiling. Recruiters and hiring managers build negotiation into the process — the budget band has room in it. The fear that asking will make them walk away is the most expensive belief a candidate can hold; offers get pulled over ultimatums and hostility, not over a polite counter backed by data. Gratitude plus a clear ask is the frame: "I'm excited about this role, and based on my research and experience, I'd like to discuss the base."

Research Before You Anchor

You cannot negotiate without a number you can defend. Pull the market band for the role, your experience level, and your location from salary surveys and job postings — then anchor at the high end of that band. A counter 5-15% above the offer is standard and rarely raises an eyebrow; beyond 20% needs serious justification. Never anchor to a number you can't back up with data or value, and never name your current salary unless you have to — it anchors you low.

Negotiate the Whole Package, Not Just Base

Base salary is one line of the offer. Signing bonus, equity or RSUs, annual bonus target, remote flexibility, title, an early performance review, professional development budget, extra vacation days — each of these is negotiable, and together they can be worth more than the raise you asked for. When a recruiter says base pay is fixed (and bands sometimes really are fixed), the correct response is: "I understand. Let's talk about what else is possible." That sentence alone has won people signing bonuses and six-month reviews.

The Email Frame: Grateful, Clear, Easy to Say Yes To

The email has four parts: (1) genuine enthusiasm for the role and team; (2) one clear ask with a number; (3) two or three sentences of justification — your experience, the results you bring, the market data; (4) an open door: you're flexible and excited to find something that works. Short, professional, no ultimatums, no apologies for asking. Send it, then don't negotiate against yourself by following up three times — let them respond.

What to Avoid

  • Bluffing a competing offer — it gets checked, and it poisons trust
  • Ultimatums ("meet this or I walk") — you lose leverage you actually had
  • Apologizing for negotiating — asking is part of the process, not a favor
  • Negotiating before you have the full written offer
  • Accepting verbally on the phone before you've seen everything in writing

Frequently Asked Questions

Should I negotiate salary over email or phone?

Email gives you precision and a paper trail; calls allow real-time discussion. Many candidates do both: a thoughtful email, then a call.

How much more should I ask for?

A counter 5-15% above the offer is common and rarely offensive; beyond 20% needs strong justification. Anchor only to numbers you can back up.

Will negotiating hurt the offer?

Almost never, done professionally. Employers expect it. Gratitude plus a clear ask backed by data is the safest frame.

What if they say the salary is non-negotiable?

Negotiate everything else: signing bonus, equity, flexibility, title, review timeline, extra vacation. Total compensation is the real target.
