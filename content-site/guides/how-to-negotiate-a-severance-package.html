How to Negotiate a Severance Package

Direct answer: a severance offer is usually a starting point, not a final offer, and the strongest move is the least dramatic one. Do not sign on the spot. Take the agreement home, read every clause, rank what matters most to you, and counter in writing with specific, reasonable changes. Pay is only one term. The end date, the reference, the benefits coverage, and the restrictions you agree to can matter just as much for your next job, and several of them cost the employer little to grant.

Step 1: Read the whole agreement before you react to the number

The pay figure is the part everyone looks at first, and it is the part that tells you the least on its own. Read the full document, slowly, and make a list of every term that affects you after your last day. Most severance agreements include some version of these:

  • The payment itself. How much, in what form, and on what schedule. A lump sum and continued pay over time are not the same thing for budgeting, benefits, or later claims, so note which form is offered.
  • Benefits coverage. When health coverage ends, whether the employer continues any part of it, and what continuation options you will have.
  • Unused time off. Whether unused vacation or paid time off is paid out, and whether that payment is separate from the severance amount.
  • The release. What claims you give up by signing, and whether anything is excluded from that release.
  • Restrictions. Non-compete, non-solicitation, confidentiality, and non-disparagement clauses, and how long each one lasts.
  • References and announcements. What the employer will say when asked about you, who handles reference calls, and how your departure is described.
  • Return of property. What equipment, keys, and access must be returned, and by when.
  • Conditions and clawbacks. Anything that could reduce or require repayment of the package, and what triggers it.

Mark anything you do not understand, and do not guess at its meaning. A clause that reads like boilerplate can be the clause that limits your next job. If a term is unclear, that is a reason to ask for clarification in writing and to get advice, not a reason to sign and hope.

Step 2: Do not sign on the spot, and get the deadline in writing

In the meeting, the correct response to a severance offer is simple: thank them, say you will review it carefully, and ask for the offer and any deadline in writing. Do not negotiate the substance in the room while you are still processing the news of the departure itself. People agree to worse terms under time pressure than they would after a night to think, and employers know it.

Verify any signing deadline from the document itself, not from memory of the conversation. If the written deadline is genuinely short, that is useful information to pass to whoever reviews the agreement for you, and it is a legitimate reason to ask, in writing, for more time to review. Keep that request polite and brief. You are asking for time to read a binding contract, which is a reasonable request on its face.

Some agreements carry legally required review periods for certain workers. This guide does not state what those periods are, because they depend on the situation and the document. If your agreement mentions a review period, check that it matches what the document actually gives you, and get advice if it does not.

Step 3: Rank your priorities before you write a single counter

Most people walk into a severance negotiation with one goal, more money, and discover too late that another term mattered more. Before you counter, rank these in order for your situation:

  • Income bridge. How long you need coverage while you search, and whether a lump sum or continued pay fits your budget better.
  • Benefits continuity. Health coverage gaps are expensive and hard to fix after the fact. Continued coverage can be worth more than a slightly larger payment.
  • Your next job. A broad non-compete or a vague reference can cost you more than the severance is worth. Narrow restrictions and an agreed reference protect the search itself.
  • Reputation and record. How the departure is described, what goes in your file, and whether non-disparagement runs both ways.
  • Retirement and equity. What happens to retirement accounts, vesting, and any equity or bonus on your departure, and what action you must take by when.

Your ranking decides your counter. If benefits continuity is your top priority, do not spend all your negotiating capital on a larger payment and leave coverage untouched. If a non-compete would block your obvious next job, narrowing it may be the single most valuable change you can ask for, even if the pay figure stays put.

Step 4: Counter in writing, item by item

Put your counter in an email or letter, not in a hallway conversation. A written counter creates a record, keeps the tone professional, and lets the other side take it to whoever actually approves changes. Keep it short, and structure it like this:

  • Thank them. One line acknowledging the offer and the working relationship. It costs nothing and sets the tone.
  • Name each change specifically. Quote or describe the clause, then state the change you want. Vague asks get vague answers.
  • Give one short reason per change. A reason tied to your situation, not a complaint about the company. "Continued coverage through the end of the benefits month would avoid a gap while I arrange new coverage" is a reason. "You owe me" is not.
  • State what you are asking for clearly. If you want a different payment arrangement, a different end date, or a narrower restriction, say so in plain terms so the answer can be yes or no.
  • Close professionally. Say you would like to resolve this promptly and are available to discuss. No ultimatums, no deadlines of your own unless you mean them, and no legal threats.

Example language for a benefits ask: "Thank you for the offer. I would like to ask that health coverage continue through the end of the current coverage period, so there is no gap while I arrange new coverage. I am happy to discuss the other terms as written." Example language for a reference ask: "I would also like to confirm that reference inquiries will be directed to the named contact, and that the company will confirm dates and title only." Short, specific, and easy to approve.

Do not threaten legal action to gain leverage unless you actually intend to take it and have advice to back it up. An empty threat damages your credibility and can harden the other side. If you do have a genuine dispute, that conversation belongs with a professional, not in a counteroffer email.

Step 5: What to watch for, clause by clause

These are the terms that most often surprise people after signing. Check each one against your situation before you agree.

  • Release scope. Does it cover only claims related to your employment, or is it broader? Does it include claims you do not yet know about? Are any categories excluded? If you have an unresolved complaint about pay, discrimination, safety, or another serious issue, do not sign a broad release without advice.
  • Non-compete and non-solicitation. What work is restricted, in what area, and for how long? Does it block work for clients, or only for direct competitors? Would it prevent you from taking the job you are most likely to get next? Ask for a narrower scope, a shorter period, or a list of excluded employers if the clause as written blocks your realistic options.
  • Non-disparagement. Does it run both ways? A one-way clause means you cannot criticize the company while the company is free to criticize you. Mutual non-disparagement is a reasonable ask.
  • Confidentiality. What counts as confidential, and does the clause prevent you from describing your own work experience to a future employer? You need to be able to talk about your job history in a job search.
  • Clawback and repayment. Could the payment be reduced or reclaimed if you take another job, violate a restriction, or for another stated reason? Know the trigger before you sign, not after.
  • End date and title. Your official last day and job title follow you into reference checks and future applications. Confirm both in the agreement and in writing from the employer.
  • Payment timing and form. When is the payment made, in how many installments, and what conditions must be met first? A payment that depends on a later event is not the same as a payment on a fixed date.
  • Benefits, retirement, and bonuses. What happens to each one, and what do you need to do, by when, to protect it? Get the answers in writing, not from a verbal assurance in the meeting.

Step 6: Ask for the low cost terms first when leverage is thin

If the employer says the pay figure is fixed, that is not necessarily the end of the negotiation. Several valuable terms cost the employer little and are easier to approve than more money:

  • An agreed reference, with a named contact and agreed wording.
  • Mutual non-disparagement instead of one-way non-disparagement.
  • A narrower non-compete, or named employers excluded from it.
  • Continued benefits coverage for a defined period.
  • Outplacement services or a job search support allowance.
  • A later official end date, which can affect benefits and your resume timeline.
  • Permission to keep specified equipment, or a clear return schedule.
  • A written confirmation of final pay, unused time off, and benefits end dates.

Ask for these even if you are also asking for more pay. They are separate items, and the employer can grant some without granting all. A negotiation that ends with three small wins and the original pay figure is still a better outcome than a negotiation that ends with nothing because the only ask was a larger number.

Step 7: Know when to get professional advice, and when to walk away

Get advice before signing if the agreement is long or complex, includes a non-compete or a broad release, involves a dispute or a serious workplace complaint, or when the amount at stake matters to your finances. A short document review can catch a clause you would not have noticed, and it is far cheaper than trying to undo a signed agreement. If cost is a concern, ask upfront about a fixed fee for a review, and bring the full agreement, not a summary of it.

Also consider walking away from the package, or at least pausing, if signing would give up a claim you are not ready to give up, if a restriction would block your livelihood, or if you were pressured to sign without time to read the document. A severance package is a trade: payment in exchange for giving something up. If what you are giving up is worth more than what you are getting, declining is a real option, and it is one to discuss with a professional before the deadline passes.

One related point, stated generally: severance payments are commonly treated as taxable income, and the way a payment is structured can affect benefits claims and tax timing. This guide does not state rates, withholding rules, or filing steps, because those depend on your situation. Check with a qualified professional about the treatment that applies to you, and do not rely on a general statement in a meeting about how the payment will be handled.

Step 8: After you sign, close the loop in writing

  • Get a fully signed copy. Signed by both sides, with every page included. Keep it outside company systems, with your other employment records.
  • Confirm the practical dates in an email. Final pay date, benefits end date, equipment return, and reference contact. If it is not in writing, it did not happen.
  • Calendar every action the agreement requires of you. Return dates, benefits elections, retirement rollovers, and any deadline tied to a payment or restriction.
  • Do not post about the departure or the terms. A non-disparagement or confidentiality clause can cover social posts, and posts outlive the feeling that wrote them.
  • Start the next search with the reference already settled. You negotiated that term for a reason. Use the agreed contact and wording, and do not improvise a different story about the departure.

FAQ

Can you negotiate a severance package?

Often, yes. A first severance offer is usually a starting point, not a final number, especially when it is presented as a standard form. Employers expect some back and forth in many cases, and asking in writing, calmly and specifically, costs you nothing as long as you do not sign first and do not make threats. The leverage varies with your role, tenure, and circumstances, so keep the ask tied to your situation rather than to what someone else received.

Should I sign a severance agreement right away?

No. Take the document home, read every line, and get advice before you sign. A severance agreement usually includes a release, which means you give up the right to bring certain claims in exchange for the package. Once signed, that trade is very hard to undo. Any deadline for signing should be in the document itself, so verify it there rather than relying on what was said in the meeting.

What should I ask for besides more severance pay?

Look beyond the pay figure: the official end date and how it is described, continued benefits coverage, payment of unused time off, an agreed reference and neutral reference contact, outplacement help, a narrower non-compete or non-solicitation clause, mutual non-disparagement, and keeping or returning equipment on clear terms. Several of these cost the employer little and matter a lot to your next job search.

How do I counter a severance offer?

Counter in writing, briefly and professionally. Thank them for the offer, name the specific terms you would like changed, give one short reason for each change, and state what you are asking for in clear terms. Avoid ultimatums, emotional language, and legal threats. A counter that is easy to say yes to, item by item, gets further than a demand for a much larger figure with no reasoning behind it.

What is a release in a severance agreement?

A release is the part of the agreement where you agree not to bring certain claims against the employer, usually in exchange for the severance payment. Read its scope carefully: what claims it covers, whether it covers claims you do not yet know about, and whether anything is excluded. If you believe you have an unresolved complaint about pay, discrimination, safety, or another serious issue, get professional advice before signing a broad release.

Does severance affect unemployment benefits?

It can, depending on how the payment is structured and on the rules where you live, which vary. How the agreement describes the payment, its timing, and whether it is tied to a period of time can all matter. Do not assume severance and unemployment work the same way in every state. Check the rules that apply to you and ask a professional if the timing of your claim depends on the answer.

When should I get a lawyer to review a severance agreement?

Get advice before signing whenever the agreement is long or complex, includes a non-compete or broad release, involves a dispute or a serious workplace complaint, or when the amount at stake matters to your finances. A short review can catch a clause you would not have noticed, and it is far cheaper than trying to undo a signed agreement. If cost is a concern, ask upfront about a fixed fee for a document review.
