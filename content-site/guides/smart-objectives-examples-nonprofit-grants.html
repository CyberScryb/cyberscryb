SMART Objectives Examples for Nonprofit Grants

What SMART means for grant objectives, ten worked before-and-after examples across real nonprofit programs, and the language traps that flag weak proposals to funders.

By CyberScryb 📅 Updated October 18, 2026 ⏱ 10 min read
⚡ Key Takeaways (TL;DR)

A SMART objective for a grant proposal is a one-sentence, checkable promise: specific about who changes, measurable by how much, achievable with the resources requested, relevant to the goal, and time-bound within the grant period. Most funders want two to four objectives per goal, and they should promise outcomes (what changed for people) rather than outputs (what you did). Run each objective through the five tests, name your measurement method, and make sure the numbers match your logic model. A strong example: "By month 24, at least 120 of 150 enrolled households will maintain stable housing for 12 months, verified through case-management records."

What SMART Objectives Are (and Why Funders Demand Them)

A goal is the big change you want to see in the world: "reduce food insecurity in our county." An objective is a concrete, checkable step toward that goal inside the grant period: "by December 2027, increase the share of enrolled households reporting improved food security from 40 percent to 75 percent, as measured by the USDA household food security survey." Funders need objectives because a goal cannot be audited. They cannot tell from your proposal whether a dream will come true, but they can tell whether you hit 75 percent by the date you named.

SMART stands for Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, and Time-bound. Grant programs did not invent the acronym, but they turned it into the standard review screen. Program officers read dozens of proposals per round, and the objectives section is where they find out whether you are making promises you can keep. Vague objectives ("empower the community," "improve outcomes") tell a reviewer you have not thought about measurement. Specific, bounded promises tell them you have run this kind of program before, or at least planned one honestly.

The rest of your proposal depends on the objectives being solid. The evaluation section describes how you will measure each one. The budget explains what the measurement and the activities cost. The narrative ties activities to the outcomes the objectives promise. If an objective is weak, all three downstream sections wobble.

The Five SMART Tests, in Plain English

Read each objective aloud and run it through these five tests. An objective that fails any one of them will get flagged in review.

Specific: who exactly changes, and how? The test: could a reviewer who knows nothing about your program restate the objective in their own words without guessing? Name the population (not "clients," but "single mothers enrolled in the job-readiness track"), the change ("complete certification," not "improve skills"), and the direction (increase, decrease, maintain). If the objective could describe three different programs, it is not specific.

Measurable: by how much, and counted how? The test: could you check a box at the end of the grant period that says yes or no? Attach a number or percentage, a baseline where possible, and the data source. "Increase reading proficiency" is uncheckable; "increase the share of third graders reading at grade level from 35 percent to 55 percent, per district assessment data" is checkable. When the measure is a survey or an intake record, name it.

Achievable: with the staff, money, and time in this proposal? The test: if a program officer asks "how," does the answer point to real capacity? The number you promise has to line up with the activities, the staffing, and the budget you describe elsewhere. Promising to serve 2,000 families with one part-time coordinator fails this test even if everything else is perfect. Reviewers discount promises that outrun the plan.

Relevant: does it point at the goal and the funder's mission? The test: if you deleted this objective, would the goal still be reachable? Every objective should be a load-bearing step toward the stated goal, and it should matter to the funder reading it. A workforce objective inside a homelessness proposal has to connect the dots ("employed participants retain housing longer"), not sit there because it sounded good.

Time-bound: by when? The test: is there a date or a month number the funder can put on a calendar? "By month 18 of the grant," "by December 2027," "within one school year." Timelines also prove sequencing: process objectives land early, short-term outcomes mid-grant, and the big outcome changes land near the end.

Ten Weak Objectives, Rewritten SMART

Each example below shows the vague version first, then the rewrite, with a note on what changed and why a funder reads the second version differently. The scenarios are fictional but typical of the programs real nonprofits run.

1. Food bank

Before: "We will feed hungry families in our community."

After: "By December 2027, at least 70 percent of the 400 enrolled households will report improved food security on the USDA household food security module, up from a 35 percent baseline at intake."

The fix: a defined population (400 enrolled households, not "families in our community"), a baseline and a target, a named instrument, and a date. A funder can now verify the claim instead of trusting it.

2. Youth mentoring

Before: "Mentored youth will do better in school."

After: "By the end of the second school year, 60 percent of the 80 mentored students will improve their GPA by at least 0.5 points, verified through school records with parent consent."

The fix: "do better" became a specific threshold (0.5 GPA points), a headcount, a deadline, and a data source. The parent-consent detail shows the program has thought about data access, which reviewers notice.

3. Housing placement

Before: "We will help homeless individuals find housing."

After: "Within 24 months, 120 of the 150 enrolled households will be placed in stable housing and retain it for at least 12 months, verified through case-management records."

The fix: placement alone is an output. "Retain it for at least 12 months" turns it into an outcome, because keeping housing is the change that matters. The 120-of-150 framing sets an honest 80 percent target instead of implying perfection.

4. Health clinic

Before: "We will provide health screenings to the uninsured."

After: "By month 18, clinic staff will screen 1,000 uninsured adults for hypertension and diabetes, and 75 percent of those diagnosed will attend a follow-up visit within 90 days, tracked in the clinic's patient records."

The fix: two linked objectives in one. Screenings are the output; the follow-up rate is the outcome. Funders want to see the handoff from activity to behavior change spelled out, not assumed.

5. Workforce development

Before: "We will train people for good jobs."

After: "By month 20, 65 percent of the 200 training graduates will be employed at or above $17 per hour within six months of completion, verified through graduate follow-up surveys and employer confirmation."

The fix: "good jobs" became a wage floor, a placement rate, and a follow-up window. Naming the verification method matters because self-reported job placement is a metric funders distrust on its own.

6. Arts program

Before: "We will bring art to underserved neighborhoods."

After: "Within 12 months, 500 residents of the target ZIP codes will complete at least one free public art workshop, and 70 percent of surveyed participants will report increased connection to their neighborhood, measured by pre- and post-workshop surveys."

The fix: attendance is the output and "increased connection" is the measured outcome. Arts programs sometimes think SMART does not apply to them; a simple before-and-after survey makes it apply cleanly.

7. Animal shelter

Before: "We will rescue more animals and find them homes."

After: "By December 2027, the shelter will increase its live-release rate from 78 percent to 90 percent and reduce average length of stay from 21 days to 14 days, per shelter management records."

The fix: this one uses the funder-recognized metric (live-release rate) plus an operational measure. Funders trust industry-standard indicators, so borrow them instead of inventing your own.

8. Disaster relief

Before: "We will respond quickly when disasters strike."

After: "Within 72 hours of a declared disaster in the service area, relief teams will deliver emergency supplies to 90 percent of the households identified in the pre-registered vulnerability list, tracked through distribution logs."

The fix: "quickly" became 72 hours and "when disasters strike" became a defined trigger. Disaster work is inherently unpredictable, so the objective is framed around response readiness to a defined population rather than a number of disasters that cannot be forecast.

9. Early literacy

Before: "Children in our program will become better readers."

After: "By June 2027, the share of enrolled second graders reading at or above grade level will rise from 40 percent to 65 percent, measured by district benchmark assessments."

The fix: a grade-level target, a baseline-to-target spread, and the district's own assessment. Using the funder's trusted third-party measure beats building your own test for this one.

10. Senior wellness

Before: "We will improve the quality of life of elderly residents."

After: "Within 12 months, 200 enrolled seniors will complete the falls-prevention exercise program, and fall-related emergency department visits among participants will decrease by 25 percent, verified through participant self-report and hospital discharge data."

The fix: "quality of life" is the classic unmeasurable phrase. It became a concrete health outcome with two data sources that cross-check each other, which is exactly how an evaluator wants to see it.

Language Traps That Flag Weak Proposals

Funders and reviewers scan for specific wording tells. These are the ones that cost proposals.

Outputs dressed up as outcomes. "Serve 500 families," "hold 40 workshops," "distribute 10,000 meals" are counts of activity. They belong in the work plan, not the objectives section. An objective has to name the change that follows the activity: of those 500 families, how many improved their situation, and how do you know? If your objective could be true while nobody's life improved, it is an output.

"Serve," "help," "support," "empower," "assist." These are the five verbs that signal a vague objective. Replace them with verbs that name the change: "place," "retain," "complete," "improve," "reduce," "increase," "gain," "maintain." If you cannot replace the verb with a checkable action, you do not have an objective yet; you have a mission statement sentence.

The process objective pretending to be a result. "Hire a program coordinator by month 3" is a milestone, not an objective. It is fine in the timeline, but the objectives section is reserved for what the program achieves for its participants. A proposal whose "objectives" are all staffing and purchasing plans reads like a shopping list.

The passive-voice dodge. "Outcomes will be improved" hides who is responsible and what exactly will happen. Objectives should name the actor and the population: "participants will complete," "graduates will retain," "residents will report." If nobody is the subject of the sentence, the sentence cannot be verified.

Fifteen objectives for a $50,000 grant. Too many objectives tell a reviewer you cannot prioritize. Each objective implies measurement, staff time, and reporting burden. Six to eight strong objectives for a mid-size grant is plenty; two to four per goal. If you have more, fold the small ones into the work plan.

The perfect 100 percent promise. "100 percent of participants will achieve..." reads as padded to anyone who has run a program. Real attrition, real data gaps, and real life exist. Targets in the 60 to 85 percent range with a clear rationale read as honest and experienced. Ambitious but reachable beats flawless but unbelievable.

How Funders Actually Read Your Objectives

A program officer reads your objectives next to your logic model. The logic model lays out inputs (staff, money, partners), activities (what you do), outputs (counts), and outcomes (short-term, intermediate, and long-term changes). Your objectives should be the short-term and intermediate outcomes from that model, written as sentences. When the numbers, populations, and timelines in the objectives match the logic model, the reviewer sees a coherent program. When they contradict each other, the proposal loses credibility on both pages.

Read each objective through the reviewer's eyes: which activity produces this outcome, which output is the bridge, and is the measurement method named somewhere believable? The 60-Second SMART Checklist

Before the objectives section leaves your desk, answer yes to all of these:

  • Named population? Every objective says exactly who changes, not "clients" or "the community."
  • Number attached? Every objective has a count, percentage, or threshold.
  • Baseline? Where it makes sense, the objective or evaluation section states where you started.
  • Measurement named? Every objective points to a data source: survey, records, assessments, or intake data.
  • Change verb? No "serve, help, support, empower, assist." Only verbs you can check.
  • Outcome, not output? Every objective names what changed for people, not what you did.
  • Matches the budget and staffing? The promised numbers are reachable with the resources described.
  • Date or month number? Every objective has a deadline inside the grant period.
  • Matches the logic model? Populations, numbers, and timelines are identical in both places.
  • Believable targets? No 100 percent promises; each target has a one-sentence rationale.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a SMART objective in grant writing?

A SMART objective is a one-sentence promise of what your program will accomplish that is Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, and Time-bound. In grant writing it sits under a broad goal and names who changes, by how much, and by when, so a funder can check whether you did what you said you would do.

What is the difference between a goal and an objective in a grant proposal?

A goal is the broad, long-term change you want to see, like reducing homelessness in your county. An objective is a concrete, measurable step toward that goal within the grant period, like placing 120 households into stable housing within 24 months. Funders expect one to three goals and two to four measurable objectives per goal.

What is the difference between outputs and outcomes?

Outputs count what you did: 500 meals served, 40 workshops held, 200 clients enrolled. Outcomes describe what changed for the people you served: 70 percent of participants improved their food security scores, 55 percent of workshop graduates gained employment. Funders fund change, not activity, so objectives should promise outcomes, and the outputs belong in your work plan.

How many objectives should a grant proposal have?

Most funders expect two to four objectives per goal, and most proposals have one to three goals. Six to eight strong objectives total is a healthy range. If you find yourself writing fifteen objectives, you are listing activities. Each objective should be big enough that a funder would recognize it as a real result.

Can a SMART objective be qualitative?

Yes, as long as you say how you will measure it. Seventy percent of participants will report increased confidence in managing their health, measured by a pre- and post-program survey, is a legitimate SMART objective. Qualitative outcomes still need a data source named in the objective or the evaluation section so the funder trusts the claim.

Should a SMART objective include how I will measure it?

It helps, especially when the measure is not obvious. Naming the instrument (a validated survey, intake records, school attendance data) inside the objective proves you already know how you will track progress. If you have a dedicated evaluation section that names every measure, you can keep the objective itself lean and let the evaluation section carry the detail.

What happens if we do not meet an objective we promised a funder?

Missing an objective is not automatically fatal, but hiding it is. Funders expect honest final reports: what you hit, what you missed, why, and what you learned. Grants that show 100 percent success on every objective can look padded. The practical guard is to set ambitious but reachable targets and document external factors, so a shortfall reads as context rather than failure.

How do SMART objectives connect to a logic model?

Your objectives are the short-term and intermediate outcomes column of your logic model written as sentences. Activities produce outputs, outputs lead to the outcomes your objectives promise, and the outcomes roll up into your long-term goal. A funder reading both side by side should see the same numbers and the same timeline in each.
