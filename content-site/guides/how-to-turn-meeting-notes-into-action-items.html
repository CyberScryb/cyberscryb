The Four Things a Meeting Owes You

Most meeting notes fail because they are a transcript of what was said rather than a record of what was decided. After every meeting you need exactly four things: (1) a 2-3 sentence summary of what it was about and where it landed; (2) the decisions — only what the group actually agreed to, not what was debated; (3) action items in verb + owner + due date form; (4) open questions still unresolved. If your notes do not contain these four, they are a diary, not a work product.

Write Action Items That Survive the Week

  • Start with a verb: "Update the launch one-pager" not "Launch one-pager"
  • One owner per item — shared ownership means no ownership
  • Include the due date as stated; if none was stated, write "due: not stated" rather than inventing one
  • Keep each item to one line so it pastes cleanly into Slack, email, or a task manager
  • Separate decisions from discussion — debated options are not decisions

The Follow-Up Email That Kills the Re-Meeting

Send the recap to attendees within the hour, while it is fresh: subject line with the meeting name and date, the decisions in two bullets, the action items with owners, and the open questions. This one email is what prevents the "wait, what did we decide?" meeting next week. Keep it short enough to read on a phone.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the best format for meeting action items?

Verb + owner + due date on one line: "[ ] Update the launch one-pager — Owner: Marcus, Due: Friday." One owner per item, always.

How soon should meeting notes go out?

Within the hour. Memory decays fast, and the recap is most useful while the conversation is fresh. Same-day at the latest.

Should I send notes even for small meetings?

Yes — especially 1:1s. A running record of what you and your manager agreed to prevents most misalignment disputes.

What if no decisions were made?

Say so explicitly and list what is blocking the decision. An honest "no decision — blocked on pricing approval" beats a vague recap.
