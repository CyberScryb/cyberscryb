What Is TDEE?

Total Daily Energy Expenditure is the single most useful number in nutrition: every calorie you burn in 24 hours. The four components, the formula behind it, and how to eat around it.

The Direct Answer

TDEE stands for Total Daily Energy Expenditure. It is the total number of calories your body burns in 24 hours, everything included: keeping you alive at rest, walking to the kitchen, working out, digesting lunch, all of it. Every diet plan on earth is just a strategy for eating relative to this number. Below it, you lose weight. Above it, you gain. At it, you hold steady. Once you know your TDEE, calorie targets stop being guesswork and become arithmetic.

Most people dramatically misjudge this number. Some assume a workout burns thousands of calories (it does not; an hour of moderate exercise is often 300 to 500). Others assume sitting still means burning nothing (wrong; your body runs a furnace around the clock). TDEE replaces both errors with one measured estimate.

The Four Components of TDEE

TDEE is not one thing. It is four buckets of calorie burn stacked on top of each other:

BMR, basal metabolic rate: 60 to 70% of TDEE. The energy your body spends just existing: breathing, blood circulation, brain function, cell repair, body temperature. This runs all day and all night and it is by far the biggest component. A bigger body and more muscle mean a higher BMR.

NEAT, non-exercise activity thermogenesis: roughly 15 to 20%. Every calorie burned outside of sleep, eating, and planned exercise. Walking, standing, typing, fidgeting, housework, playing with your kids. This is the sneakiest variable in the whole formula: two people with identical desk jobs can differ by up to 800 calories a day just from how much they move without thinking. NEAT is why some people seem to eat whatever they want.

TEF, the thermic effect of food: roughly 10%. Digestion costs energy. Protein is expensive to process (20 to 30% of its own calories), carbs are cheaper (5 to 10%), and fat is cheapest (0 to 3%). This is a minor lever for weight loss but a real one for food choices.

Exercise activity thermogenesis: roughly 5 to 10%. Planned workouts: lifting, running, sports, classes. Notice how small this is. Exercise matters enormously for health, muscle, and mood, but as a pure calorie burner it is the smallest bucket. You cannot out-train a fork. That is why the calculator cares more about your honest daily activity level than your gym schedule.

The Mifflin-St Jeor Formula

Most TDEE calculators estimate BMR with the Mifflin-St Jeor equation, the most validated resting-metabolism formula for general use. It takes four inputs: sex, weight, height, and age.

Male BMR = 10 x weight (kg) + 6.25 x height (cm) - 5 x age + 5

Female BMR = 10 x weight (kg) + 6.25 x height (cm) - 5 x age - 161

TDEE = BMR x activity multiplier

Worked example. A 30-year-old woman, 5'5" (165 cm), 140 lb (63.5 kg), lightly active. BMR = 10 x 63.5 + 6.25 x 165 - 5 x 30 - 161 = 635 + 1,031.25 - 150 - 161 = 1,355 cal/day. Multiply by the lightly active factor of 1.375: TDEE = 1,863 cal/day. Her cut target (20% deficit) is about 1,491, her bulk target (10% surplus) about 2,050. Activity Multipliers

The multiplier is where most people go wrong, usually upward. Pick the level that describes your real week:

Activity levelWhat it meansMultiplier
SedentaryLittle or no exercise, desk job1.2
Lightly activeLight exercise or sports 1-3 days/week1.375
Moderately activeModerate exercise or sports 3-5 days/week1.55
Very activeHard exercise 6-7 days/week1.725
Extra activePhysical job or training twice a day1.9

When in doubt, round down. An overestimated multiplier inflates the whole number, which makes your cut target too high and your deficit disappear. If you sit most of the day and train three times a week, you are moderately active at best. The extra active tier belongs to manual labor and two-a-day athletes.

How to Use TDEE for Cut, Maintain, or Bulk

Your TDEE is the anchor. Every goal is just a fixed offset from it.

Cut: eat at TDEE x 0.8. A 20% deficit is the standard sustainable cut. The math behind fat loss: a pound of body fat holds roughly 3,500 calories, so a daily deficit of 500 calories loses about a pound a week. A 20% deficit on a 2,000-calorie TDEE is 400 a day, about 0.8 lb per week. Never eat below your BMR for extended periods; the deficit should come from the gap between BMR and TDEE, not from starving your organs.

Maintain: eat at your TDEE. Weight holds steady. This is also the recomp zone: beginners who lift hard while eating at maintenance can slowly trade fat for muscle, because the training stimulus partitions nutrients toward muscle growth.

Bulk: eat at TDEE x 1.1. A 10% surplus gives your body the raw material to build muscle without piling on fat. That is roughly 200 to 300 extra calories a day, gaining about half a pound per week, and with strength training most of it should be muscle. Push the surplus higher and the extra weight is almost all fat, which you will have to cut later anyway.

Accuracy Limits: What the Number Cannot See

The formula estimates your BMR from population averages, so expect any TDEE estimate to be off by 10 to 20% for a given individual. It cannot measure your muscle mass, and muscle burns more at rest than fat does, so two people at the same weight can have very different BMRs. It cannot see genetics, thyroid or hormonal conditions, medications, sleep debt, or chronic stress, all of which move the needle. And it cannot measure your NEAT, which as noted can swing 800 calories a day.

The practical fix is to treat TDEE as a starting point, not a verdict. Eat at your target for two to three weeks and track your weight. If the scale moves the wrong way or stalls, adjust by 100 to 200 calories and repeat. The formula gives you the compass heading; the scale gives you the map. Also recalculate when things change: after losing 10 or more pounds, changing activity level, or starting a new training program, because the TDEE of your old body no longer applies.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is TDEE in simple terms?

TDEE is the number of calories your body burns in 24 hours. It covers everything: your resting metabolism, daily movement, workouts, and digestion. Eat more than it and you gain weight; eat less and you lose it. It is the single most useful number in nutrition.

What are the 4 components of TDEE?

The four components are BMR (basal metabolic rate, 60 to 70% of the total), NEAT (non-exercise activity thermogenesis, the calories from daily movement like walking and standing), TEF (the thermic effect of food, roughly 10%, spent digesting what you eat), and exercise activity thermogenesis (planned workouts, typically 5 to 10% for most people).

Should I eat below my BMR to lose weight?

No. Your BMR is the floor your body needs to run its basic systems. Aim to eat below your TDEE but above your BMR. If your TDEE is very close to your BMR, for example because you are sedentary, raise your activity to create the deficit instead of starving your body.

Why is my TDEE different from a friend's?

Because every input differs: sex, age, height, weight, muscle mass, activity level, and NEAT. NEAT alone can swing the result by 800 calories a day between two people with the same desk job. Same formula, different people, different numbers.

How often should I recalculate my TDEE?

Recalculate whenever something changes: significant weight change (10 lb or more), a new activity level, a new training program, or aging into a new decade. During a cut, recalculate every month or two, since a smaller body burns fewer calories and the original TDEE stops being accurate.
