How to Defend a Debt Collection Lawsuit

You got sued over a debt. The summons has a deadline, the collector has to prove the debt is yours and the amount is right, and doing nothing is the one move that guarantees a loss. Here is what to do first, what defenses actually get used, and how to show up prepared.

By CyberScryb 📅 Updated November 11, 2026 ⏱ 11 min read
⚡ Key Takeaways (TL;DR)

A summons is the start of a civil case, not a judgment. Your first job is the answer deadline printed on the summons: it varies by state, and missing it can hand the collector a default judgment without a hearing. The collector has to prove the debt is yours, the amount is correct, and it has the right to collect it. Common defenses are mistaken identity, wrong amount, an expired statute of limitations, and lack of standing. Gather every record about the debt before you file anything, and get help from a legal aid organization or a consumer attorney if the amount is large or the facts are messy. What a summons means

A summons and complaint are the papers that start a civil lawsuit. The complaint says who is suing you, how much they claim you owe, and what the debt supposedly is. The summons tells you which court the case is in and the deadline to respond. Neither document means you have already lost, and neither is a criminal charge. Debt collection lawsuits are civil cases, and you cannot be jailed for owing a debt in the usual sense. What you can lose is a judgment, and that only happens after the case runs its course or after you fail to respond.

Read the whole complaint, not just the amount at the top. Note the name of the plaintiff, because it is often not the company you originally owed money to. Debts are bought and sold, sometimes several times, and the company suing you may be a debt buyer you have never heard of. Note the account number, the claimed balance, the date of the last payment the complaint lists, and the court and case number. Write all of it down in one place, because you will need it for every step that follows.

Also note how you were served. In most places the papers must be delivered in a specific way, such as handed to you in person or left with someone at your home, and the person who served them files proof of service with the court. If the papers were just left on your door, mailed to an old address, or handed to a stranger, that may matter, and it is worth telling the court clerk or a legal aid organization exactly what happened. Do not assume service was proper, and do not assume it was not. The proof of service is part of the court file, and you can ask the clerk for a copy.

The answer deadline, and why it comes first

The single most important date in the case is the deadline to file your answer, and it is printed on your summons. Deadlines vary by state and sometimes by county, commonly in the range of 14 to 30 days from when you were served. Some places give more time, some less, and the clock usually starts on the service date, not the day you open the envelope. There is no universal number, so never take a deadline you read in an article, including this one, at face value. Check the summons itself and your state or local court rules, and if the two disagree, ask the court clerk which one controls.

An answer is your formal response to the complaint. In it you respond to each claim the collector makes, usually by admitting, denying, or saying you do not have enough information to admit or deny it. You also list your defenses. The answer is usually a short form available from the court clerk or your state court website, and it gets filed with the court and served on the collector's attorney. Filing fees for an answer are often low or waived, and many courts have a fee waiver form if you cannot afford it.

If the deadline is days away, do not spend it researching the perfect defense. File a short answer that denies the claims you are not sure about and lists the defenses you know apply, then build the details after. A timely, imperfect answer keeps the case alive. A late answer, or no answer, can end it against you. If you have already missed the deadline, do not assume the case is over. Contact the court clerk or a legal aid organization right away and ask whether you can still file, and whether a default judgment has already been entered.

What happens if you do nothing

If you do not answer, the collector can ask the court for a default judgment, and courts often grant it without a hearing, because there is no defense on the record. A default judgment is a court order saying you owe the amount claimed, and it gives the collector tools to collect: wage garnishment, a bank levy, or a lien on property, depending on your state. Some income, such as Social Security benefits, is generally protected, and many states have exemptions for a portion of wages or a bank balance, but those protections do not apply themselves. You usually have to claim them.

A judgment also lasts. In most states a judgment is good for a number of years, commonly 10 to 20, and can sometimes be renewed. Interest can accrue on it. It can show up on credit reports and background checks, and it can make it harder to rent an apartment or get a loan. None of that is a reason to panic. It is a reason to answer the summons, because answering is the step that keeps all of your other options open.

If a default judgment has already been entered against you, it may still be possible to ask the court to set it aside, especially if you were never properly served or you had a good reason for missing the deadline. The window to ask is usually short, and the standard is strict, so get help from a legal aid organization or a consumer attorney as soon as you find out about the judgment, and bring the summons, the proof of service, and any records of where you lived when the papers were supposedly delivered.

Your rights under the FDCPA

The Fair Debt Collection Practices Act is a federal law that limits how third-party debt collectors can behave. It generally covers debts collected by a third party, not the original creditor collecting its own debt, though many states have similar rules that do cover original creditors. Knowing the basics helps you spot conduct that crosses a line and gives you a record to show a lawyer or the court.

  • Validation. Within five days of first contacting you, a collector must send a written notice with the amount of the debt, the name of the creditor, and a statement that you have 30 days to dispute it. If you dispute the debt in writing within that 30-day window, the collector must stop collection activity until it provides verification of the debt.
  • No harassment or threats. Collectors cannot call at unreasonable hours, use abusive language, threaten arrest, or threaten to take action they do not intend to take or are not legally allowed to take.
  • No lies about the debt. Collectors cannot misstate the amount, the legal status of the debt, or their identity. They cannot claim to be a lawyer or a government agency when they are not.
  • Work and contact limits. A collector cannot contact you at work after you tell them your employer does not allow it, and must stop contacting you altogether if you send a written cease-and-desist, except to tell you it is ending collection or that it intends to sue.
  • Third parties. A collector generally cannot discuss your debt with anyone except you, your spouse, or your attorney. It may contact other people only to find your address or phone number, and it cannot say it is collecting a debt.

Keep a written log of every contact: the date, the time, who called, what they said, and what you said back. Save every letter, text, and voicemail. If a collector breaks the law, that log is the evidence a consumer attorney or your state attorney general's office will want to see, and some consumer attorneys take FDCPA cases on contingency, meaning you pay nothing unless they win.

Gathering your debt records

Before you file anything, pull together every record you have about the debt. The collector's case is only as strong as its paperwork, and your defense is only as strong as yours. You do not need a perfect file. You need a complete one, organized by date.

  • The original account. Statements, the original agreement, and anything showing the account number, the original creditor, and the balance when the account was open.
  • Payment history. Bank statements, cancelled checks, payment app records, and receipts showing what you paid, when, and to whom. The date of your last payment matters, because it usually sets the statute of limitations clock.
  • Collector letters. Every letter, email, and text from the collector or debt buyer, including the validation notice, any settlement offers, and any letter claiming the debt was sold.
  • Your validation request. If you sent a written dispute or a request for validation, keep a copy and proof you sent it, such as the certified mail receipt. If you have not sent one and the lawsuit is already filed, a validation request still matters for the record, but it does not stop the lawsuit clock, so file your answer first.
  • Credit reports. Your reports from the three major credit bureaus, which show the account, the reported balance, the date of last activity, and whether the debt is listed under a name or account number you do not recognize.
  • Identity theft records. If the debt is not yours, a police report, an FTC identity theft affidavit, or a letter from the original creditor saying you never had the account. File a dispute with the credit bureaus too.
  • Your timeline. A one-page list of dates: when the account was opened, when you last paid, when the account was charged off, when the collector first contacted you, when you disputed it, and when you were served. Dates are the backbone of a debt defense, and a clear timeline is easier for a judge to follow than a stack of loose papers.

Do not throw anything away, and do not admit the debt is yours in writing or on a recorded call until you know where the statute of limitations stands. In some states, a payment or a written acknowledgment can restart the clock on a debt that was about to be time-barred. If a collector calls, you can say you are reviewing the matter and ask for everything in writing.

Common defenses, and how they actually work

A defense is a reason the collector should not win, or should not win the full amount it claims. You have to raise your defenses in your answer. The court will not raise them for you, and a defense you never mention is usually treated as waived. These are the defenses that come up most often. Which ones fit your case depends on your facts and your state law.

  • The debt is not mine. Mistaken identity, a mixed-up file, or identity theft. The collector has to prove you are the person who owes the debt, and a debt buyer with a spreadsheet of purchased accounts sometimes cannot tie a specific account to a specific person.
  • Wrong amount. The balance is inflated by fees, interest, or charges that were never in the agreement, or payments you made were never credited. Make the collector show the math: the original balance, every charge added, and every payment subtracted.
  • Statute of limitations. The collector sued after the legal window closed. The length of the window depends on your state and the type of debt, and commonly runs three to six years from the last payment or last activity, though it can be shorter or longer. This is a defense you must raise in your answer.
  • Lack of standing. The company suing you cannot show it owns the debt. Debts change hands, and the buyer has to prove the chain: the original creditor sold it, each buyer sold it on, and this plaintiff is the one holding it now. A bill of sale that lists a batch of accounts without your account on it is weak evidence, and judges do notice.
  • No proof of the debt. The collector has no contract, no statements, and no records showing the account terms or the balance. A bare claim that you owe a round number, with no documents behind it, is not the same as proof, and the burden is on the collector to produce the records.
  • Already paid or settled. You paid the debt, settled it for less, or the account was discharged in bankruptcy. Bring the receipts, the settlement letter, or the discharge order.
  • Improper service. The papers were never properly delivered to you. If the court never had jurisdiction over you because service was defective, a judgment entered against you can be challenged, but the window to challenge it is usually short.

Do not invent a defense you cannot support. A judge who sees one honest, documented defense trusts you more than a defendant who throws every defense at the wall. Pick the ones your records actually back, and be ready to show the document that proves each one.

Preparing for the hearing

Many debt collection cases never reach a hearing. They settle, they get dismissed because the collector cannot produce the records, or they end in a default judgment because the defendant never answered. If yours does reach a hearing, preparation is what decides it.

  1. Know the case file. Read the complaint and your answer again, and read anything the collector filed since. Know what they claim, what you denied, and what defenses you raised, because the judge will ask about them.
  2. Organize your exhibits. Bring three copies of each document: one for the judge, one for the collector's attorney, and one for yourself. Number the exhibits and keep a list of what each one is, so you can hand over the right paper in seconds.
  3. Write your one-page story. The account, the last payment date, the dispute, and the defense, in date order, one paragraph each. If a sentence has no date and no document behind it, cut it or find the document.
  4. Expect settlement talk. The collector's attorney may offer a settlement before the hearing, often for less than the claimed amount, sometimes on a payment plan. Do not agree to anything on the spot unless you can afford the payments and the deal is in writing. A settlement that includes a lump sum you cannot pay is worse than no settlement. If you do settle, get the terms in writing before you pay anything, and make sure the agreement says the case will be dismissed once you pay.
  5. Plan the room. Arrive at least 30 minutes early and check in with the clerk. Dress neatly, turn your phone off, and address the judge as Your Honor. Answer the question asked, then add the context. Do not interrupt the other side. If the judge asks whether something is true, say so if it is. Judges trust people who concede the small points.

In the hearing itself, the collector goes first, because it has the burden of proof. Its attorney will present its documents and ask the judge to rule. Your job is to point out what is missing or wrong: the account is not yours, the amount does not add up, the statute of limitations has run, or the plaintiff cannot show it owns the debt. You do not have to prove you owe nothing. You have to show the collector has not proved what it claims.

Costs, fees, and what help actually costs

Defending a debt case is cheaper than most people expect, and the cost of doing nothing is usually higher.

  • Filing an answer: often free or a small fee, and many courts have a fee waiver form if you cannot afford it. Ask the clerk.
  • Copies and postage: three copies of every exhibit, plus certified mail for anything you send to the collector. Print at home or the library, not the courthouse.
  • Legal aid: free in many areas for people below an income limit. Search for your state's legal aid organization, or call 211 and ask for consumer debt help. Law school clinics also take these cases.
  • Consumer attorney: many take debt defense cases for a flat fee or on contingency, especially where an FDCPA violation is involved, because the law can make the collector pay the attorney's fees. A short consultation, often free, is worth it before you file your answer if the amount is large.
  • Your time: the answer, the hearing, and the follow-up. Half a day at minimum, usually more. It is time well spent next to a default judgment that can follow you for a decade.

When to consult an attorney

Many debt collection cases can be handled without a lawyer, especially when the amount is small and the defense is simple, such as a debt that is clearly not yours. But some situations call for one, and the consultation is often free or low cost. Get a lawyer if any of these apply:

  • The amount claimed is large, or a judgment would mean garnishment you cannot afford.
  • You have already missed the answer deadline or a default judgment was entered.
  • The collector has violated the FDCPA, which may give you a claim of your own.
  • The debt involves a mortgage, a car loan, or a debt secured by property you could lose.
  • You are being sued in a court far from where you live, or the plaintiff is claiming a debt you settled or that was discharged in bankruptcy.
  • You are not sure which defenses apply, or the collector's attorney is pressing you to sign something you do not understand.

If you cannot afford a lawyer, start with legal aid in your state, a law school clinic, or your state bar's lawyer referral service, which often includes a low-cost first consultation. Bring the summons, the complaint, and every record you gathered, so the lawyer can give you a real answer instead of a general one.

Mistakes that backfire

  • Ignoring the summons. The one move that guarantees a loss. A default judgment can follow without a hearing.
  • Admitting the debt on a call. A payment or a written acknowledgment can restart the statute of limitations clock in some states. Ask for everything in writing and check the deadline first.
  • Missing the deadline while researching. File a short answer on time, then build the details. A timely, imperfect answer beats a perfect one that is late.
  • Throwing every defense at the wall. One honest, documented defense beats five you cannot support. Pick the ones your records back.
  • Agreeing to a settlement you cannot pay. A payment plan you miss can leave you worse off than before, with a judgment on the record. Get the terms in writing and make sure you can afford them.
  • Bringing a story instead of a file. "I paid it" is not evidence. The receipt is. If it is not written down, dated, and in your file, the judge may never hear it.
  • Paying the collector without a paper trail. Pay by check or bank transfer, never cash, and keep the receipt and the written agreement. A payment with no record is a payment you cannot prove.
  • Assuming the collector's math is right. Balances get inflated by fees and interest that were never in the agreement. Make them show the calculation.

FAQ

What does a debt collection summons mean?

A summons means a collector or a debt buyer has filed a lawsuit against you and is asking the court to order you to pay. It is not a notice that you already owe a judgment, and it is not a criminal charge. It is the start of a civil case, and the summons itself tells you the deadline to respond, the court, and the amount claimed. Read the whole document, not just the amount. This guide is general information, not legal advice.

How long do I have to answer a debt collection lawsuit?

It depends on your state and sometimes your county. Deadlines are commonly in the range of 14 to 30 days from when you were served, but some places give more or less, and the clock usually starts on the service date, not the day you open the envelope. Never assume one number is universal. The deadline is printed on your summons and in your state or local court rules. Check both, and if the deadline is close, contact the court clerk or a legal aid organization the same day.

What happens if I ignore a debt collection lawsuit?

If you do nothing, the collector can ask the court for a default judgment, and courts often grant it without a hearing, because there is no defense on the record. A default judgment lets the collector pursue wage garnishment, a bank levy, or a lien, depending on your state. Ignoring the case does not make the debt go away, and it removes your chance to raise any defense, including that the debt is not yours or that the amount is wrong. Answering, even with a short general denial, keeps the case alive.

What rights do I have under the FDCPA?

The Fair Debt Collection Practices Act is a federal law that limits how third-party debt collectors can contact you and what they can do. Collectors cannot call at unreasonable hours, threaten you, lie about the amount, or contact you at work after you tell them not to. You also have the right to ask for validation of the debt and to dispute it, and the collector must stop collection activity until it provides verification. The FDCPA generally covers debts collected by third parties, not the original creditor collecting its own debt, though many states have similar rules for original creditors.

What is the statute of limitations on a debt?

The statute of limitations is the window during which a collector can legally sue you over a debt. The length depends on your state and the type of debt, such as a written contract, an open account like a credit card, or an oral agreement, and it commonly runs three to six years, though it can be shorter or longer. The clock usually starts on the date of your last payment or last activity on the account, but the exact rule varies. If the deadline has passed, the debt may be time-barred, which is a defense you have to raise yourself, because the court will not raise it for you. Be careful: in some states, making a payment or acknowledging the debt in writing can restart the clock, so do not admit or pay anything until you know where the deadline stands.

Can a debt collector sue me for a debt that is not mine?

They can file the suit, but they have to prove the debt is yours, the amount is correct, and they have the right to collect it. Debts are bought and sold in bulk, sometimes with incomplete records, and collectors do sue the wrong person or the right person for the wrong amount. If the debt is not yours, that is a defense, and your answer should say so. Gather anything that proves it: your own account records, a letter from the original creditor, proof of identity theft, or records showing you never had an account with that creditor.

What should I bring to a debt collection hearing?

Bring the summons and complaint, your filed answer with the court stamp, every document about the debt you have, and three copies of each document: one for the judge, one for the collector's attorney, and one for yourself. That includes account statements, payment records, letters from the collector, your validation request and their response, and any proof that supports a defense, such as the date of your last payment. Organize everything by date and bring a one-page timeline of the account. Dress neatly, address the judge as Your Honor, and stick to the facts and the dates.

Is this legal advice?

No. This guide is general information about how debt collection lawsuits work. Deadlines, procedures, defenses, and exemptions vary by state and by county. For anything that could affect your case, check your state court website, ask the court clerk what form to use, or talk to a legal aid organization or a consumer attorney in your state before you file anything.
