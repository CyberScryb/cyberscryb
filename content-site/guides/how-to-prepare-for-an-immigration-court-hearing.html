How to Prepare for an Immigration Court Hearing

You have a hearing date in immigration court, and the stakes could not be higher. What happens at a master calendar hearing versus an individual hearing, the evidence that matters, and how to walk in prepared.

By CyberScryb 📅 Updated November 13, 2026 ⏱ 11 min read
⚡ Key Takeaways (TL;DR)

An immigration court hearing is a proceeding before an immigration judge, held when the government says a person is removable from the United States. The first hearings are short master calendar hearings where the judge confirms your information and asks whether you will seek relief. The individual hearing is the long one where you testify and the judge decides. Evidence and a clear, dated, consistent story decide most cases, so gather your documents early, write your timeline, and prepare your testimony with an attorney if you can. Never miss a hearing: a judge can order removal in your absence. This guide is general information, not legal advice.

What an immigration court hearing is

Immigration court is part of the Executive Office for Immigration Review, a part of the U.S. Department of Justice. It is not a criminal court. The proceedings are civil, but the outcome can be removal from the United States, so the stakes are as high as any court case most people will ever face.

The case starts when the government files a Notice to Appear, often called an NTA, with the court. The NTA says who you are, why the government believes you are removable, and when and where your first hearing is. Three people sit at the center of the case: the immigration judge, who runs the hearing and decides it; the government attorney, who represents the Department of Homeland Security and argues for removal; and you, the person in proceedings, who can present a defense and ask for relief, such as asylum, cancellation of removal, adjustment of status, or another form of protection. You may have a lawyer, and the judge will ask whether you do. There is no jury. The judge hears testimony, reviews evidence, and issues a decision.

A hearing is not a single event. Most cases run through several hearings over months or years, and each one has a different job. Understanding which hearing you are walking into is the first piece of preparation, because the wrong preparation for the wrong hearing wastes the time you need most.

Master calendar hearing vs individual hearing

There are two kinds of hearings in immigration court, and they could hardly be more different.

  • Master calendar hearing. A short hearing, often only a few minutes per person, with many cases scheduled in the same session. The judge confirms your name and address, checks that you received the Notice to Appear, reads the charges against you, and asks whether you admit or deny them. The judge also asks whether you have a lawyer, whether you need an interpreter, and whether you will apply for relief from removal. If you say you will apply for relief, the judge sets a deadline for filing the application and schedules the next hearing. Most people have several master calendar hearings before their case reaches an individual hearing. Do not bring all your evidence to a master calendar hearing unless the judge asks for it. Do bring your hearing notice, your identification, and your current address.
  • Individual hearing. Sometimes called a merits hearing, this is the long hearing where your case is actually decided. You testify under oath, your witnesses testify, your evidence is presented, and the government attorney can question you and your witnesses and present its own evidence. The judge may ask questions at any point. An individual hearing can run for hours, and in complex cases it can stretch across more than one day. This is the hearing the whole preparation in this guide is aimed at.

Your hearing notice says which kind of hearing you have. If it is not clear, ask the court clerk or your attorney before the hearing date. Showing up ready for the wrong kind of hearing is one of the most common avoidable mistakes.

The case timeline that leads to a hearing

Knowing where you are in the timeline matters, because each step has its own deadline and its own preparation.

  1. The Notice to Appear. The government files the NTA with the court and serves a copy on you. Read it the day it arrives. It lists the charges against you, the factual allegations behind them, and the date, time, and location of your first hearing. Keep it with your court papers. If anything in it is wrong, such as your name, your date of entry, or the charges, write down what is wrong and tell your attorney or the judge.
  2. The first master calendar hearing. The judge confirms your identity and address, reads the charges, and asks whether you admit or deny them and whether you will seek relief. If you do not have a lawyer yet, the judge may give you time to find one and set another master calendar hearing. Never admit to a charge you do not understand. If you are unsure, say so, and ask the judge or your attorney to explain it.
  3. Filing for relief. If you are seeking asylum, cancellation of removal, adjustment of status, or another form of relief, you file an application with the court by the deadline the judge sets. The application is the backbone of your case. File it on time and keep a copy of everything you file, plus proof that you filed it.
  4. More master calendar hearings. The judge checks on the progress of your application, sets deadlines for evidence, and schedules the individual hearing. Use these hearings to ask questions about deadlines and procedure, and to tell the court if your address or phone number has changed.
  5. The individual hearing. You testify, your witnesses testify, evidence is presented, and the judge decides. The judge may decide the same day or issue a written decision later.
  6. After the decision. If the judge grants relief, the case ends in your favor, though the government may appeal. If the judge orders removal, you generally have a short window to appeal to the Board of Immigration Appeals, and the appeal deadline is printed in the decision. Mark it the day the decision arrives, and talk to an attorney immediately, because an appeal is usually decided on the record from the hearing rather than a new hearing, which is why the record you build at the hearing matters so much.

The deadlines that matter

Immigration deadlines are strict, and missing one can end a case that would otherwise have won. The exact deadlines depend on your case and on what the judge orders, so confirm every date against your own court notices and with your attorney. These are the ones people most often miss.

  • Filing for relief. The judge sets a deadline for filing your application for relief, such as asylum or cancellation of removal. Miss it and the judge can treat the application as abandoned. Some forms of relief also have their own filing windows set by law. Ask your attorney what applies to your application and mark the judge's deadline the day it is set.
  • Keeping your address current. You must keep the court informed of your address at all times, and you must file a change of address with the court promptly when you move. If the court sends a hearing notice to an old address, the hearing still counts, and you can be ordered removed in your absence for missing a hearing you never knew about. File the change of address the day you move, and keep proof that you filed it.
  • Evidence deadlines. The judge usually sets a deadline for submitting evidence before the individual hearing. Evidence filed late can be excluded, which means the judge may never see it. Gather your documents early and file them by the judge's deadline, not the day before the hearing.
  • Appealing the decision. If the judge orders removal, there is a short window to appeal, and the deadline is printed in the decision. The appeal usually goes to the Board of Immigration Appeals and is decided on the hearing record, not a new hearing. If you think you may appeal, talk to an attorney the day the decision arrives.
  • Biometrics and appointments. The court or the government may ask you to attend a biometrics appointment or a check-in with immigration authorities. Missing one can delay your case or be used against you. Keep every appointment notice with your court papers and attend every appointment.

If you have already missed a deadline, do not assume the case is over. Tell your attorney immediately and ask whether the court can reopen or reconsider the case, and do it right away, because that request has its own deadline.

The evidence and documentation checklist

Build your file before the individual hearing, organized by date, so the judge can follow it without your narration. Bring three copies of each document: one for the judge, one for the government attorney, and one for yourself. Leave originals at home in a safe place and bring copies, unless the judge specifically asks for an original. If a document is not in English, it needs a certified English translation, and the translator must sign a certificate saying the translation is complete and accurate.

  • Your court papers. The Notice to Appear, every hearing notice, and every order the judge has issued. These prove where your case stands and what has been decided so far.
  • Identity and immigration documents. Your passport, any national identity card, birth certificate, prior visas, work authorization, and any prior immigration decisions. If you do not have a passport, bring whatever identity documents you have, and tell your attorney what is missing.
  • Proof of your time in the United States. Rent receipts or a lease, utility bills, pay stubs, tax returns, school records for you or your children, medical records, and bank statements, organized by year. For relief that depends on how long you have lived here, this is the evidence that proves it.
  • Proof of your ties here. Marriage certificate, your children's birth certificates, school enrollment records, letters from employers, and evidence of community ties such as church or volunteer involvement. The judge weighs your ties to the United States against the case for removal.
  • Evidence for your relief claim. What this looks like depends on the relief you are seeking. For asylum, that often means a detailed personal statement, country conditions reports about the place you fear returning to, police reports, medical records of injuries, and letters from people who know what happened to you. For other forms of relief, it may mean proof of hardship to a qualifying family member or proof of good moral character. Ask your attorney exactly what your application needs.
  • Witness letters and statements. Written, signed, dated statements from people who know your story, with their full names, addresses, and phone numbers. A witness who will testify in person is stronger than a letter, but a detailed letter is far better than no witness at all. Ask your witnesses early, while their memory is fresh, and tell them what the hearing is for so their letters speak to the right facts.
  • Medical and mental health records. Records of treatment for injuries or trauma related to your claim, and any evaluation by a doctor or mental health professional. If a professional has evaluated the effect of your experiences on you, that evaluation can be powerful evidence. Keep going to your appointments, because the records matter more than anything you say about your health in the room.
  • Your timeline. A one-page list of dates: when you entered the United States, where you have lived, when key events happened, when you filed applications, and when each hearing took place. Dates are the backbone of an immigration case, and a clear timeline is easier for a judge to follow than a stack of loose papers. Check every date against your documents before the hearing.
  • Your personal statement. A written account of your story, in date order, that matches your application and your testimony exactly. Write it with your attorney. If your statement, your application, and your testimony disagree on a date or a detail, the government attorney will find the difference and use it.

Preparing your testimony

You will testify at the individual hearing, under oath, about your history, why you came to the United States, and why you should be allowed to stay. The best preparation is to write your story down before the hearing, in date order, with the document behind each fact. If a sentence has no date and no document behind it, cut it or find the document. Practice telling the story with your attorney, out loud, more than once.

  1. Start with who you are. Your full name, where you are from, your family, and when and how you came to the United States. Keep it to what happened, in order, with dates.
  2. Then what happened before you left. The events that led you to leave, in date order, with the people involved and the places named. If there was harm or threats, say what happened, when, and who did it, without exaggeration. Specific facts are credible. General statements like "it was dangerous" are not, and they invite the cross-examination that hurts the most.
  3. Then your life in the United States. Where you have lived, worked, and studied, your family here, and what your life looks like now. This is where your proof of time and ties comes in.
  4. Then why you fear returning. What you believe would happen if you were sent back, and why you believe it, tied to the specific facts of your case and to the country conditions evidence in your file. Be honest about what you know and what you do not know. Guessing at an answer is worse than saying you do not know.
  5. End with what you are asking for. The relief you are seeking, in one sentence.

Practice answering the hard questions before the hearing, because the government attorney will ask them:

  • Inconsistencies. If your application, your personal statement, and your testimony disagree on a date, a place, or a detail, the government attorney will ask about it. The answer is preparation: check every date against your documents before the hearing, and if you genuinely do not remember a detail, say so instead of guessing.
  • Why you did not seek help. If you did not report harm to the police in your home country, or did not apply for protection sooner, have the honest reason ready: the police were part of the problem, you feared retaliation, you did not know you could apply. A reason is defensible. No reason looks like the harm was not real.
  • Your time and travel. The government attorney may ask about trips you took, places you lived, and how you entered the United States. Have the facts ready, in date order, and do not guess. If a date is uncertain, say it is approximate and say why.
  • Your credibility. The judge is deciding whether to believe you, and credibility is often the whole case. Do not exaggerate. Do not hide facts your attorney knows about, because the government attorney may already know them too. Judges trust witnesses who concede the small points, and distrust witnesses who deny everything.

Answer the question asked, then add the context. Do not guess at dates, places, or names. If you do not remember, say so. If you do not understand the question, say so and ask for it to be repeated. This is especially important through an interpreter, where a misunderstood question can produce a wrong answer that looks like a lie.

What happens at the hearing

The courtroom is smaller and less formal than a criminal courtroom, but it is still a formal proceeding, and everything you say is under oath and recorded.

At a master calendar hearing, arrive early, at least 30 minutes before the hearing starts, and check in with the clerk. The judge calls cases one by one, and your turn may last only a few minutes. The judge confirms your name and address, reads the charges in the Notice to Appear, and asks whether you admit or deny them and whether you will seek relief. Answer clearly. If you need an interpreter, say so. If you do not have a lawyer yet, say so, and the judge may give you time to find one.

At an individual hearing, the hearing usually runs like this. The judge calls the case and states what is being decided. The government attorney presents its case first, then your side presents yours. You testify, your witnesses testify, and each side can question the other's witnesses. The judge may ask questions at any point, and may interrupt to clarify a date or a fact. Then each side gives a short closing statement. The judge may decide the same day or issue a written decision later.

A few things that help in the room:

  • Arrive early and dress neatly. Arrive at least 30 minutes early, turn your phone off, and dress as you would for a job interview. First impressions are real, and the judge is watching.
  • Address the judge as Your Honor. Stand when the judge enters, speak clearly, and wait for the judge to finish before you answer.
  • Answer the question asked. If the judge asks a yes-or-no question, answer it, then add the context. Rambling answers lose the judge's attention and your credibility.
  • Do not interrupt. When the government attorney is speaking, write down what you disagree with and raise it when it is your turn. Interrupting looks bad and the judge will stop you.
  • Admit what is true. If a date in your application is wrong, say so and correct it. If you do not remember, say so. Judges decide credibility fast, and the calm side usually wins it.
  • Bring the documents, not just the story. If you say you lived at an address for three years, have the lease or the utility bill in your hand. If you say you were harmed on a date, have the record. The hearing is long, but the judge will not wait while you search your phone.

The interpreter and the language of the hearing

The court provides an interpreter for the hearing, usually by phone or video, in the language listed on your file. The interpreter translates everything: the judge's questions, the government attorney's questions, and your answers. Nothing you say reaches the judge except through the interpreter, so the interpreter is part of your case.

  • Check the language early. Tell the court as soon as possible if the language on your file is wrong, or if you need a specific dialect. An interpreter in the wrong language or dialect can cause misunderstandings that hurt your case, and the time to fix it is before the hearing, not during it.
  • Speak in short pieces. Say one or two sentences, then stop and let the interpreter finish. Long answers get summarized instead of translated, and the summary may lose the detail that matters.
  • Speak to the judge, not to the interpreter. Look at the judge when you answer. The interpreter is a voice, not the person you are persuading.
  • Say so if the translation is wrong. If the interpreter says something you did not say, or you do not understand the question as translated, tell the judge right away. It is better to pause and fix a translation than to answer a question you did not understand.
  • Bring your own interpreter only if the court allows it. Some courts allow a family member or friend to help outside the hearing room, but the court's interpreter is the one who translates during the hearing. Ask your attorney what your court allows.

Hearing-day logistics

The practical details matter, because a missed hearing can mean removal in your absence.

  • Confirm the date, time, and court. Read your hearing notice the day it arrives and again the day before the hearing. Courts move cases between courtrooms, and hearing times change. If anything is unclear, call the court clerk or the automated case information line, or ask your attorney to confirm.
  • Plan to arrive early. Courts have security screening, and lines can be long. Arrive at least 30 minutes early, and allow extra time if you are traveling from far away. If you are delayed, call the court clerk as soon as you can.
  • Bring identification. Bring your hearing notice and a photo ID. If you do not have a photo ID, bring whatever identification you have and tell your attorney.
  • Arrange childcare. Children are generally not allowed in the courtroom, and a child who needs your attention will distract you from the hearing. Arrange childcare for the day, and bring only the people who need to be there.
  • Do not bring food, and leave weapons at home. Courts do not allow food in the courtroom, and weapons are prohibited in the building. Bring water and a snack for the wait outside the courtroom, and eat before you go in.
  • Know the building rules. Most immigration courts are inside federal buildings with security screening. Leave anything that could be taken for a weapon at home, and expect your bags to be searched.

Costs, fees, and what help actually costs

An immigration case can be expensive, but free and low-cost help exists, and the cost of losing a case you could have won is usually higher than the cost of getting help.

  • Filing fees: some applications have filing fees, and some do not. Ask your attorney or check the fee schedule for your specific application, and ask about fee waivers if you cannot afford the fee. Do not let a fee stop you from filing on time without asking first.
  • Translations: every document not in English needs a certified translation, and translators charge per page. Budget for this early, and ask your attorney whether a community organization can help with translations at low cost.
  • Immigration attorney: private immigration attorneys usually charge a flat fee for a case, and the fee varies by the type of relief and the complexity of the case. Ask for the fee in writing before you sign anything, and ask what it covers: the application, the hearing, the appeal. Some attorneys offer payment plans.
  • Free and low-cost help: legal aid organizations, law school immigration clinics, and nonprofit immigration programs help people who cannot afford a private attorney, often for free. The Executive Office for Immigration Review publishes a list of free legal service providers by state, and your court clerk can point you to it. Start early, because these programs have waiting lists.
  • Watch out for notarios and unlicensed help. In many communities, people who call themselves notarios offer immigration help for a fee. A notario is not a lawyer, and bad advice from an unlicensed preparer can ruin a case. Only an attorney or a representative accredited by the government should give you legal advice or file papers for you. If someone asks for a large fee up front and promises a result, walk away.
  • Your time: the preparation, the hearings, and the follow-up. The individual hearing alone can take a full day. It is time well spent next to a removal order that could have been avoided.

When to consult an attorney

You are allowed to represent yourself in immigration court, but immigration law is complex, the rules change, and the stakes are removal from the United States. Most people with a hearing date should talk to an immigration attorney, and the consultation is often free or low cost. Get a lawyer if any of these apply:

  • You have a hearing date and do not have a lawyer yet.
  • You are seeking asylum or another form of relief, because the application and the evidence requirements are detailed and unforgiving.
  • You have a criminal record, a prior removal order, or a prior immigration case, because any of these can change which relief you can ask for.
  • You entered the United States recently or have a prior order of removal.
  • You do not understand the charges in your Notice to Appear.
  • You have missed a deadline or missed a hearing, because the case may still be fixable if you act fast.
  • Someone has offered to help you for a fee and you are not sure they are a licensed attorney or an accredited representative.

If you cannot afford a lawyer, start with the free legal service provider list for your state, a law school immigration clinic, or a nonprofit immigration program. Bring your Notice to Appear, every hearing notice, and your timeline, so the lawyer can give you a real answer instead of a general one.

Mistakes that backfire

  • Missing a hearing. If you do not show up, the judge can order you removed in your absence. Keep your address current with the court, read every notice the day it arrives, and never assume a hearing was moved or canceled unless the court tells you in writing.
  • Missing a filing deadline. A late application for relief can be treated as abandoned. File by the judge's deadline, and keep proof that you filed.
  • Not updating your address. The court sends notices to the address on file. If you move and do not tell the court, the hearing still counts. File the change of address the day you move.
  • Admitting to a charge you do not understand. At a master calendar hearing, the judge asks whether you admit or deny the charges in the Notice to Appear. Never admit to a charge you do not understand. Say so, and ask the judge or your attorney to explain it.
  • Inconsistencies between your papers and your testimony. If your application, your personal statement, and your testimony disagree on a date or a detail, the government attorney will find it. Check every date against your documents before the hearing.
  • Exaggerating or guessing. One contradiction, one statement that does not match the records, and the judge doubts everything else you say. Be specific and be honest about what you remember and what you do not.
  • Using a notario or unlicensed preparer. Bad advice from someone who is not a lawyer can ruin a case that a lawyer could have won. Only an attorney or an accredited representative should give you legal advice or file papers for you.
  • Bringing a story instead of a file. "I cannot go back" is not evidence. The country conditions report, the police report, the medical record, and the witness letter are. If it is not written down, dated, and in your file, the judge may never hear it.
  • Talking about the case on social media. Assume the government attorney can see it. Do not post about your case, your hearing, or your travel, and do not post anything that contradicts what you told the court.

FAQ

What is an immigration court hearing?

An immigration court hearing is a proceeding before an immigration judge in the Executive Office for Immigration Review, held when the government says a person is removable from the United States. The judge hears the government's case and any defense or relief the person asks for, such as asylum or cancellation of removal, and issues a decision. This guide is general information, not legal advice.

What is the difference between a master calendar hearing and an individual hearing?

A master calendar hearing is a short, early hearing where the judge confirms your identity and address, reads the charges in your Notice to Appear, and asks whether you admit or deny them and whether you will seek relief. An individual hearing, sometimes called a merits hearing, is the longer hearing where you and your witnesses testify, evidence is presented, and the judge decides the case. Most people have several master calendar hearings before one individual hearing.

Do I need a lawyer for an immigration court hearing?

You are allowed to represent yourself, but immigration law is complex and the stakes are high, so a lawyer is worth serious thought. Free and low-cost legal help exists in many areas through legal aid organizations, law school clinics, and nonprofit immigration programs. If you cannot afford a lawyer, start with those programs as early as possible, because a lawyer needs time to prepare your case. Bring your Notice to Appear and every court paper to your first meeting.

What documents should I bring to an immigration court hearing?

Bring your Notice to Appear, every hearing notice, your passport and any identity documents, your immigration paperwork, evidence that supports the relief you are seeking, such as country conditions reports, medical records, police reports, or letters from witnesses, and proof of your time in the United States and your ties here. Bring three copies of each document: one for the judge, one for the government attorney, and one for yourself. Keep originals safe and bring copies.

Will there be an interpreter at my immigration court hearing?

The court provides an interpreter for the hearing, usually by phone or video, in the language listed on your file. Tell the court as early as possible if the listed language is wrong or if you need a specific dialect, because an interpreter in the wrong language or dialect can cause misunderstandings that hurt your case. Answer in your own language and wait for the interpreter to finish before continuing.

What happens at an individual immigration hearing?

At an individual hearing you testify under oath about your history, why you came to the United States, and why you should be allowed to stay. Your witnesses testify, your lawyer or you present evidence, and the government attorney can question you and your witnesses and present its own evidence. The judge may ask questions at any point. The judge may decide the same day or issue a written decision later. Dress neatly, answer the question asked, and stick to the facts and the dates.

What happens if I miss my immigration court hearing?

If you do not show up for a hearing, the judge can order you removed in your absence. That is one of the most serious consequences in the system, and it can be very hard to undo. Keep your address current with the court at all times, read every notice the day it arrives, and if an emergency keeps you from a hearing, contact the court and an immigration attorney immediately. Do not assume a hearing was moved or canceled unless the court tells you in writing.

Is this legal advice?

No. This guide is general information about how immigration court hearings work. Immigration law, deadlines, and procedures change, and the right steps depend on your individual case. For anything that could affect your case, talk to an immigration attorney or a recognized legal aid organization before your next hearing date.
