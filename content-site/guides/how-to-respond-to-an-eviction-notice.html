How to Respond to an Eviction Notice: The First 48 Hours

An eviction notice is a demand, not a court order. What to do in the first 48 hours: read the notice type, calendar the deadline, respond in writing, document everything, and get help before the deadline, not on it.

By CyberScryb 📅 Updated October 21, 2026 ⏱ 9 min read
⚡ Key Takeaways (TL;DR)

A notice is not an eviction. Do not move out before you understand the deadline. Read the notice type (pay or quit, cure or quit, no-cause), calendar the stated deadline, respond in writing, fix what you can fix, keep paying rent if you stay, and contact legal aid or a tenants' rights org immediately. This is general information, not legal advice.

Hour 0: Read the Notice Like a Checklist

The notice tells you three things. Find all three before you do anything else:

  • The type. A pay-or-quit (pay rent or quit) notice gives a short window to pay what is owed. A cure-or-quit (comply or vacate) notice gives a window to fix a lease violation. A no-cause or termination notice ends the tenancy with a longer window, usually 30 to 90 days. The words on the notice name the type.
  • The deadline. The notice states how many days you have. Pay-or-quit is often 3 to 5 days, cure-or-quit 10 to 30, no-cause 30 to 90. The exact number is set by your state and written on the notice. Calendar it the hour you read it.
  • The reason. What does the landlord claim? Late rent, a lease violation, or no reason at all. Compare the claim against your own records before you react.

Only a court can evict you. A notice is the landlord's demand that starts the process. Do not move out before you understand the deadline: leaving early can surrender defenses you did not know you had, and it does not erase rent you owe.

Hours 1 to 24: Fix What You Can Fix, in Writing

If it is a pay-or-quit notice and you can pay the full amount, pay it within the window and keep the receipt. A satisfied pay-or-quit generally ends that notice. Pay by a traceable method: cashier's check, money order, or bank transfer with a confirmation. No cash without a signed receipt.

If it is a cure-or-quit notice and the violation is fixable (unauthorized pet, noise, an unapproved occupant who can leave), fix it fast and document the fix in writing: photos, dates, and a short written response to the landlord stating what was cured and when. Send that response in writing; email is fine.

If the claim is wrong, write that down too: the rent was paid (receipt attached), the violation did not happen (dates, witnesses), or the notice is retaliation for a repair request you made (dates of your requests). Your written response creates the record the notice started.

Hours 24 to 48: Get Help Before the Deadline

Do not wait until the last day. Contact a local legal aid office or tenants' rights organization in the first 48 hours. They handle eviction notices daily, many are free, and they need time to review your notice before any court date. Bring the notice, your lease, your payment records, and any repair requests you have made.

While It Is Pending: The Rules That Protect You

  • Keep paying rent if you stay. Unpaid rent is the cleanest eviction case a landlord can bring. Do not hand it to them.
  • Do not let the landlord self-evict you. Changing the locks, shutting off utilities, or removing your belongings without a court order is illegal in most states. If it happens, document it immediately and contact a tenants' rights organization. Do not break back in yourself.
  • Show up to everything. If the landlord files in court, appear at every hearing with your case file. Missing a court date is the fastest way to lose by default.
  • Negotiate from the record. Many evictions settle: a payment plan, a move-out date you choose, a neutral reference. Landlords settle with tenants who have a documented record, not with tenants who ignored the notice.

What Not to Do

  • Do not ignore the notice. Ignoring it does not make it expire; it turns a notice into a court filing.
  • Do not move out in a panic. You may owe the remaining rent anyway, and you lose the chance to defend yourself.
  • Do not argue by phone. Every substantive statement goes in writing. Calls get logged, not relied on.
  • Do not withhold rent as a tactic. Unless a legal aid attorney tells you otherwise in writing, withholding rent during an eviction case is self-harm.

After the Notice: Build the Record Either Way

Whether the notice ends with a payment, a cure, a settlement, or a court date, the discipline is the same: written responses, logged exchanges, kept receipts, and one file that holds it all. An eviction notice that gets answered on time with a paper trail often ends as a payment plan or a delayed move-out you controlled, not a judgment.

FAQ

Do I have to move out when I get an eviction notice?

Usually not. An eviction notice is a landlord's demand, not a court order. Only a judge can order you out. Read the notice to find what it asks for and by when, and do not move out before you understand the deadline; leaving early can surrender defenses you did not know you had.

What is a pay or quit notice?

A pay-or-quit (or pay rent or quit) notice tells the tenant to pay the rent owed within a short window, usually a few days, or vacate. If you pay the full amount within the window, the notice is generally satisfied. Keep the receipt. The exact window is set by your state, so check the number on the notice itself.

What is a cure or quit notice?

A cure-or-quit (comply or vacate) notice tells the tenant to fix a lease violation, such as an unauthorized pet or excessive noise, within the stated window or leave. If you can fix the violation quickly and document it in writing, do it and keep the proof.

How long do I have after receiving an eviction notice?

It depends on the notice type and your state: pay-or-quit notices often run 3 to 5 days, cure-or-quit notices 10 to 30 days, and no-cause termination notices 30 to 90 days. The notice itself states the deadline; read it first, calendar it, and act before it, not on it.

Can my landlord change the locks or shut off utilities?

In most places, no: lockouts, utility shutoffs, and removing your belongings without a court order are illegal self-help evictions in most states. If it happens, document it immediately and contact a tenants' rights organization or attorney. Do not break back in yourself.

Is this legal advice?

No. Eviction rules, notice requirements, and tenant defenses vary by state and city. This guide describes a general response method. Talk to a local tenants' rights organization, legal aid office, or attorney for advice about your situation, especially before any court deadline.
