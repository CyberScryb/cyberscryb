How Digital SAT Scoring Works

The digital SAT adapts to you mid-test: Module 1 routes you to a harder or easier Module 2, and your raw score is converted to a 200-800 scale per section. Here is how it all works, what a good score looks like, and how to plan your way up.

By CyberScryb 📅 Updated October 6, 2026 ⏱ 7 min read
⚡ Key Takeaways (TL;DR)

The digital SAT is out of 1600: two sections, Reading & Writing and Math, each scored 200-800. Each section has two modules; Module 1 decides whether your Module 2 is harder or easier, and the harder path reaches higher scaled scores. There is no guessing penalty. The Direct Answer

The digital SAT (the only SAT format since 2024) scores you on a 400-1600 scale: Reading & Writing, 200-800, plus Math, 200-800. Your raw score in each section is simply the number of questions you answered correctly. There is no penalty for wrong answers, and blank answers count the same as wrong ones, so guessing always beats skipping.

Raw scores are then converted to scaled scores through a process called equating, which adjusts for the difficulty of the specific questions you saw. Equating is why a raw score on one test date does not always convert to the same scaled score as the same raw score on another date: the point is fairness across test forms, so a 700 means the same ability level no matter when you tested.

Adaptive Modules: How Module 1 Routes You

Each section has two modules: Reading & Writing has 27 questions per module (54 total, 64 minutes), and Math has 22 per module (44 total, 70 minutes). Everyone gets the same first module, with a mix of easy, medium, and hard questions. How you do on Module 1 determines which version of Module 2 you see:

Do well on Module 1, get the harder Module 2. This is the path you want. The harder module has a higher scoring ceiling: a perfect raw score here can reach a full 800. You also need fewer correct answers to reach a high scaled score, because harder questions are worth more under equating.

Struggle on Module 1, get the easier Module 2. The easier module caps your score: roughly 630 in Reading & Writing and 590 in Math at the very top. No matter how many you get right on the easier path, you cannot reach elite scores. Two students with identical raw correct counts can get wildly different scaled scores because of this routing difference.

The adaptation is per-section, not per-test: you can get the harder Module 2 in Math and the easier one in Reading & Writing, or vice versa. Each section's routing stands on its own.

The 200-800 Scale, Explained

Each section converts separately to a 200-800 scale in 10-point increments, and the two section scores add up to the 400-1600 total. The floors exist because the scale starts at 200 per section, not zero: even a raw score of zero is not a zero scaled score.

What a Good Score Looks Like for Colleges

Colleges publish their admitted students' 25th and 75th percentile SAT ranges, and that is the only "good score" definition that matters. Scoring at or above a school's 75th percentile makes your application stronger on the testing front; scoring below the 25th percentile means your score is a weakness there and you should either retake or apply test-optional if the school allows it.

Rough benchmarks, because ranges vary by school: many open-admission and regional state schools admit students across the 1000-1200 range. Flagship state universities typically cluster in the 1200-1400 range. Highly selective colleges usually show 1400-1500+ ranges. Merit scholarships often have explicit score cutoffs, sometimes as low as 1200-1300, which can make raising your score a straight financial gain.

Score your practice tests with the SAT score calculator and line the estimate up against each school's published range. That comparison tells you exactly how far you need to climb and where a retake is worth it.

Superscoring: Your Best Sections Combined

Superscoring means a college takes your highest section scores across multiple test dates and combines them into one total. Score 700 Reading & Writing and 600 Math in March, then 650 and 700 in May, and your superscore is 1400 (700 + 700), even though neither single sitting reached it.

Many colleges superscore the SAT, and the digital format was designed with superscoring in mind. This changes your strategy: a strong section is never wasted, and a lopsided score is fine if you plan to retake the other section. Always check each college's stated policy, since some do not superscore.

How Many Questions Can You Miss for Your Target?

Working backward from a target is the best study plan. Rough estimates on the harder Module 2 path, modeled on College Board practice-test conversions:

1400 total: ~40 of 54 in Reading & Writing (miss ~14) and ~29-31 of 44 in Math (miss ~13-15)

1300 total: ~36 of 54 in Reading & Writing and ~27 of 44 in Math

1200 total: ~28 of 54 in Reading & Writing and ~19 of 44 in Math

On the easier Module 2 path, high targets are simply out of reach no matter how many you get right. The single highest-leverage goal in your prep is to lock in the harder Module 2 by getting comfortable on Module 1 questions.

How to Raise Your Score

Secure the harder Module 2. Because the easier path caps your score, performing well on Module 1 is the single biggest structural lever. Drill medium-difficulty Module 1 questions until they are automatic.

Never leave a question blank. No guessing penalty means every blank is a free miss. Guess on everything; an educated guess is better than a random one.

Study by question type, not by section. The digital SAT reuses tight question types: algebra, functions, grammar rules, evidence-based reading. Find your worst types in practice tests, drill those specifically, and retest. Targeted drills beat endless full tests.

Time the modules, not just the total. Module-level timing is the real constraint: about 1 minute 11 seconds per Reading & Writing question, about 1 minute 35 seconds per Math question. Practice skipping and returning; burning three minutes on one hard question costs you three easy ones later.

Use the calculator on all Math questions. The built-in Desmos graphing calculator is available for the entire Math section. Practice with it before test day; students who know its shortcuts solve system-of-equations and function questions in seconds.

Plan your retakes around superscoring. If one section is strong and the other is dragging, focus the retake prep on the weak section and let the superscore carry the strong one. Verify each target college superscores first.

Frequently Asked Questions

How is the digital SAT scored?

The digital SAT is section-adaptive. Each section (Reading & Writing, Math) has two modules. Everyone gets the same first module; your performance on it routes you to a harder or easier second module. Your raw score is the number of questions answered correctly, with no penalty for wrong answers. The College Board converts that raw score to a 200-800 scaled score per section using equating, which adjusts for the difficulty of the questions you saw.

What is a good SAT score?

The national average is around 1050-1060 out of 1600. A 1200 is roughly the 80th percentile, 1350 roughly the 90th, and 1450+ roughly the 96th. What counts as good depends on the school: many state schools admit students in the 1000-1200 range, while highly selective colleges typically look for 1400-1500+. Compare your score to the published ranges of your specific colleges.

How many questions can I miss and still get a 1400?

As a rough estimate on the harder Module 2 path: about 40 of 54 correct in Reading & Writing (missing about 14 questions) and about 29-31 of 44 correct in Math (missing about 13-15 questions), for around 700 per section. On the easier Module 2 path, 1400 is out of reach entirely. These are estimates, not official numbers.

Is the SAT out of 1600 or 2400?

The SAT is out of 1600: two sections, Reading & Writing and Math, each scored 200-800. The 2400 scale with a separate Writing section was retired in 2016. Old 2400-scale scores cannot be directly compared to current scores.

What is superscoring?

Superscoring means a college takes your highest section scores across multiple test dates and combines them. If you scored 700 Reading & Writing and 600 Math in March, then 650 and 700 in May, your superscore is 1400 (700 + 700). Many colleges superscore the SAT, so a strong single section is never wasted.
