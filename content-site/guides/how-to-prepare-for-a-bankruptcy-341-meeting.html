How to Prepare for a Bankruptcy 341 Meeting

You filed for bankruptcy and the court set your meeting of creditors. What the trustee asks, what to bring, the deadlines that matter, and what happens after.

What a 341 meeting is

The 341 meeting is officially called the meeting of creditors. It takes its name from Section 341 of the Bankruptcy Code, which requires a meeting in every bankruptcy case. It is the one appearance most people make in their entire case, and it is far less formal than the word meeting suggests.

A bankruptcy trustee runs the meeting, not a judge. There is no courtroom, no witness stand, and no one arguing against you. The trustee is a person appointed to administer your case: to verify that the information in your petition is accurate and complete, and to find out whether you have any nonexempt assets that could be used to pay your creditors. The trustee is not your lawyer, and the trustee is not your creditor either. The trustee is checking the paperwork.

The trustee puts you under oath at the start, and the meeting is recorded. Everything you say is sworn testimony. The trustee then works through a standard set of questions about your petition, your debts, your property, and your income. In most consumer cases the whole thing lasts 5 to 15 minutes. Creditors get notice and have the right to attend and ask questions, but in most consumer cases they do not show up.

Many districts now hold 341 meetings by video, often over Zoom, rather than in person. Your meeting notice says the format. A video meeting is not a lesser meeting: you are still under oath, it is still recorded, and the trustee asks the same questions.

The 341 meeting is not a hearing where the trustee decides whether you deserve a discharge. The trustee verifies facts. The discharge decision comes later, after the objection window closes, and it comes from the court, not from the trustee.

Chapter 7 vs Chapter 13 at the 341 meeting

The meeting looks similar in both chapters, but what the trustee is looking for is different, because the two chapters do different things.

  • Chapter 7. Chapter 7 wipes out most unsecured debts, like credit cards and medical bills, in about 3 to 4 months. The trustee looks for nonexempt assets that could be sold to pay creditors. Most Chapter 7 cases are no-asset cases, which means everything the debtor owns is protected by exemptions and there is nothing for the trustee to sell. The trustee asks about property, recent transfers, lawsuits, and expected tax refunds, because those are the places hidden value turns up.
  • Chapter 13. Chapter 13 is a repayment plan that runs 3 to 5 years. You keep your property and pay what you can afford to creditors through the trustee. The trustee checks that your proposed plan is feasible, that your income and expenses are stated accurately, and that you are paying in everything the plan requires. The trustee also asks about your plan payments, your mortgage or car payments, and whether you are current on the payments you have to keep making outside the plan.

If you filed jointly with a spouse, both of you attend the meeting and both of you answer questions under oath. If only one spouse filed, only the filing spouse attends, though the trustee may still ask about household income and joint property.

The timeline that leads to a 341 meeting

The meeting date is set by the court, not by you, and it comes fast after filing. Here is how the schedule usually runs.

  1. Credit counseling before filing. You must complete a credit counseling course from an approved agency within 180 days before you file. The certificate goes in with your petition. If you file without it, the case can be dismissed.
  2. Filing day. You file the petition and schedules, pay the filing fee or ask to pay it in installments, and the automatic stay takes effect. The stay stops most collection calls, lawsuits, wage garnishments, and foreclosures while the case runs.
  3. The meeting notice. The court mails a notice with the date, time, and format of your 341 meeting, usually within a week or two of filing. Read it the day it arrives. It also lists the trustee assigned to your case and where to send documents.
  4. The 341 meeting. In a Chapter 7 case the meeting must be held 21 to 40 days after filing. In a Chapter 13 case the window is 21 to 50 days. In practice most meetings land about a month after filing.
  5. After the meeting. The trustee either concludes the meeting, asks for more documents, or continues it to a later date. In a Chapter 7 case, the 60-day objection window starts running from the first date set for the meeting, and the discharge usually follows once it closes. In a Chapter 13 case, the confirmation hearing for your plan comes after the meeting.

The deadlines that matter

The 341 meeting sets several deadlines, and some of them run before the meeting even happens. Confirm every date against your own court notices and with your attorney.

  • At least 14 days before the meeting. Give the trustee a copy of your government-issued photo ID and proof of your Social Security number. Acceptable proof of the number includes the original Social Security card, a W-2, or a letter from the Social Security Administration. If the trustee does not have these in time, the meeting can be continued.
  • At least 7 days before the meeting. Give the trustee a copy of your most recent federal income tax return, or a transcript of it. This is required by federal law. If you did not file a return, tell your attorney right away, because the trustee will ask about it.
  • Pay stubs covering the 60 days before filing. You must have filed proof of earnings, usually pay stubs, for the 60 days before your petition date. Your attorney files these with the court, and the trustee compares them against the income on your schedules.
  • 30 days after the first meeting date. Deadline for the trustee or a creditor to object to the exemptions you claimed. If no one objects in time, your exemptions stand.
  • 60 days after the first meeting date. Three things are due by this date in a Chapter 7 case: the deadline for creditors or the trustee to object to your discharge, the deadline to file your financial management course certificate, and the deadline to file any reaffirmation agreement for a debt you want to keep, like a car loan. The 60 days run from the first date set for the meeting, not from the date the meeting actually concluded.
  • Financial management course. After filing, you must complete a debtor education course, also called a financial management course, from an approved provider and file the certificate with the court before the 60-day deadline. The court will not grant a discharge without it.

What to bring to the 341 meeting

Most documents go to the trustee before the meeting, not at it. What you bring on the day is mostly identification and your own copies, so you can answer questions from the paper instead of from memory.

  • Your meeting notice. It has the date, time, format, trustee name, and case number. Bring it, or have it open on your phone for a video meeting.
  • Government-issued photo ID. A driver's license, passport, or state ID. The trustee verifies your identity at the start of the meeting. If your ID is expired, tell your attorney before the meeting, because an expired ID can be a problem.
  • Proof of your Social Security number. The original Social Security card, a W-2, or a Social Security Administration letter. The trustee needs to match the number on your petition to you.
  • Your filed petition and schedules. Bring your own copy, or have it open if the meeting is by video. When the trustee asks about a number on your schedules, you want to read it from the page, not guess.
  • Recent bank statements. Statements for every account you held on the filing date, since the trustee may ask about balances. Have them ready even if the trustee did not ask for them in advance.
  • Pay stubs and proof of income. The stubs covering the 60 days before filing, plus your most recent stub, in case the trustee asks about a change since filing.
  • Your most recent tax return. Your copy of the return you already sent the trustee, in case questions come up about it.
  • Mortgage and car loan statements. If you have a house or a car, the current statements show the balance, the payment, and whether you are current.
  • Any documents the trustee specifically requested. Trustees often ask for extra records before the meeting, such as proof of insurance on a car or house, divorce papers, or business records. If the trustee asked for it, bring it. A missing document is the most common reason a meeting gets continued.

Do not bring your children to the meeting. They are not allowed in the meeting room, and a child who needs your attention will distract you from sworn testimony. Arrange childcare for the day.

Questions the trustee asks

The trustee's questions follow a standard pattern. They are not trying to trick you. They are confirming that your filing is accurate and that you reviewed what you submitted. If you read your petition before the meeting, none of these should catch you off guard. Here is what most filers are asked, grouped the way the trustee asks them.

Identity and oath:

  • Please state your name for the record.
  • Please show your photo identification.
  • Do you swear that the testimony you are about to give is the truth, the whole truth, and nothing but the truth?

Petition accuracy:

  • Did you sign the petition, schedules, and statements?
  • Did you read them before you signed them?
  • Is all of the information in your petition true and correct to the best of your knowledge?
  • Have you listed all of your assets and all of your creditors?
  • Have you filed all of your tax returns?
  • Did you review the bankruptcy information sheet and the statement of your rights?

Assets and financial history:

  • Have you transferred or sold any property in the past two years?
  • Does anyone owe you money?
  • Are you involved in any lawsuits, or do you have a claim against anyone?
  • Do you expect to receive an inheritance, a tax refund, or an insurance settlement?
  • Have you repaid any family member or friend in the months before filing?
  • Do you own any real estate, vehicles, or other property not listed on your schedules?

Income, expenses, and debts:

  • Is the income listed on your schedules still accurate?
  • Has your income or your expenses changed since you filed?
  • Do you have any bank accounts, retirement accounts, or life insurance policies?
  • Are you current on your mortgage, your car payment, and your rent?
  • Have you used a credit card or taken a cash advance since deciding to file?

Chapter 13 only:

  • Do you understand your proposed repayment plan?
  • Can you afford the monthly plan payment?
  • Are you current on your plan payments?
  • Do you understand that you need the trustee's permission before taking on new debt while the plan runs?

The trustee may ask follow-up questions about anything that looks unusual in your petition: a large deposit, a missing account, a debt to a relative, a business you owned, or a piece of property with no value listed. If a question is about a number, read the number from your schedules. If you do not know the answer, say so. Guessing under oath is how small problems become big ones.

How to answer the trustee

The meeting is short because the answers are supposed to be short. The trustee asks a question, you answer it, the trustee moves on. The people who get into trouble are the ones who argue, explain, or volunteer more than they were asked.

  • Answer the question asked, then stop. If the trustee asks a yes or no question, answer yes or no, then add the context only if the trustee asks for it. Long answers invite follow-up questions.
  • Tell the truth, even when it hurts. The trustee compares your answers against your schedules, your bank statements, and your tax return. An inconsistency the trustee finds is far worse than a fact you admit yourself.
  • Do not guess. If you do not remember a date, an amount, or a name, say so. "I don't remember" is an honest answer. A guessed number that turns out wrong looks like a lie.
  • Do not volunteer information. Answer what was asked. Do not offer extra facts, side stories, or explanations the trustee did not request. Volunteered details create new lines of questioning.
  • Listen to the whole question. On a video meeting especially, wait for the trustee to finish before you answer. A misunderstood question produces a wrong answer that looks like a contradiction.
  • Let your attorney speak when needed. If you have an attorney, the attorney is in the meeting with you. If a question is unclear or calls for a legal conclusion, your attorney can clarify it or object. Do not answer over your attorney.
  • Stay calm and polite. The trustee is doing a job, and the meeting is routine for them. A calm, direct answer builds credibility. Arguing with the trustee does the opposite.

What happens at the meeting, step by step

The meeting follows the same order in almost every case. Knowing the order takes the fear out of it.

  1. Check-in. For an in-person meeting, you arrive, check in with the trustee's staff, and wait your turn. Several cases are set at the same time, so you may wait while other debtors go first. For a video meeting, you join the link at your scheduled time and wait in the virtual waiting room until the trustee admits you.
  2. Identity check. The trustee asks for your photo ID and proof of your Social Security number, and confirms they match the petition.
  3. The oath. The trustee swears you in. From this point on, you are testifying under oath and the meeting is recorded.
  4. The standard questions. The trustee works through the questions listed above: the petition, the assets, the transfers, the income. Your attorney is present and can clarify questions.
  5. Creditor questions, if any. If a creditor attended, the trustee gives them a chance to ask questions. In most cases no creditor is there, and this step takes no time at all.
  6. The conclusion. The trustee either concludes the meeting, which means the trustee has what is needed for now, or continues it to a later date if documents are missing or questions remain. A concluded meeting does not mean the case is over. It means the trustee's examination is done unless something new comes up.

A real example: a filer in a routine Chapter 7 case joins the video meeting, shows a driver's license and a Social Security card, swears the oath, answers yes to the petition questions, confirms no property was transferred and no one owes her money, and the trustee concludes the meeting in seven minutes. No creditor attended. The discharge arrived about two months later. That is the normal case, and it is the most common outcome.

If a creditor shows up

Creditors get notice of the meeting and have the right to attend and ask you questions, but in most consumer cases they do not. A creditor usually appears only when the debt involves something specific: a recent large charge on a credit card, a secured debt like a car loan, a debt the creditor believes was obtained by fraud, or a debt the creditor thinks should not be discharged.

If a creditor does attend, the trustee still runs the meeting. The creditor asks questions, you answer them under oath, and your attorney can object if a question goes outside the scope of the case. A creditor showing up does not mean your discharge is in danger. It means the creditor wants information, usually about one debt. Do not argue with the creditor. Answer the question, then stop, the same way you answer the trustee.

Meeting-day logistics

  • Confirm the date, time, and format. Read your meeting notice the day it arrives and again the day before. Times and formats change. If anything is unclear, call your attorney or the trustee's office and confirm.
  • Arrive early. For an in-person meeting, arrive at least 30 minutes early. Courthouses and trustee offices have security screening. For a video meeting, join a few minutes early and test your camera, microphone, and connection the day before with the same device you will use.
  • Pick a quiet room for a video meeting. A room with a door that closes, good light on your face, and nothing distracting behind you. Silence every other device and close every other app. Keep the trustee's phone number and your attorney's number on paper next to you in case the video drops.
  • Dress neatly. You do not need a suit, but dress as you would for a job interview. First impressions are real, and the trustee is watching.
  • Bring identification. Bring your meeting notice and a photo ID, plus proof of your Social Security number. For an in-person meeting you need both to get into the building and into the meeting room.
  • Do not bring food, and leave weapons at home. Courthouses and federal buildings do not allow food in meeting rooms, and weapons are prohibited. Bring water and a snack for the wait outside the room, and eat before you go in.
  • Know the building rules. Most federal buildings have security screening. Leave anything that could be taken for a weapon at home, and expect your bags to be searched.

What not to do

Most 341 meeting problems are not caused by the questions. They are caused by something the debtor did before the meeting, or by one careless answer in it.

  • Do not lie or leave assets off your schedules. Hiding property, income, or a bank account is bankruptcy fraud, and it can cost you the discharge and lead to criminal charges. If you forgot something, tell your attorney before the meeting so the schedules can be amended. Amending before the meeting is a fix. Getting caught at the meeting is a problem.
  • Do not transfer property before filing. Giving a car, a house, or cash to a relative before filing does not protect it. The trustee can undo transfers made to hide assets, and the trustee will ask about transfers in the past two years.
  • Do not repay family right before filing. Paying back a loan from a relative in the months before filing can be treated as a preferential payment, and the trustee can demand the money back from the relative. Tell your attorney about any payments to family before you file.
  • Do not run up credit cards before filing. Large charges, cash advances, or luxury purchases in the months before filing can be challenged as fraud, and the creditor can ask the court not to discharge that debt.
  • Do not miss the meeting. If you do not show up, the trustee can ask the court to dismiss your case. A dismissal ends the automatic stay, and your creditors can start collecting again. If you cannot attend, contact your attorney and the trustee before the meeting date and ask to reschedule.
  • Do not file without reading your petition. The trustee's first questions are whether you signed the petition and read it before signing. If you have not read it, say so to your attorney before the meeting, not to the trustee at it.
  • Do not take on new debt in Chapter 13 without permission. While a Chapter 13 plan runs, you generally need the trustee's or the court's permission before taking on new debt. A new car loan or credit card without permission can jeopardize the plan.
  • Do not talk about your case on social media. Assume the trustee and your creditors can see it. Do not post about your debts, your assets, your spending, or your meeting, and do not post anything that contradicts what you told the trustee.

What happens after the 341 meeting

The meeting ending does not mean the case is over. What happens next depends on your chapter.

In a Chapter 7 case:

  • The 60-day objection window. Creditors and the trustee have 60 days after the first date set for the 341 meeting to object to your discharge, or to object to the discharge of a specific debt. Objections are rare in routine cases, but the window has to close before the court acts.
  • The financial management course. Complete the debtor education course and file the certificate with the court before the 60-day deadline. Without it, the court will not grant a discharge.
  • Reaffirmation agreements. If you want to keep a car or a house and keep paying the loan, the reaffirmation agreement must be filed within 60 days after the first date set for the meeting. Talk to your attorney before signing one, because reaffirming a debt means you owe it again even after bankruptcy.
  • The discharge. If no one objects and the course certificate is on file, the court enters the discharge order shortly after the 60-day window closes. That puts the total at roughly 3 to 4 months from filing to discharge in most Chapter 7 cases. The discharge wipes out your dischargeable debts.
  • Case closing. The court usually closes the case a week or two after the discharge. If the trustee is still handling property, the case can stay open longer.

In a Chapter 13 case:

  • The confirmation hearing. After the 341 meeting, the court holds a confirmation hearing to approve your repayment plan. The trustee reports on whether the plan is feasible, and creditors can object. Most plans are confirmed, sometimes with changes.
  • Plan payments. You start making plan payments to the trustee, usually within 30 days of filing, and you keep paying for 3 to 5 years. The trustee distributes the money to creditors under the plan.
  • The discharge. There is no quick discharge in Chapter 13. The discharge comes after you complete the plan and the financial management course. If you fall behind on plan payments, the trustee or a creditor can ask the court to dismiss the case, so tell your attorney immediately if your income drops.

If the trustee continued your meeting:

A continued meeting is not a failure. It usually means the trustee needs a document you did not bring, or has a question that needs a record you do not have with you. Get the trustee the document as fast as you can, and show up to the continued date with it in hand. A continued meeting becomes a problem only when the debtor ignores it.

Costs, fees, and what help actually costs

Bankruptcy costs money up front, which is hard when the reason you are filing is that you do not have money. Know the numbers before you file, and know what help is free.

  • Chapter 7 filing fee: currently $338. You can ask the court to pay it in installments, or to waive it entirely if your income is low enough. The fee schedule is set by the court, so confirm the current amount before you file.
  • Chapter 13 filing fee: Chapter 13 has its own filing fee, set by the court. In many districts it can be paid through the plan rather than up front. Confirm the current amount and the payment options with the court or your attorney.
  • Credit counseling and debtor education: both courses are required, and each usually costs $10 to $50 through an approved provider. Fee waivers are available if your income is low enough. Ask the provider before you pay.
  • Attorney fees: a Chapter 7 attorney typically charges a flat fee, often $1,000 to $2,500 depending on where you live and how complex the case is, usually paid before filing. A Chapter 13 attorney's fee is often paid through the plan, which means little or nothing is due up front. Get the fee agreement in writing before you sign, and ask what it covers.
  • Free help: legal aid organizations help low-income filers for free. Many bankruptcy courts run self-help desks. Upsolve is a nonprofit that helps people file simple Chapter 7 cases for free. Your local bar association can refer you to a bankruptcy attorney for a low-cost consultation.
  • Your time: the preparation, the meeting, and the follow-up. The meeting itself usually runs 5 to 15 minutes, but the preparation is the real work, and it is time well spent next to a dismissed case or a lost discharge.

When to get a bankruptcy attorney

You are allowed to file bankruptcy without an attorney, and some people with simple cases do. But the petition is long, the exemptions are state-specific, and a mistake can cost you property or the discharge. Most people with a meeting date should talk to a bankruptcy attorney, and the consultation is often free or low cost. Get an attorney if any of these apply:

  • You own a house, a car with equity, or other property you want to keep.
  • You transferred property, repaid family, or ran up charges before filing.
  • You have a business, rental property, or self-employment income.
  • A creditor has sued you, garnished your wages, or threatened to object to discharge.
  • You are behind on a mortgage or a car loan and want to keep the property.
  • You do not understand the exemptions for your state, because the wrong exemption can cost you an asset.
  • You already filed and the trustee asked for documents you do not understand.
  • Someone has offered to help you file for a fee and you are not sure they are legitimate.

If you cannot afford an attorney, start with legal aid or your court's self-help desk. Bring your meeting notice, your petition, and your list of debts and assets, so the attorney can give you a real answer instead of a general one.

Mistakes that backfire

  • Missing the meeting. If you do not show up, the trustee can ask the court to dismiss your case, and the automatic stay ends with it. Keep your address current with the court, read every notice the day it arrives, and never assume a meeting was moved unless the court tells you in writing.
  • Missing the document deadlines. Photo ID and Social Security proof 14 days before, tax return 7 days before. A missing document is the most common reason a meeting gets continued, and a continued meeting costs you time.
  • Filing without reading the petition. The trustee asks whether you read it. If the petition has an error you never noticed, the trustee will find it. Read every page before you sign, and fix errors before the meeting.
  • Leaving assets or debts off the schedules. An omitted bank account, a loan from a relative, or a piece of property the trustee finds later looks like fraud, even when it was an honest mistake. List everything, then check the list twice.
  • Guessing under oath. A guessed number that turns out wrong looks like a lie. "I don't remember" is an honest answer, and the trustee would rather hear it than a wrong number.
  • Volunteering too much. Every extra fact is a new question. Answer what was asked, then stop.
  • Transferring property or repaying family before filing. The trustee can undo transfers and claw back preferential payments. Tell your attorney about every transfer and every payment to family before you file.
  • Using credit after deciding to file. Charges and cash advances in the months before filing can be challenged as fraud, and the creditor can ask the court not to discharge that debt.
  • Skipping the financial management course. The court will not grant a discharge without the certificate, and the certificate is due within 60 days after the first date set for the meeting. Take the course early, not at the deadline.
  • Signing a reaffirmation without advice. Reaffirming a car loan or a mortgage means you owe the debt again after bankruptcy. Never sign one without talking to your attorney about whether keeping the property is worth it.

FAQ

What is a 341 meeting in bankruptcy?

A 341 meeting, officially called the meeting of creditors, is a required step in every bankruptcy case. It takes its name from Section 341 of the Bankruptcy Code. A bankruptcy trustee runs it, puts you under oath, verifies your identity, and asks questions about your petition, your debts, your assets, and your finances. There is no judge present and it is not held in a courtroom. Most meetings last 5 to 15 minutes. This guide is general information, not legal advice.

How long does a 341 meeting take?

For most routine consumer cases, the 341 meeting itself lasts 5 to 15 minutes. The trustee asks a standard set of questions, and if your petition is accurate and complete, it is over quickly. Complicated cases, missing documents, or answers that do not match the petition can take longer, and the trustee can continue the meeting to a later date if more documents are needed.

What do I need to bring to a 341 meeting?

You must give the trustee a government-issued photo ID and proof of your Social Security number, usually at least 14 days before the meeting. You must also give the trustee a copy of your most recent federal income tax return at least 7 days before the meeting, and you must have filed pay stubs or other proof of earnings covering the 60 days before you filed. Bring your meeting notice, a photo ID, and a copy of your filed petition and schedules to the meeting itself, plus any documents the trustee specifically asked for, such as bank statements, mortgage statements, or proof of insurance.

What questions does the trustee ask at a 341 meeting?

The trustee starts by putting you under oath and confirming your identity. Then the questions follow a standard pattern: did you sign the petition, did you read it before signing, is everything in it true and correct, did you list all of your assets and all of your creditors, have you transferred or sold any property in the past two years, does anyone owe you money, are you involved in any lawsuits, and do you expect an inheritance or a tax refund. In a Chapter 13 case, the trustee also asks about your proposed repayment plan.

What happens if I miss my 341 meeting?

If you miss your 341 meeting, the trustee can ask the court to dismiss your case, and a dismissal means you lose the protection of the automatic stay and your creditors can start collecting again. If you cannot attend, contact your attorney and the trustee immediately, before the meeting date, and ask to reschedule. Do not simply fail to show up. If you missed it already, contact your attorney the same day, because in some cases the trustee will agree to a new date if you act fast.

Do creditors come to the 341 meeting?

Creditors get notice of the meeting and have the right to attend and ask you questions, but in most consumer cases they do not show up. When a creditor does attend, it is usually because the debt involves something specific, such as a recent large charge, a secured debt like a car loan, or a debt the creditor believes should not be discharged. The trustee still runs the meeting, and your attorney can object if a creditor asks questions outside the scope of the case.

When do I get my discharge after the 341 meeting?

In a Chapter 7 case, creditors and the trustee have 60 days after the first date set for the 341 meeting to object to your discharge. If no one objects, you have completed the financial management course, and any reaffirmation agreements are filed, the court usually enters the discharge order shortly after that 60-day window closes, which puts the total at roughly 3 to 4 months after filing. In a Chapter 13 case there is no quick discharge. You make plan payments for 3 to 5 years, and the discharge comes after you complete the plan.

Is this legal advice?

No. This guide is general information about how bankruptcy 341 meetings work. Bankruptcy law, deadlines, exemptions, and procedures vary by state and by district, and the right steps depend on your individual case. For anything that could affect your case, talk to a bankruptcy attorney or a legal aid organization before your meeting date.
