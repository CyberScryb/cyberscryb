How to Write a Pet Resume for a Rental Application

A pet resume is a one-page summary that sells your pet as a low-risk tenant. Most landlords decide on pets in under a minute, so the resume does its work fast: photo, health records, training, house habits, references, and your pet-deposit offer. Here is exactly what to include, what to leave out, and a full example you can copy.

By CyberScryb 📅 Updated November 2, 2026 ⏱ 8 min read
⚡ Key Takeaways (TL;DR)

A pet resume is a one-page summary that sells your pet as a low-risk tenant, and most landlords decide on pets in under a minute. Put the essentials on a single scannable page: a photo, breed or mix, age, weight, spay/neuter status, vaccination records, training and behavior notes, house habits, references from a prior landlord, vet, or trainer, an emergency contact, and your offer of a pet deposit or pet rent. Skip cute filler, guarantees you cannot back up, and anything you would not want read aloud in a dispute. Attach it to your rental application and bring a copy to any pet interview.

Step 1: Understand What the Pet Resume Is Selling

A landlord's worry about pets is never about your pet's personality. It is about three concrete risks: damage to the unit, noise and complaints from neighbors, and liability if the animal hurts someone. Your pet resume exists to answer those three fears before the landlord voices them. Every line on the page should map to one of them: vaccinated and neutered addresses health and liability, crate trained and quiet addresses noise and damage, insured and supervised addresses liability.

This is also why length matters. Landlords screen in volume, and most pet decisions are made in under a minute. A two-page resume will not get read; a one-page sheet with a photo, clear headings, and contact info will. Think of it as a job resume for your pet: the reader is skimming for reasons to say yes, not reading for the story.

Step 2: What to Include

Include these items, in roughly this order. Landlords look for proof of responsibility, so third-party documentation beats your own adjectives every time:

  • A clear photo of your pet. A friendly, recent photo. This is what gets remembered after the application stack is put down, and it keeps your pet from being an abstract risk.
  • Breed or mix, age, and weight. Be honest. Many insurers have breed restrictions the landlord is checking against anyway, and a mislabeled breed that gets spotted in a pet interview destroys your credibility instantly.
  • Spay/neuter status and vaccination records. List vaccinations as current and note the date of the last vet visit. If your vet will provide a short statement, attach it or note that it is available.
  • Behavior and training. Obedience class completion, certifications, leash manners, socialization. One named credential beats three adjectives.
  • House habits. House-trained, crate trained, what happens when home alone, how the pet is exercised and supervised. This is the paragraph that kills the noise-and-damage worry.
  • References. A prior landlord who will say there were no complaints or damage, your vet, a trainer or dog walker. Name and phone number, with their permission.
  • Pet insurance. If you carry liability coverage for the pet, say so and list the carrier. It is a small line that answers the liability fear directly.
  • An emergency contact for the pet. Someone who can take the animal in an emergency. This signals that the pet is accounted for in a crisis, not the landlord's problem.
  • Your offer of a pet deposit or pet rent. State what you are willing to pay per month and as an upfront deposit. Offering first frames you as a negotiator, not a petitioner.

Step 3: What NOT to Include

The resume gets shorter here on purpose. These are the lines that make landlords pause, and none of them help you:

  • Cute filler. Nicknames, favorite toys, the story of how you met. Sweet, but it pushes the proof off the page.
  • Guarantees you cannot back up. "Will never bark," "completely silent," "zero damage guaranteed." No pet is, and a landlord who has heard it before reads it as a red flag.
  • Excuses for past incidents. If a prior landlord had a problem with the pet, do not litigate it on the resume. Address it briefly and honestly if asked, or leave that reference off.
  • Your own complaints about past landlords. Anything that reads as adversarial tells the next landlord you might be one too.
  • Legal threats or bluffs. A pet resume is a sales document, not a negotiation letter. Save advocacy for the actual conversation.
  • Unnecessary personal detail. The resume is about the pet and your responsibility for it. Your application covers everything else.

Step 4: The Complete Example Pet Resume

Here is a full example for a fictional pet. Copy the structure and swap in your own details:

Pet Resume: Milo

Photo
[Attach a clear, friendly photo of your pet here.]
The Basics
Milo — 4-year-old neutered Labrador mix, 62 lbs. Rabies, distemper, and bordetella vaccinations current (last vet visit March 2026). Licensed with the city.
Behavior & Training
Completed Basic Obedience at Northside Dog Training (2023). Walks on leash without pulling, settles in crate on command, socialized with other dogs and people. No bite history.
House Habits
Fully house-trained; crated when home alone during work hours. Exercised twice daily. No noise complaints or damage at previous residence (two years). Litter-style schedule: morning, midday, evening.
References
Prior landlord: Dana R., (555) 014-2288 — "No complaints, no damage." Veterinarian: Ponderay Animal Clinic, Dr. L. H., (555) 014-9910. Trainer: Northside Dog Training, (555) 014-5531.
Insurance & Emergency Contact
Pet liability insurance through Fetch, policy #PET-88412. Emergency contact: my sister, Kara M., (555) 014-7702, who can take Milo same-day if needed.
Financial Offer
Willing to pay a $300 refundable pet deposit and $35/month pet rent. Happy to sign a pet addendum to the lease.

Notice the pattern: every claim is checkable. A landlord who wants to verify can call three numbers. A landlord who does not want to verify still feels the weight of someone who can be checked.

Step 5: Attach It, Bring It, Update It

How you get the resume in front of the landlord matters:

  • Attach it to every rental application. If the application has an upload field, use it. If it is paper, paperclip it to the front. It should be part of the first impression, not an afterthought.
  • Bring a copy to any pet interview. Some landlords do a meet-and-greet with the pet before approving. Hand them the resume as a leave-behind; it keeps selling after the meeting ends.
  • Update it when facts change. New vaccination cycle, new training certificate, new landlord reference after a lease. A stale resume with an expired vet date invites the opposite of the signal you want.
  • Tailor the financial offer per listing. If a listing already states its pet deposit, match it instead of underbidding. The point is to remove friction, not to haggle.

FAQ

Do I need a pet resume for renting?

Not always, but it is one of the cheapest ways to get ahead in a competitive rental market. A pet resume answers a landlord's unspoken questions (is it vaccinated, trained, quiet, supervised?) before they have to ask, and it shows you take their property seriously. In a pet-restricted or competitive listing, it can be the tiebreaker between two otherwise equal applicants.

What should a pet resume include?

A photo of your pet, its breed or mix, age, weight, and spay/neuter status, current vaccination records, behavior and training credentials, house habits (house-trained, crate trained, supervised when home alone), references from a prior landlord, vet, or trainer, pet insurance if you carry it, an emergency contact for the pet, and your offer of a pet deposit or monthly pet rent. One page, scannable, every claim checkable.

Do landlords actually read pet resumes?

Most landlords skim rather than read, which is why the resume should be one page and scannable. The pet resume works as a visual signal of responsibility: a renter who bothered to document their pet's behavior reads as a renter who will bother to manage that behavior in the unit. Landlords report that most pet decisions are made in under a minute.

What is a pet interview?

A pet interview (sometimes called a meet-and-greet) is an in-person meeting where the landlord or property manager meets your pet before approving the application. They are checking temperament, how the animal behaves around strangers, and how you handle it. Bring the pet resume to the interview as a leave-behind, keep your pet calm and leashed or contained, and answer behavior questions honestly.

How do I prove my pet is well-behaved?

Stack third-party proof: a reference letter from a prior landlord saying the pet caused no damage or complaints, a statement from your vet about the pet's temperament, any obedience or training certificates, and a clean vaccination and spay/neuter record. Specifics beat adjectives; "crate trained, no noise complaints in two years at previous unit" beats "very well behaved."

Should I mention my pet's breed?

Yes, be honest about it. Hiding or mislabeling the breed backfires when the landlord meets the pet, and many insurance policies have breed restrictions that the landlord is checking against anyway. A mixed-breed pet can be listed as a mix. Pair the breed line with temperament and training proof so the breed is one fact among many, not the whole story.

Do ESA or service animals need a pet resume?

Assistance animals are treated differently from pets under fair housing law: they are generally not subject to pet fees, deposits, or breed and size restrictions, and a pet resume is not the mechanism for requesting accommodation. Keep valid documentation from your provider and follow the accommodation request process. This guide is about pet resumes for pets, not legal advice about assistance animals.
