How to Prepare for Small Claims Court

Small claims court is the one court built for people without lawyers: a short form, a small fee, and a hearing that is usually a conversation with a judge, not a trial. The people who win are the ones who show up with the right documents in the right order. Here is how to get there.

What small claims court is

Small claims court is a division of the local court that handles smaller money disputes with a stripped-down procedure. There are no lawyers required, no jury in most states, no formal rules of evidence, and no long trial. You file a short form, pay a small fee, and a judge or magistrate hears both sides, usually in 15 to 30 minutes, and decides.

It exists for the disputes that are too small for a lawyer to be worth hiring and too real to walk away from: a landlord who kept the deposit, a contractor who took a deposit and never did the work, a friend who borrowed money and stopped answering, a seller who sold you a car with a known defect, a client who never paid the invoice. If the dispute is about money and the amount fits under your state's limit, small claims is usually the right room.

It is not the right room for everything. Small claims courts generally cannot hear cases about divorce, child custody, restraining orders, or disputes where you want the court to order someone to do something rather than pay money. Some states also exclude certain kinds of claims, such as defamation or cases against the government. If you are not sure your case belongs there, the court clerk can usually tell you, and your state court website lists what small claims covers.

Who can file, and for how much

Almost anyone can file a small claims case: individuals, and in most states small businesses and landlords too. You must be 18 or older, or file through a parent or guardian if you are not. You file in the county where the other party lives or does business, or where the dispute happened, and the clerk's office will tell you if you have the wrong county.

The dollar limit is the first thing to check, because it is set by state law and it varies a lot. Most states set the limit between $5,000 and $10,000. A few states go higher, up to $25,000 in some cases. The number also changes over time as states raise it, so do not take a limit you read in an old article at face value. Check your state court website or call the clerk and ask for the current limit before you file.

If the amount you are owed is above your state's limit, you have two choices. You can reduce your claim to fit under the limit and give up the rest, which is sometimes the right call when the extra amount is small and the case is simple. Or you can file in regular civil court, where there is no dollar cap but the procedure is more formal, the fees are higher, and a lawyer is usually worth having. Do not split one dispute into two small claims cases to get around the limit; courts treat that as one claim.

There is also a deadline. Every kind of claim has a statute of limitations, the window during which you are allowed to sue, and the window depends on the type of claim and your state. A written contract, an oral agreement, and property damage can each have a different deadline, commonly anywhere from two to six years. If the deadline has passed, the other side can ask the court to dismiss the case. Check the deadline for your type of claim before you spend the filing fee.

Before you file: three things to do first

Filing is the last step in the preparation, not the first. Three things belong before it.

  1. Send a written demand. Write to the other party, state what they owe and why, give a specific deadline, usually 14 to 30 days, and say you will file in small claims court if it is not paid. Send it by a method you can prove: certified mail, email with a read receipt, or text message you screenshot. Many cases settle at this step, because a dated demand letter shows the other side you are serious. And if the case does go to court, the demand letter is evidence that you tried to resolve it first. Judges notice that.
  2. Get the other party's exact legal name right. You have to sue the right person or business under the right name, or the judgment can be unenforceable. For a person, that is their full legal name, not a nickname. For a business, it is the registered business name, which you can usually look up on your secretary of state's business search. If you sue a landlord, find out whether you are suing the person or the LLC that owns the property. Get the address right too, because that is where the court papers get served.
  3. Ask whether they can pay. A judgment against someone with no wages, no bank account, and no property is a piece of paper. Before you file, think honestly about whether the other party has income or assets you could collect against. A business that is still operating usually does. A person with a steady job usually does. Someone who has already disappeared and left no trace usually does not, and no amount of preparation changes that.

Filing your case

Filing is a short form, usually called a statement of claim or complaint. The clerk's office has it, and most state court websites let you download it. The form asks for your name and address, the other party's name and address, the amount you are claiming, and a short description of what happened. Keep the description plain and factual; the details belong in your statement of facts and your evidence, not on the form.

The filing fee is usually between $30 and $100, depending on the state and the amount claimed. Some states charge more for larger claims. If you cannot afford the fee, ask the clerk for a fee waiver form and bring proof of income, such as pay stubs or a benefits letter. The fee is paid when you file, and it is usually not refunded if you lose, though in some states the winner can ask the court to add the filing fee to the judgment.

After you file, the court sets a hearing date, usually four to eight weeks out. Write the date down the moment you get it, and put it on a calendar you actually check. If the date conflicts with something you cannot move, contact the clerk right away to ask about rescheduling; courts are far more flexible before the date than after you miss it.

Serving the other party

The other party has to be formally notified of the case, and the court will not hear your case until that has happened. This is called service of process, and the rules are strict: you generally cannot serve the papers yourself.

  • Certified mail. In many states the clerk mails the papers by certified mail, and the signed receipt is the proof of service. This is the cheapest option, and it works when the other party has a stable address.
  • Process server. A licensed process server hand-delivers the papers for a fee, usually $30 to $75. This is the reliable option when certified mail goes unclaimed or the other party is avoiding service.
  • Sheriff or constable. In some counties the sheriff's office serves small claims papers for a similar fee. Ask the clerk which methods your county allows.

Whoever serves the papers has to file proof of service with the court. If service fails, the hearing gets postponed, not cancelled, so check with the clerk before the hearing date to confirm the other party was served. If they were not, ask the clerk what to do next rather than showing up to a hearing that cannot happen.

The evidence and documentation checklist

Small claims judges decide on documents, not stories. Your job before the hearing is to build a file that tells the story by itself, in date order, so the judge can follow it without your narration. Gather what applies to your case and bring three copies of each document: one for the judge, one for the other party, and one for yourself.

  • The agreement. The contract, lease, written quote, invoice, or work order. If the agreement was verbal, write down what was agreed, when, and who was present, and note any witness who can confirm it.
  • Payment records. Bank statements, cancelled checks, receipts, payment app records, and invoices showing what you paid and what is still owed. Circle the relevant lines so the judge does not have to hunt.
  • Photos and video. Dated photos of damage, unfinished work, or the condition of the property, with dates printed or written on them. Photos with timestamps are stronger than photos without them.
  • Messages in writing. Text messages, emails, and letters between you and the other party, printed with dates and times. If a promise was made in a message, highlight it. If a message shows the other party admitting the problem, highlight that too.
  • Your written demand. The demand letter you sent before filing, plus proof you sent it: the certified mail receipt, the email, or the screenshot. This shows the judge you tried to settle first.
  • Repair estimates and receipts. If you are claiming the cost of fixing something, bring the estimate or the receipt for the repair, and the receipt for anything you already paid to fix it. A written estimate from a licensed repair person carries more weight than your own guess at the cost.
  • Witnesses. A witness who saw what happened, or who was present when an agreement was made, can testify in person. A written statement from a witness is sometimes accepted, but live testimony is stronger. Tell your witness the date and time in advance, and make sure they know to stick to what they saw, not what they think.
  • Your timeline. A one-page list of dates: when the agreement was made, when you paid, when the problem started, when you complained, when you sent the demand, when you filed. Dates are the backbone of a small claims case, and a clear timeline is easier for a judge to follow than a stack of loose papers.

Leave originals at home and bring copies. Number your exhibits and keep a list of what each one is, so when the judge asks for the invoice you can hand it over in seconds instead of shuffling through a folder.

How to write your statement of facts

The statement of facts is the short written account of what happened, in order. Some courts ask for it with the filing; in others you bring it to the hearing. Either way, write it before the hearing, because writing it forces you to find the gaps in your case while there is still time to fill them.

  1. Start with the agreement. One paragraph: who the parties are, what was agreed, when, and for how much. "On March 4, 2026, I hired [name] to repair the roof on my house at [address] for $2,400, with a written quote dated March 2."
  2. Then the facts, in date order. One paragraph per event, each with a date and a document that proves it. "I paid a $1,200 deposit on March 5 (receipt, Exhibit 2). The work was scheduled for March 12. No one came. I called on March 13 and was told the work would start March 20 (text message, Exhibit 3)."
  3. Then the harm. What went wrong, what it cost you, and how you calculated the amount you are claiming. Show the math: the deposit paid, the cost of the repair, the total, minus anything already refunded. A judge trusts a claim with a visible calculation more than a round number with no breakdown.
  4. Then what you did about it. The complaint, the demand letter, the deadline you gave, and the response or lack of one. "I sent a written demand on April 2 asking for a refund by April 16 (Exhibit 6). I received no reply."
  5. End with the ask. One sentence: the amount you are asking the court to award, plus the filing fee if your court allows it.

Keep it to one or two pages. Write in plain language, not legal language. Leave out insults, opinions about the other party's character, and anything that happened before the agreement. If a sentence does not have a date and a document behind it, cut it or find the document.

What happens at the hearing

Arrive early, at least 30 minutes before your case is called, and check in with the clerk. Bring your organized file, your timeline, and your statement of facts. Dress neatly, turn your phone off, and address the judge as Your Honor.

The hearing usually runs like this. The judge calls your case and swears both sides in. You speak first, since you filed the case. You tell the story in order, hand over your documents as you reach them, and the judge asks questions. Then the other side tells their version, and the judge asks them questions. You may get a chance to respond to what they said. Witnesses testify if you brought any. Then the judge decides, either on the spot or by a written decision sent later.

A few things that help in the room:

  • Stick to the facts and the dates. The judge wants to know what happened, when, and what it cost. Feelings are real, but they are not evidence.
  • Answer the question asked. If the judge asks a yes-or-no question, answer it, then add the context. Rambling answers lose the judge's attention and your credibility.
  • Do not interrupt. When the other side is speaking, write down what you disagree with and raise it when it is your turn. Interrupting looks bad and the judge will stop you.
  • Admit what is true. If the judge asks whether you were late with a payment, or whether you agreed to a change, say so if it is true. Judges trust people who concede the small points, and distrust people who deny everything.
  • Bring the documents, not just the story. If you say you paid, have the receipt ready. If you say they promised, have the message ready. The hearing is short, and the judge will not wait while you search your phone.

Costs and fees, honestly

Small claims court is cheap compared to hiring a lawyer, but it is not free, and the costs are worth counting before you file.

  • Filing fee: usually $30 to $100, depending on the state and the amount claimed. Ask the clerk for the exact amount and whether a fee waiver is available.
  • Service: $0 if the clerk mails the papers, $30 to $75 if you use a process server or the sheriff.
  • Copies and postage: small, but three copies of every exhibit adds up. Print them at home or the library, not the courthouse.
  • Your time: the filing, the trip to the courthouse, the hearing, and the follow-up. Half a day at minimum, usually more.
  • Collecting: if you win and the other party does not pay, each enforcement step, such as a garnishment or a levy, has its own filing fee.

In many states the winning side can ask the court to add the filing fee and service costs to the judgment. Ask the clerk whether that is allowed where you file, and include the costs in your claim if it is.

After the hearing: collecting a judgment

This is the part people are least prepared for. Winning the case does not put money in your hand. A judgment is a court order saying the other party owes you. If they pay voluntarily, you are done: get the payment in a traceable form, such as a check or a bank transfer, and file a satisfaction of judgment with the court so the record shows the debt is paid.

If they do not pay, you have to enforce the judgment, and enforcement is a separate process with its own filings. The common tools are:

  • Wage garnishment. The court orders the other party's employer to send part of each paycheck to you until the judgment is paid. You need to know where they work, and federal and state law limit how much of a paycheck can be taken.
  • Bank levy. The court orders the other party's bank to turn over funds from their account. You need to know which bank holds the account.
  • Property lien. The judgment is recorded as a lien against real property the other party owns. A lien does not get you paid right away; it usually gets paid when the property is sold or refinanced.

Each of these requires a separate filing and fee, and each has its own rules about what is exempt. Some income, such as Social Security benefits, is generally protected from garnishment. Some judgments go unpaid because the person has no wages, no bank balance, and no property to collect against. That is why the "can they pay" question belongs before you file, not after you win.

Judgments also expire. In most states a judgment is good for a number of years, commonly 10 to 20, and can sometimes be renewed. Keep the judgment paperwork and the record of any payments, and check your state's renewal rules if the debt is still unpaid years later.

Mistakes that backfire

  • Suing the wrong name. Suing a nickname, a trade name, or the wrong LLC can leave you with a judgment against someone who does not legally exist. Look up the registered business name before you file.
  • Filing in the wrong county. The case gets dismissed or moved, and you pay the fee twice. File where the other party lives or does business, or where the dispute happened, and confirm with the clerk.
  • Skipping the demand letter. It costs almost nothing, it settles a surprising number of cases, and the judge notices when you tried to resolve the dispute before suing.
  • Missing the deadline. The statute of limitations and the hearing date are both hard deadlines. A late filing can be dismissed, and a missed hearing can be decided without you.
  • Bringing a story instead of a file. "He promised" is not evidence. The message where he promised is. If it is not written down, dated, and in your file, the judge may never hear it.
  • Claiming a round number with no math. A claim for $5,000 with no breakdown is weaker than a claim for $4,380 with receipts. Show the calculation.
  • Losing your temper in the hearing. Judges decide credibility fast, and the calm side usually wins it. Say the facts, answer the questions, and let the documents do the arguing.
  • Assuming the judgment is the money. A judgment is an order, not a payment. If the other party does not pay voluntarily, collecting takes more filings, more fees, and more time.

FAQ

What is small claims court?

Small claims court is a division of the local court that handles smaller money disputes without lawyers, formal rules of evidence, or long trials. It is built for ordinary people: filing is a short form, the fee is low, and the hearing is usually a conversation with a judge rather than a trial. Typical cases are unpaid debts, security deposits, damaged property, and unpaid work. This guide is general information, not legal advice.

How much money can I sue for in small claims court?

It depends on your state. Most states set the limit between $5,000 and $10,000, and a few go as high as $25,000. The limit is set by state law and changes over time, so check your state court website or your county clerk before you file. If the amount you are owed is above your state's limit, you either reduce your claim to fit under it or file in regular civil court, where the procedure is more formal and a lawyer is usually worth having.

Do I need a lawyer for small claims court?

No. Small claims court is designed for people to represent themselves, and in some states lawyers are not allowed in small claims hearings at all. You can bring a lawyer in most states if you want one, but the cost often eats the amount you are claiming. If the case involves a complicated legal question, or the other side has a lawyer, a short consultation with a legal aid organization or a local attorney before the hearing is worth the time.

How much does it cost to file a small claims case?

Filing fees are usually between $30 and $100, depending on the state and the amount you are claiming. Some states charge more for larger claims. Serving the other party costs extra if you use a process server or the sheriff, usually another $30 to $75. If you cannot afford the fee, most courts have a fee waiver form: ask the clerk for it and bring proof of income. The fee is paid when you file, not when you win.

What evidence do I need for small claims court?

Bring the documents that prove what happened and how much you are owed: the contract or agreement, invoices, receipts, photos of damage, text messages and emails with dates, payment records, and any written demand you sent. Small claims judges decide on documents, not stories, so organize everything chronologically and bring three copies of each document: one for the judge, one for the other party, and one for yourself.

What happens at a small claims hearing?

The judge or magistrate calls your case, swears both sides in, and asks each side to tell their version in turn. You present your documents, the other side responds, and the judge asks questions. There is no jury in most small claims courts. Hearings are usually short, often 15 to 30 minutes. The judge may decide on the spot or send a written decision later. Dress neatly, address the judge as Your Honor, and stick to the facts and the dates.

How do I collect a small claims judgment?

Winning the case does not put money in your hand. A judgment is a court order saying the other party owes you, and if they do not pay voluntarily you have to enforce it. Common enforcement tools are wage garnishment, a bank levy, and a lien on property, and each one requires a separate filing with the court. The procedure varies by state, and some judgments go unpaid because the person has no wages or assets to collect from. Before you file, ask yourself whether the other party has money or property you could actually collect against.

Is this legal advice?

No. This guide is general information about how small claims court works. The dollar limits, filing fees, deadlines, and procedures vary by state and by county. For anything that could affect your case, check your state court website, ask the court clerk what form to use, or talk to a legal aid organization in your state before you file.
