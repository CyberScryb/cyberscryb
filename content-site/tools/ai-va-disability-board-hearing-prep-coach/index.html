What actually matters

The judge decides from the file plus your testimony

A Board hearing turns on two things: what the record already shows, and what you say under oath. The judge has your claims file, the C&P exam, and your treatment records. Your testimony fills the gaps the paper cannot: when symptoms started, how they changed, what a normal day costs you. Everything you prepare must answer one question: what does service have to do with this condition, and what does it cost you now. The hearing strategy mode exists to lock onto that question first.

The link to service is the fight in most cases

Service connection turns on whether your condition started in, or was made worse by, your service. A diagnosis alone does not win a hearing; the connection does. Service treatment records that show the same complaint visit after visit, a nexus letter that explains the link in plain terms, buddy statements from people who saw it happen: those decide hearings. Gather every record before the hearing, and send new evidence to the judge within 90 days after the hearing.

Short, concrete answers win

Board hearings usually run under an hour and judges keep a tight docket. Answer in facts: what you do, how long it takes, what forces you to stop, and how long recovery takes. Do not guess, do not exaggerate, and say you do not remember when you do not. Practice keeping answers under a minute; the mock hearing room is built for exactly that.

Never miss the hearing

A missed hearing is treated as withdrawn, and the judge decides your appeal without your testimony. If the date truly does not work, send the Board a written request with a good reason at least two weeks before the hearing. Treat the hearing date like the court date it is, and keep treating and keep records current while you wait. After the hearing you have 90 days to send new evidence.

FAQ

What happens at a VA disability Board hearing?

A Board hearing is an informal, non-adversarial hearing before a Veterans Law Judge at the Board of Veterans' Appeals. You testify under oath about your service, your conditions, and how they limit your daily life and work. The judge asks most of the questions, reviews your file, and lets you and your representative make your case. There is no VA lawyer arguing against you, and no jury. The judge does not decide on the spot; a written decision comes later, usually months after the hearing. The mock hearing room above lets you rehearse the question-and-answer rhythm before the real one. This is general information, not legal advice.

How do I request a Board hearing?

You request a hearing on VA Form 10182, the Decision Review Request: Board Appeal, by choosing the hearing docket. The form must reach the Board within one year of the date on the rating decision letter. Enter your decision date above and the tool works out your one-year deadline. If you already filed, check your docket choice with your representative before you prepare. This is general information, not legal advice.

What are the three Board dockets?

Direct Review means the judge decides from the evidence already in your file, with no new evidence and no hearing. Evidence Submission means you can send new evidence within 90 days after the Board receives your appeal, but there is no hearing. Hearing means you get a hearing before a Veterans Law Judge, and you can send new evidence at the hearing or within 90 days after it. You pick the docket on Form 10182, and you generally cannot switch later, so talk to an accredited representative before you file.

How long does a Board hearing last?

A hearing usually runs under an hour, though complex cases run longer. The judge works through your service history, your conditions, and your daily limits, then lets your representative ask questions. Short, specific answers about what you can and cannot do matter more than long explanations. Practice keeping answers under a minute.

Can I bring new evidence to my Board hearing?

Yes. You can bring new evidence to the hearing and hand it to the judge, and you have 90 days after the hearing to send more. Enter your hearing date above and the tool works out when that 90-day window closes. Do not wait until the last week; send evidence as soon as you have it, and keep copies of everything you send.

Can I bring someone with me to my Board hearing?

Yes. You can bring an accredited representative, an accredited agent, or an attorney, and witnesses who know your service and your daily limits firsthand, such as a fellow service member or a spouse. Tell the Board ahead of time if a witness will attend, and tell witnesses to stick to what they personally saw and what they see you struggle with each day.

What will the judge ask me at a Board hearing?

Expect questions about when each condition started, what happened in service, what your C&P exam found, what a typical day looks like, how long you can sit, stand, walk, and lift, and what work you can still do. The judge may also ask about treatment, medications and side effects, and the jobs you have held since service. Answer in plain, concrete terms: what you do, how long it takes, and what forces you to stop. A one-minute opening statement framing your situation helps; the prep packet mode drafts one from your facts.

What if I miss my Board hearing?

A missed hearing is treated as withdrawn, and the judge decides your appeal without your testimony, though you still have 90 days from the missed date to send evidence. If the date truly does not work, send the Board a written request with a good reason at least two weeks before the hearing. If an emergency makes you miss it, contact the Board and your representative right away. This is general information, not legal advice.

Is this legal advice?

No. This tool prepares you for a hearing from the facts you enter: strategy, a prep packet, and mock practice. It does not assess your claim, predict the outcome, or cite law. For a close case or if anything sensitive comes up, review with an accredited Veterans Service Organization representative, an accredited agent, or an accredited attorney before the hearing.
