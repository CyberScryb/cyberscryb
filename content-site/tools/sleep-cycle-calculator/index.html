Sleep Cycle Calculator

You do not wake up refreshed because of how long you slept. You wake up refreshed when you wake at the end of a full 90-minute sleep cycle instead of in the middle of one. Pick a mode below and plan your sleep around complete cycles. Instant, private, free forever.

These bedtimes assume about 15 minutes to fall asleep. If you know you take longer, move each bedtime earlier by the difference. Highlighted times give you 5 to 6 full cycles, which is the sweet spot for most adults.

Wake times count 15 minutes of drifting off, then full 90-minute cycles after that. Set your alarm for one of these times to wake at a cycle boundary instead of mid-cycle.

How the Math Works

The average adult sleep cycle runs about 90 minutes, cycling through light sleep, deep sleep, and REM sleep before the next one starts. Most adults need 5 to 6 complete cycles a night, which is 7.5 to 9 hours of actual sleep. The calculator adds the average 15 minutes it takes to fall asleep, so the bedtimes and wake times it shows land on cycle boundaries, not in the middle of deep sleep.

Worked example: you need to be up at 7:00 AM. Six cycles is 9 hours of sleep, plus 15 minutes to drift off: count back 9 hours 15 minutes from 7:00 AM and you get 9:45 PM. Five cycles is 7 hours 30 minutes of sleep, plus 15 minutes: 11:15 PM. Four cycles is the bare minimum at 6 hours 15 minutes of sleep plus 15 minutes: 12:45 AM, which will get you through one rough day but should not be the plan. The full breakdown of what happens inside each 90-minute cycle is in our guide to how sleep cycles work.

Why Waking Mid-Cycle Ruins Your Morning

Being dragged awake out of deep sleep triggers sleep inertia, that heavy, foggy grogginess that can take up to an hour to burn off. This is why you can sleep 8 full hours and still feel wrecked: your alarm fired during the deepest part of a cycle. Waking at a cycle boundary, when the brain is already near the surface in light sleep, skips most of that inertia. The total hours matter less than where your alarm lands inside the cycle.

The classic trap is the snooze button. Nine extra minutes of fragmented dozing gives you no real sleep and starts a new cycle you will never finish, so you wake groggier than if you had just gotten up. One of the most useful things this calculator does is remove the guesswork: set one alarm at a cycle boundary and get up the first time.

When People Reach for a Sleep Cycle Calculator

Shift workers with odd start times use it to back out a bedtime that still gives them 5 full cycles when a normal schedule is impossible. Students before exam week use it to stop the all-nighter spiral: better to get 4.5 or 6 hours aligned to cycles than 7 hours cut mid-cycle. New parents, people dealing with insomnia, and anyone training for a race or a big physical day use it to protect the deep-sleep cycles that drive recovery.

It also settles the bedtime argument honestly. "I go to bed at 11 and get up at 6" sounds like 7 hours, but minus 15 minutes to fall asleep and a mid-cycle alarm it is really 6.75 hours with a rough landing. Seeing the actual cycle counts makes the tradeoffs concrete, and most people pick a bedtime 30 to 90 minutes earlier than they expected.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long is a sleep cycle?

About 90 minutes for the average adult. Each cycle moves through light sleep, deep sleep, and REM sleep before the next cycle starts. Most adults need 5 to 6 complete cycles per night, which is why 7.5 to 9 hours is the standard recommendation.

Why do I wake up tired even after 8 hours of sleep?

Usually because you woke in the middle of a deep or REM phase instead of at the end of a complete cycle. An alarm cutting into deep sleep causes sleep inertia, the heavy groggy feeling that can linger for an hour. Waking at a cycle boundary feels dramatically better even on fewer total hours.

Is 5 sleep cycles enough?

Yes, for many adults. Five full 90-minute cycles equals 7.5 hours of sleep, which sits inside the recommended 7 to 9 hour range. Six cycles (9 hours) is better if you are sleep-deprived, recovering, or training hard.

Why does the calculator subtract 15 minutes for falling asleep?

Because a sleep cycle does not start when you climb into bed, it starts when you fall asleep. The average adult takes about 15 minutes to fall asleep, so the calculator counts from there. If you know you take longer, shift the bedtimes earlier by the difference.

Should I wake up at the same time every day?

Yes, as much as possible. A consistent wake time anchors your circadian rhythm, which makes falling asleep faster and improves sleep quality. Even on weekends, try to stay within an hour of your weekday wake time rather than sleeping in for hours.
