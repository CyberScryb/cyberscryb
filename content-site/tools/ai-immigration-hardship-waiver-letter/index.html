Evidence checklist

The letter argues; the evidence proves. Tap items as you collect them, progress saves on this device.

  • Family ties: marriage certificate, birth certificates, photos of family life together, proof of shared address
  • Health conditions: doctor letters explaining the condition and what an interruption in care would mean, treatment records, prescription history
  • Financial impact: pay stubs, tax returns, bank statements, lease or mortgage records, debt statements showing dependence on the applicant's income
  • Educational disruption: school enrollment records, report cards, letters from teachers about the cost of a move mid-year
  • Country conditions: State Department travel advisories, reputable news or NGO reports on conditions the qualifying relative would face
  • Other factors: community letters, employment records, proof of rehabilitation or civic involvement, anything tying the hardship story to documents
  • Organize every item by the hardship factor it supports, translated if not in English, and bring the whole packet to your attorney before filing

What actually matters

Hardship is measured on the qualifying relative

The waiver does not ask how hard removal would be for the applicant. It asks what would happen to the qualifying relative if the applicant cannot stay or return. Every fact in the letter should answer that question from the relative's point of view.

Ordinary separation is not enough

USCIS looks for hardship beyond what most families face when separated by immigration enforcement. Stack the factors together: health plus finances plus country conditions is a far stronger showing than any one factor alone.

Proof beats adjectives

A doctor's letter saying what an interruption in care would cause does more work than any paragraph calling the hardship severe. The checklist above exists for a reason: collect the document for every claim the letter makes.

The letter is not the application

A waiver package includes forms, fees, evidence, and sometimes an interview. The letter is one piece of the package, and the weakest piece is the one filed without attorney review. Draft here, finalize with counsel.

FAQ

What is an immigration hardship waiver?

A hardship waiver asks USCIS to forgive a ground of inadmissibility, such as unlawful presence or certain criminal grounds, so a visa or adjustment of status can move forward. Most waivers turn on proving the qualifying relative would suffer extreme hardship if the applicant were removed or barred from returning. This tool drafts the hardship letter only; it does not file the application.

What is the difference between I-601, I-601A, and 212(h)?

I-601 waives grounds of inadmissibility after a finding is made. I-601A is a provisional unlawful-presence waiver for qualifying relatives of U.S. citizens and permanent residents, sought before departing the United States. 212(h) waives certain criminal grounds of inadmissibility. The tool asks which one you are drafting so the letter uses the right framing.

What counts as extreme hardship?

USCIS weighs factors like family ties, health conditions, financial impact, educational disruption, country conditions, and other relevant circumstances, both separately and together. Ordinary separation is not enough on its own. The tool walks you through each factor so the letter shows the full picture.

Who is a qualifying relative?

For most hardship waivers the qualifying relative is a U.S. citizen or lawful permanent resident spouse or parent. The letter must name that person and their status, because the hardship is measured against what happens to them. Enter this carefully; the tool does not verify eligibility.

What evidence should I attach to the waiver?

Medical records and doctor letters for health claims, bank statements, pay stubs, and debt records for financial impact, school records for educational disruption, country-condition reports, marriage and birth certificates for family ties, and proof of community involvement. The tool generates a checklist from the factors you checked so you can collect proof before filing.

Is this legal advice?

No. This tool drafts a hardship letter from the facts you enter. It does not tell you whether you qualify, which waiver fits your case, or what outcome to expect. Have a licensed immigration attorney review the draft and your eligibility before filing anything with USCIS.
