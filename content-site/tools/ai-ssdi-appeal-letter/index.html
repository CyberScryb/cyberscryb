Filing checklist

Do these the day you file. Tap items as you finish them; progress saves on this device.

  • Calendar the deadline first: the date printed on the denial letter, plus 5 days for mailing, plus 60 days. Write it on the envelope the letter came in.
  • Appeal, do not file a new application. A new application gets a new filing date and can cost you months of back pay.
  • For reconsideration, file Form SSA-561 and, if the appeal is about the medical decision, Form SSA-3441-BK, the Disability Report Appeal.
  • For a hearing, file Form HA-501 within 60 days of the reconsideration denial.
  • Attach the new evidence, not the same file SSA already denied: new treatment records, an RFC form from your treating doctor, third-party statements.

How an SSDI appeal actually works

Appeal, do not reapply

The most common and most expensive mistake after a denial is starting over with a fresh application. A new application gets a new filing date, which can cost you months of back pay, and sends you back to the start of the same queue with the same file. An appeal keeps your original filing date and moves your existing claim up the ladder, where a new decision-maker looks at it.

Reconsideration is a paper review, not the place cases are won

A different reviewer at your state Disability Determination Services office, someone who had no role in the original decision, looks at your entire file plus any new evidence. There is no hearing and no live testimony. Expect a wait of three to seven months, and published figures put approval somewhere around 10 to 15 percent. For most people, reconsideration is a required step on the way to the hearing.

The hearing is where denials reverse

If reconsideration denies you, a judge who had no part in the earlier decisions hears the case in person, by video, or by phone. Advocates commonly cite approval rates near 45 to 55 percent at the hearing stage. The judge sees you, hears your testimony, and weighs your treating doctor's opinion directly. The wait is long, roughly 286 days on average, and all written evidence must reach the judge no later than five business days before the hearing.

SSA decides on function, not diagnosis

Two people with the same diagnosis get different answers because one can still work a desk job and the other cannot sit for an hour. Your evidence has to prove what you cannot do, in concrete terms, day after day. A diagnosis alone almost never wins an appeal. The RFC form from your treating doctor, stating your limits in work terms, is the single most useful document in most appeals.

New evidence is the whole strategy

A reconsideration that submits the same file SSA already denied will get the same answer. Quote the decision back, name the specific new evidence, and tie both to a date. New treatment records, a new diagnosis, a new specialist, or a treating doctor's statement that was not in the original file are what give the new reviewer something different to decide on.

Keep treating, keep the diary, keep the record straight

Gaps in treatment are read as proof you got better. If you cannot afford care, say so in writing and keep every free-clinic, ER, or urgent-care visit on the record. A dated notebook of symptoms, sleep, medication side effects, and what you could and could not do that day turns vague complaints into a dated record. Describe your limits the same way to SSA, to your doctors, and in your diary; inconsistencies are the fastest way to lose credibility.

Common questions

How long does a Social Security disability appeal take?
Reconsideration usually takes three to seven months. If you go to an ALJ hearing, expect another eight to fourteen months or more after you request it, depending on your hearing office backlog. The full path from denial to a hearing decision commonly runs well over a year, which is why filing the appeal immediately matters more than filing it perfectly. This tool does not predict any timeline for your case.
What are my chances of winning a disability appeal?
Reconsideration approves roughly 10 to 15 percent of claims, so most people who win do so later. At the ALJ hearing stage, advocates commonly cite approval rates near 45 to 55 percent. Your odds depend on your medical evidence, not on how forcefully you argue, so the work that moves the number is new evidence and a clear record of your functional limits. This tool does not assess your chances or predict any outcome.
Do I need a lawyer to appeal an SSDI denial?
No, you can appeal on your own at every level, and many people do. A disability representative or attorney usually works on contingency, taking a percentage of past-due benefits only if you win, with the fee capped by SSA rules, so there is generally no upfront cost. Representation tends to help most at the hearing stage, where cross-examining a vocational expert and framing the medical record matter. If your case is straightforward and your records are strong, self-representation at reconsideration is reasonable.
Can I work while my disability appeal is pending?
Working while you appeal is risky. Earning above substantial gainful activity levels signals to SSA that you can work, which undercuts the claim that you cannot. Short-term or very part-time work below those levels does not automatically kill a claim, but any work activity will be examined. Talk to a representative before taking a job during an appeal, and keep records of any work attempt, including hours, duties, and why it did or did not last.
What happens if I miss the 60-day appeal deadline?
If you miss the 60-day window without a good reason, the denial becomes final and you lose the right to appeal that claim. SSA may accept a late appeal if you can show good cause, such as a serious illness, a death in the family, or never receiving the notice. Without good cause, your only option is filing a brand-new application, which usually means a new filing date and lost back pay for the months in between. If you are already late, file anyway and explain the reason in writing. The deadline panel above works the date out from your denial letter.
What is the difference between SSDI and SSI when appealing?
SSDI is based on your work history and the Social Security taxes you paid; SSI is needs-based, for people with limited income and resources. The appeal process is the same for both: reconsideration, ALJ hearing, Appeals Council, and federal court, each with a 60-day deadline. The difference shows up in the denial reason. A technical denial for SSDI often means not enough work credits, while for SSI it often means income or resources above the limit. Read your letter to see which kind of denial you got, and pick that reason in the form above.
What evidence helps most in a disability appeal?
The evidence that wins appeals describes what you cannot do, not just what diagnosis you have. Strongest items: treatment records showing ongoing care, a residual functional capacity (RFC) form completed by your treating doctor, your own function report describing a typical day in concrete terms, statements from people who see your limits daily, and a record of failed work attempts. A new diagnosis alone rarely changes a decision; new proof of functional limits does. The evidence prep mode above drafts the function statement from your facts.
