Habits, not a password

A strength checker can look at one password. It cannot see that the same password also opens your email, or that the backup codes for that email are sitting in the inbox. Those are habits. This quiz asks about the habits and then stops. It does not want the password, a username, or the name of a bank.

Reuse is the habit that turns one breach into many. Attackers try a leaked password on mail first, because mail can reset the other accounts. A different password on each account limits that. Length is the other half: a word plus the year is an early guess, and a capital letter with a symbol on the end does not change the pattern. Sixteen characters, or four unrelated words, is a practical target when something else remembers them.

The account that resets the others

The email that receives password resets is the floor under everything else. It needs its own password and a second step that does not arrive in that same inbox. An authenticator app, a passkey, or a hardware key still works when someone only has the password. A text message is better than nothing, and a SIM swap can redirect it. Backup codes are the spare key for a lost phone. They belong in a password manager or on paper, not in the camera roll and not in the inbox they protect.

Security questions are a second password that is often already public. A first pet or a street name can be guessed from records and family posts. Nonsense stored in the manager, or a second factor instead of the question, closes that door. Sharing a mail or bank password by text leaves a copy in a chat thread. A streaming login shared in a household is a smaller risk, as long as that password is not also the email password.

When a password should change

Change a password when there is a reason: a breach notice, another person learned it, or you typed it into a page you reached from a message. NIST 800-63B tells organizations not to require a change every 30, 60, or 90 days, because the usual result is a predictable edit, such as a new digit on the end. A breach you ignore leaves the stolen copy working. Change the named account, then change every other account that used the same password.

FAQ

Does this ask for my password?

No. Every question is multiple choice about a habit, such as whether accounts share a password or where backup codes are kept. There is no field for a password, and an answer that is not one of the choices is ignored.

Does this upload my answers?

No. The quiz runs in your browser and does not send the answers anywhere. The only thing saved is the last completed result, as choice ids in this browser's local storage. Clear removes that saved result.

What does the score mean?

Each question is worth up to 3 points. The total is out of 36, and the tier is Steady vault, Solid habits, A few open doors, Worth a weekend, or Start here. The score describes the habits you reported. It is not a test of any real password and it is not a guarantee about your accounts.

How is the plan ordered?

Weaker habits that open more accounts come first: a reused password, the inbox that receives resets, and a missing second factor outrank a short password or a scheduled change. The first three are the ones to do before the rest. Each action has a why and a how. If a habit is already strong, it stays off the fix list.

Where does the last result live, and what does Clear do?

Only in this browser. Finish a quiz and the result replaces whatever was saved. Clear deletes that saved result from local storage. Clearing site data for cyberscryb.com removes it too. The quiz does not ask for an account.

Does a high score mean my accounts are safe?

No. These are general habits. A strong routine still loses to a fake login page, and a real account can have a setting this quiz does not see. When a password is involved, open the service from a bookmark you already trust.
