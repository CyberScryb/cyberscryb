What Are the Navier-Stokes Equations?

The Navier-Stokes equations are a set of partial differential equations that describe the motion of viscous fluid substances. First developed by Claude-Louis Navier and George Gabriel Stokes in the 19th century, these equations form the foundation of computational fluid dynamics (CFD) — one of the most important fields in modern engineering and physics. They remain one of the seven Millennium Prize Problems in mathematics. In plain terms, the equations track two things at every point in a fluid: how fast it is moving, and how pressure pushes it around. Solve those two quantities over and over, fast enough, and you get smoke curling, water swirling, and dye blooming exactly the way real fluids behave.

How to Use the Simulator

No setup, no account, no install. The simulation starts the moment the page loads. Follow these steps to get the most out of it:

  1. Click and drag on the canvas. Your pointer injects dye and force into the fluid. The direction and speed of your drag set the direction and strength of the flow.
  2. Try short flicks for vortices. Quick, tight circles spin up small whirlpools that drift and merge. Long, slow sweeps create broad rolling waves.
  3. Pick a palette. Cyber, Fire, Plasma, Ocean, and Mono each inject different dye colors. Switching mid-drag blends palettes in the same swirl.
  4. Tune the physics. Lower viscosity for wild, long-lasting swirls. Raise it to calm the fluid down. Raise diffusion to soften edges and blend colors.
  5. Pause and save. Hit Pause when a pattern looks right, then use Save to download a 1024 by 1024 PNG of the frame.
  6. Clear and start over. The Clear button resets the grid instantly so you can try a new idea without reloading the page.

What Each Control Does

Every slider changes a real parameter of the solver, not a visual filter. Here is what each one means:

  • Viscosity (0 to 0.001). The fluid's internal friction. At zero, swirls spin almost forever. At the top of the range, the fluid thickens and motion dies out quickly, like stirring honey. Water sits near the low end; oil and syrup sit near the high end.
  • Diffusion (0 to 0.001). How fast dye spreads through the fluid. Low diffusion keeps sharp, crisp color boundaries. High diffusion blurs edges and mixes colors into soft gradients.
  • Force (50 to 500). How hard your drag pushes the fluid. Low force makes gentle currents; high force throws dye across the canvas and spins up tight vortices.
  • Brush Size (1 to 8). The radius of the area your pointer affects. A small brush paints fine detail and thin streams. A large brush stirs broad regions at once.
  • Color Palette. Five dye palettes: Cyber (red tones), Fire (orange to yellow), Plasma (magenta to cyan), Ocean (blue to teal), and Mono (white to gray). The two endpoints of each palette are blended randomly as dye is injected, so no two strokes look identical.
  • Pause, Clear, Save. Pause freezes the frame so you can study a pattern. Clear wipes the grid. Save exports the current frame as a PNG image you own outright.

How This Simulator Works

This tool implements Jos Stam's "Stable Fluids" algorithm (1999), widely used in VFX and game dev. Each frame performs four operations:

  1. Add Forces — Your mouse input is added to the velocity field
  2. Diffuse — Viscosity equalizes neighboring velocities
  3. Advect — The velocity field moves itself and dye through back-tracing
  4. Project — Pressure solve ensures divergence-free (mass-conserving) flow

The simulation runs on a 128 by 128 grid with Gauss-Seidel relaxation. RGB dye channels are tracked independently, allowing natural color mixing. Runs at 60+ FPS entirely client-side. The "stable" in Stable Fluids matters: older solvers blew up numerically when forces got large, producing NaN values and a frozen screen. Stam's semi-Lagrangian advection keeps the simulation stable no matter how hard you drag, which is why you can fling the fluid around at full force without breaking it.

  • VFX and motion reference. Animators and game developers use fluid sims to study how smoke curls, how ink blooms in water, and how fire rolls before they build the same effect in their own tools.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is this a real physics simulation?

Yes. It solves the Navier-Stokes equations for incompressible flow each frame. While simplified to 2D, the underlying mathematics are identical to professional CFD software.

What do the viscosity and diffusion controls actually change?

Viscosity controls the fluid's internal friction. At zero viscosity, swirls persist indefinitely. At high viscosity, the fluid behaves like honey. Diffusion controls how quickly the dye spreads through the fluid.

How do I make the most dramatic swirls?

Set viscosity and diffusion near zero, pick a high-contrast palette like Fire or Plasma, raise the Force slider, and drag in fast, tight circles. Short, quick flicks create small vortices; long sweeping drags create large rolling waves. Pause the simulation when a pattern looks right, then save it as a screenshot.

Why does the simulation slow down on my device?

The solver runs a 128 by 128 grid with a pressure solve every frame, which is heavy work for low-end phones and older laptops. If the frame rate drops, clear the canvas, lower the brush size, and close other tabs. The FPS counter in the top-left corner of the canvas shows your current frame rate.

Is my data sent anywhere?

No. Everything runs 100% in your browser. Screenshots are generated locally via the Canvas API.

Can I use screenshots commercially?

Yes. Any images you export are yours to use however you wish.

What is the difference between viscosity and diffusion?

Viscosity affects motion: it damps velocity, so swirls decay faster. Diffusion affects color: it spreads dye from high-concentration areas to low-concentration ones, blurring sharp edges. A fluid can be thick and slow-moving but still hold crisp color boundaries if diffusion is low, or thin and fast with colors that blend quickly if diffusion is high.

Can I use this simulator on my phone or tablet?

Yes. Touch and drag works the same as a mouse. On small screens the controls stack below the canvas. Performance depends on your device; modern phones typically hold a smooth frame rate at the default settings.

How is this different from professional CFD software?

Professional CFD tools solve the same equations in 3D, on grids with millions of cells, with precise boundary conditions, turbulence models, and quantitative output for engineering decisions. This simulator is a 2D teaching and visualization tool on a 128 by 128 grid. It shows the same physics qualitatively, but it is not a substitute for engineering-grade simulation.
