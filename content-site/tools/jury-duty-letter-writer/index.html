Ask for the Postponement, Not the Pardon

Here is the fact that changes the whole letter: courts deny most excusal requests but approve most deferral requests. A deferral says "I cannot serve on March 12, but I can serve in June." That is easy for a clerk to grant. An excusal says "excuse me from jury duty entirely," which requires a much stronger showing. If your situation is temporary, ask for the thing courts say yes to.

The second fact: documentation is the whole game. Courts see hundreds of hardship letters, and the difference between the ones granted and the ones denied is almost never the wording. It is the attachment. A doctor's note for a medical claim, an employer letter for a work claim, enrollment records for a student claim. This tool lists your supporting documents in the letter itself, so the clerk sees the proof is real and attached.

Then send it early and keep a copy. File two to four weeks before your summons date if you can, and keep a copy of everything you sent. If the court denies the request, show up anyway. A denial letter plus a no-show becomes a much bigger problem than a denial alone.

Real Ways People Use It

Medical recovery: Scheduled surgery, ongoing treatment, or a doctor's order to avoid sitting for long periods. The doctor's note carries the request; the letter just delivers it formally.

Sole-income financial hardship: Hourly and gig workers for whom two weeks of unpaid service means missing rent. Pay stubs plus a plain statement of the math.

Caregiving with no backup: A parent of a special-needs child or a caregiver for an aging relative, where no alternate care exists. Documentation from the care provider or medical records support it.

Exam-season students and critical work dates: Temporary conflicts, exactly what deferral was designed for. Name a date after the conflict ends.

Frequently Asked Questions

Should I ask for a deferral or an excusal?

Most people should ask for a deferral to a specific future date. Courts grant deferrals routinely; full excusal requires a much stronger showing and is mostly approved for permanent medical conditions or similar long-term situations.

What reasons do courts actually accept?

Documented medical conditions, caregiving with no backup, real financial hardship, exam-season student conflicts, and critical work obligations. In every category, documentation is what separates granted requests from denied ones.

What happens if I just don't show up?

You risk fines, contempt of court, or a warrant depending on the jurisdiction. Filing a request before your summons date is always safer than a no-show.

How far ahead should I file?

As soon as you know you cannot serve, ideally two to four weeks before the summons date. Filing early also gives you time to refile or appear if the request is denied.

Does my employer have to give me time off for jury duty?

Federal law and most state laws require time off and forbid firing you for jury service, but paid time off is not always required. If lost wages are the hardship, say so plainly and attach pay stubs.

Is this tool legal advice?

No. It writes a formal letter from the facts you provide. The court decides, and rules vary by jurisdiction. If your situation is complicated, talk to a local attorney.
