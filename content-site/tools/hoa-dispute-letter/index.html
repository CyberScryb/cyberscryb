Key Homeowner Due Process Protections

1. Mandatory Notice & Cure Period

State statutes prohibit boards from levying fines on a first notice. HOAs must grant a statutory opportunity to cure (usually 10 to 30 days) and specify the exact covenant alleged to be breached.

2. Right to an Impartial Hearing

Under California Davis-Stirling § 5855, Florida FS § 720.305, and Texas Prop Code § 209.006, homeowners have a statutory right to present evidence and testimony before fines become enforceable.

3. Tolling of Fines Pending Review

Timely submission of this formal written dispute letter pauses (tolls) further fines, interest, or collection referrals until the Board reviews your submission and renders a formal ruling.

How to Dispute an HOA Violation in 5 Steps

1. Read the notice and mark the deadline

Note the violation cited, the rule or CC&R section, the fine amount, and the response deadline. The deadline usually runs 10 to 30 days from the notice, and it is set by your governing documents and your state law.

2. Pull your governing documents

Find the rule cited, the fine schedule, and the dispute and hearing procedure in your CC&Rs and bylaws. Check whether the fine amount matches the published schedule, and whether the board followed its own notice and cure steps.

3. Gather your evidence

Collect dated photos of your property, photos of identical conditions on other lots, ARC approval letters, contractor receipts, and any emails with the board or management company. Evidence beats argument at a hearing.

4. Generate and send the letter

Fill in your HOA, lot, and notice details above, pick the defense that fits your case, and export the letter. Send it by certified mail with return receipt requested, and keep a copy plus the receipt.

5. Prepare for the hearing

Bring printed copies of your letter, your photos, and your receipts. Ask for the HOA's evidence in advance if your state gives you that right, and get the board's decision in writing afterward.

When This Letter Fits

Lawn and landscaping

Cited for weeds, dead grass, or overgrowth after freeze damage or while a landscaper was already booked to fix it.

Trash bins and recycling

Bins left out past pickup day, or stored where the HOA says they should not be, while neighbors do the same without a citation.

Paint, siding, and fences

Cited for fading paint or a fence repair the ARC already approved in writing, or that matches the original build.

Parking and vehicles

A work truck, trailer, or guest car the HOA says violates a parking rule, or a rule that is not applied to other lots.

Work done without ARC approval

A deck, shed, or modification the ARC actually approved, or work that predates the rule the HOA is now citing.

Signs and holiday decor

A sign or decoration cited under a rule the HOA does not enforce on other homes in the community.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can an HOA fine you without a hearing?

In most states, no. State law usually requires the HOA to give you written notice of the violation and a chance to be heard before a fine becomes final. California requires at least 10 days' written notice and a hearing before the board, Florida requires a hearing before a fining committee, and Texas requires certified mail notice plus a chance to request a hearing. Check your state statute and your CC&Rs, because the exact procedure varies.

How long do I have to respond to an HOA violation notice?

There is no single national deadline. It comes from your governing documents and your state law, and it is commonly 10 to 30 days from the notice. Texas gives owners 30 days to request a hearing after notice is mailed. Arizona guidance calls for a written response by certified mail within 21 calendar days of receiving a property condition notice. Read your notice the day it arrives, mark the earliest deadline on a calendar, and send your dispute before it passes. A missed deadline is the most common way owners lose a winnable dispute.

Does requesting a hearing stop fines from accumulating?

In many associations, yes. A timely written dispute and hearing request pauses, or tolls, further fines and enforcement while the board reviews your case. Tolling is not automatic everywhere, so your letter should state plainly that you dispute the violation and request a hearing, and you should keep proof of when you sent it.

What is the selective enforcement defense against an HOA?

Selective enforcement means the HOA fines you for a condition it ignores on other homes. If neighbors have the same trash bins, paint color, or parking setup without a citation, the board is not enforcing the rule evenly. Dated photos of identical conditions on other lots are the core evidence for this defense. Courts and hearing committees treat uneven enforcement as a breach of the board's duty to enforce rules fairly.

What if I already fixed the violation?

Say so in writing and ask for a re-inspection. Many notices cover a condition that was already cured by the time the letter arrived, such as a lawn repaired after freeze damage or bins moved back inside. Send dated photos showing the fix, keep contractor receipts, and ask the HOA to confirm in writing that the violation is closed and no fine applies. In several states, curing within the cure period means no fine can be assessed at all.

Should I send an HOA dispute letter by certified mail?

Yes. USPS Certified Mail with Return Receipt Requested gives you a timestamped proof of delivery. That receipt proves you responded inside the deadline if the board later claims it never got your letter. Keep the receipt and the tracking record with your copy of the letter.

What happens if the board ignores my dispute letter?

Your certified mail receipt becomes your record. If the board fines you or records a lien without giving you the hearing you requested, it has skipped a required step, which can make the fine void and expose the association to a claim for your legal fees. Follow up in writing, restate the request, and keep copies of everything. If the board still refuses, your next step is usually internal dispute resolution or mediation under your state law.

Should I attend the HOA hearing in person?

Yes. Attend in person or by video if the board offers it. Bring two printed copies of your dispute letter, dated photos, contractor receipts, and your certified mail receipt. State your position in one or two sentences, stick to facts, and stay calm. The hearing also creates the record you will need if you appeal or take the dispute further.

Can an HOA put a lien on my home over unpaid fines?

In many states, yes, unpaid fines and assessments can become a lien on your home. But several states limit what the HOA can do with a fine-only lien. California bars foreclosure on a lien made up only of fines, and Texas bans foreclosing on fine-only liens. The rules depend on your state, so check your statute before assuming the worst, and do not let a fine sit unanswered while interest and collection costs pile up.

What can I do if the board upholds the fine?

You can appeal through the process in your governing documents, request internal dispute resolution or mediation, and in some states ask a state ombudsman for an advisory opinion. California owners can request internal dispute resolution under Civil Code sections 5900 through 5920 before suing. If the fine is small, compare the cost of fighting it against paying it, but get the board's written decision first. You need that decision to appeal.
