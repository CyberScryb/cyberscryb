How eviction responses actually work

A notice is not an eviction

Only a court can evict you. A notice is the landlord's demand that starts the process. Do not move out before you understand the deadline: leaving early can surrender defenses you did not know you had, and it does not erase rent you owe.

Pay-or-quit ends with payment

If the notice is pay-or-quit and you can pay the full amount within the window, pay it by a traceable method and keep the receipt. A satisfied pay-or-quit generally ends that notice. Your written response should state that you paid, when, and how.

Cure-or-quit ends with proof

If the violation is fixable, fix it fast and document it: photos, dates, and a short written response stating what was cured and when. The fix without the letter is invisible; the letter without the fix is empty.

Retaliation needs dates

A retaliation defense turns on sequence: repair requests or complaints first, notice second. Dates, copies of the requests, and the landlord's replies are the defense. A claim without dates is just an accusation.

Everything in writing

Phone calls get logged, not relied on. Every substantive statement goes in writing, and every mailing goes certified with return receipt. The written response plus the mail log is the record a judge or a legal aid attorney reads.

Keep the case file

The notice, your response, receipts, repair requests, and the mail log, in one file. If the dispute ends in a payment plan, a settlement, or a court date, the file is what decides it. The tenant case companion runs the same file across a longer dispute.

Common questions

Do I have to move out when I get an eviction notice?
Usually not. An eviction notice is a landlord's demand, not a court order. Only a judge can order you out. Read the notice to find what it asks for and by when, respond in writing before the deadline, and do not move out before you understand the deadline.
What is a pay or quit notice?
A pay-or-quit notice tells you to pay the rent owed within the window printed on the notice, often 3 to 5 days, or vacate. If you pay the full amount within the window, the notice is generally satisfied. Keep the receipt and respond in writing stating that you paid.
What is a cure or quit notice?
A cure-or-quit notice tells you to fix a lease violation, such as an unauthorized pet or noise, within the window printed on the notice or leave. If you can fix the violation, do it fast, document it with photos and dates, and respond in writing stating what was cured and when.
Can my landlord evict me for asking for repairs?
Retaliation for exercising a legal right, such as requesting repairs or reporting a code violation, is illegal in most states, but the protection usually covers a limited window after the protected act and does not excuse unpaid rent. A retaliation defense needs dates: when you requested repairs, what the landlord did, and when the notice arrived. Put those dates in your written response.
How do I calculate my response deadline?
Take the date the notice was served and add the number of days printed on the notice. Enter both above and the deadline panel shows the deadline date and how many days are left. The notice itself states the deadline; never guess it from a general rule.
How should I send my response letter?
In writing, by a method you can prove. Email plus a mailed copy is a common pair. If you mail it, send it certified with return receipt requested, keep the receipt and the tracking number, and log the mailing date in the log below. Keep a copy of everything.
What happens if the landlord files in court anyway?
A court filing starts a case, not an eviction. Appear at every hearing with your case file: the notice, your written response, payment receipts, repair requests, and the certified-mail log. Missing a court date is the fastest way to lose by default.
Can my landlord change the locks or shut off utilities?
In most places, no: lockouts, utility shutoffs, and removing your belongings without a court order are illegal self-help evictions in most states. If it happens, document it immediately and contact a tenants' rights organization or attorney. Do not break back in yourself.
