Would You Click It?

Sixteen fictional things you might meet while browsing or using an app. A popup, a download, an ad, a permission, a code on a poster. Tap Click or Skip, then read what gave it away. The pictures are drawings. Nothing in them can be opened.

  1. Look past the button The loudest control is often the one that wants the wrong thing.
  2. Check who is asking A recipe page does not scan a computer, and a notes app does not need your mail.
  3. Leave the trap Skip is a complete answer. Some prompts are ordinary, and those are fine to click.

The things a page puts in front of you

This round is about the web and the apps you already have open. It is not an inbox, and it is not a phone call. The trap is a surface: a box over a recipe, a green button on a file page, a sponsored card, a consent screen, a sticker on a pay station. The useful habit is the same each time. Read who is asking, and how much they want, before you press the loud control.

The companies here are fictional. Harborline, Cedar Files, Maple, Rivermark, Lumen Notes, Northwind, Herald, Brightpath, and Pine Maps are not real services. The addresses use .example, which is reserved for examples and does not point at a website. The code on the poster is a drawing, not a code a camera can follow. The line in the fake human-check is the words echo sample-only. It is not a command to run, and a real page that asks you to paste one is the same kind of trap.

A loud button is not the file

Download pages rent their biggest button to something else. The file you came for is often a smaller line with a name and a size. A scare screen that says a computer is infected is the same idea with a clock on it: a webpage cannot scan the machine, and a phone number in the box is the ask. A prize banner and a gift-card ad work by being too generous. You did not win a phone for opening an article, and a feed does not select you for a cash card.

Permissions, stickers, and a window inside the page

An account prompt can be on the real account address and still ask for too much. A notes app needs notes. It does not need your mailbox, your contacts, or the right to send mail. A map that asks for your place while it is open is a narrower question, and that difference is the point. A notification box drawn inside an article is not the browser's own prompt, which sits on the address bar. A code taped over a pay station can cover the real instructions. A login window drawn inside a shop is still the shop. The address that counts is the browser's own bar.

A cookie banner can hide Reject and pre-check a sale of your activity. That is a design trick, and the easy button is the one to leave. A browser update comes from the browser's own menu, or from Settings you opened yourself. An article does not deliver it. A chat bubble on a recipe cannot see a problem on your computer, and remote help would hand the screen over.

The ClickFix pattern dresses up as a human check, then tells you to open a run box and paste a line. A real check stays on the page: a box, or a set of pictures. Close a page that asks you to paste anything into a run box.

FAQ

Does this upload my answers?

No. The quiz runs in your browser. The only thing saved is your best score, in this browser's local storage. Individual answers are not stored and are not sent anywhere. You can confirm that in the network panel: choosing Click or Skip adds no request.

Do the pictures go anywhere?

No. Every popup, ad, code, and button is a drawing. There is no link to open, no file to download, and no command that runs. The only controls that do anything are Click, Skip, the timer, Copy result, and Play again.

Are the companies real?

No. Harborline, Cedar, Maple, Rivermark, Lumen Notes, Coblog, Northwind, Herald, Kit, Brightpath, Reelbox, Lark, and Pine Maps are fictional. Addresses use the .example domain, which is reserved for examples.

Does the timer run for everyone?

No. The timer is off until you turn it on with a pointer. It stays off when the browser asks for less motion. It also stays off for a screen reader and for keyboard use, so a prompt does not change while you are reading it. If time runs out, that prompt counts as a skip.

Does a perfect score mean I will catch a real trap?

No. This is practice on a fixed set of drawings. A real page can lie without these patterns, and a real update or login can be ordinary. When a password, a download, or a permission is involved, check the browser's own address and how narrow the ask is.

Where does the best score live, and can I play again?

Play again shuffles the same fictional prompts. Your best score stays in this browser only. Clearing site data for cyberscryb.com removes it. The round itself is forgotten when you leave or shuffle.
