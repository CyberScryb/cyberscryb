Two Minutes of Attention You Cannot Afford to Waste

Somebody hands you the spotlight at a party and says "say a few words." That is the whole job of a toast: a few words, delivered well, that make the person next to you feel something true. The fear is not the speaking — it is standing up with nothing worth saying.

This tool builds the toast around your person: who they are, what you admire, the detail that makes it real. It opens with who you are, lands one specific story or observation, and closes with a line the whole room can drink to. Short sentences, room to breathe, an actual ending — the three things most improvised toasts are missing.

Read it aloud once, standing, phone in hand or printed on an index card. Then when the moment comes, you are not improvising. You are delivering.

How It Works

Step 1: Name the occasion. Birthday, rehearsal dinner, retirement, anniversary, New Year, graduation — each one asks for a different register.

Step 2: Describe the honoree. Your relationship, what you admire, one real detail or story. Specifics make it sound like yours.

Step 3: Set tone and length. Funny at a birthday, dignified at a retirement, heartfelt at an anniversary — and short enough that the room wants more.

Step 4: Rehearse once and deliver. Read it aloud standing up. End with the actual toast — "To Maria" — and lift your glass.

Real Ways People Use It

Birthdays: A milestone birthday toast that is warm without being a roast — one funny memory, one sincere truth.

Wedding rehearsals: The night-before toast for the couple — shorter and looser than the wedding speech, just as heartfelt.

Retirements: A dignified send-off for a colleague: the career, the character, and a genuine wish for what is next.

Anniversaries, New Year, graduation: The couple's story, the year's close, the graduate's next chapter — each with a closing line the room can drink to.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long should a toast be?

One to three minutes, roughly 150 to 400 words. Two minutes of sincerity beats five minutes of rambling.

What is the structure of a good toast?

Opening (who you are and why you are raising the glass), one specific story or detail, and a closing line the room can drink to — "To Sarah."

What should you avoid in a toast?

Inside jokes the room will not get, exes, anything the grandmother could not hear, roasting the honoree, and apologizing for your speaking.

Can a toast be funny?

Yes — one or two affectionate laughs are ideal. Humor with the honoree, never at them; self-deprecation is the safest laugh.
