The Adjective Trap

Most recommendation letters say nothing. "Hardworking team player with excellent communication skills" could describe anyone, so it recommends no one. The letters that actually move a hiring manager or admissions officer are built from evidence: the project they led, the number they moved, the Friday-night outage they fixed.

That is what the guided inputs are for. You feed in the real stuff — achievements, stories, results — and the letter is built around proof instead of adjectives. It opens with who you are and how you know them, puts two or three specific pieces of evidence in the middle, and closes with a clear, unambiguous recommendation.

One honest rule: only put in facts that are true. The letter goes out under your name, and your credibility is what makes it worth reading. A specific, truthful letter beats a glowing, vague one every single time.

How It Works

Step 1: Name the candidate and yourself. "Sofia Rivera, his engineering manager of 3 years" — the relationship is what gives the letter weight.

Step 2: Drop in the evidence. Achievements, results, stories. Concrete beats impressive-sounding every time — fragments are fine.

Step 3: Pick the letter type and length. Job and academic letters run about a page; LinkedIn recommendations are shorter and warmer.

Step 4: Review, adjust, sign. Read it once for accuracy — every fact should be yours — then send it out under your name.

Real Ways People Use It

Job references: A polished one-page letter from a manager or colleague for a job application.

Academic applications: A professor's recommendation for grad school, scholarships, or programs — drafted by the student, signed by the professor.

LinkedIn recommendations: Short, warm, platform-native recommendations that actually get read.

Volunteer and internship references: Letters for people early in their careers who need credible evidence of their work.
