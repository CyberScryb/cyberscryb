What actually makes workplace accommodation requests succeed

Lead with functional limitations, not raw chart notes

HR and supervisors do not need your full clinical history to evaluate an adjustment. They need to know the functional limitation (for example, photophobia triggered by overhead fluorescent glare or a 20-pound lifting restriction) and how your proposed change removes the workplace barrier so you can perform your essential duties.

Always bring an Option A and an Option B

Under EEOC Enforcement Guidance, an employer may choose among effective accommodations. Offering two concrete, workable options (Option A as your primary request and Option B as a practical fallback) keeps you in the driver's seat of the interactive process if HR rejects your first preference.

Propose a 60- or 90-day trial period

When a manager worries an adjustment might disrupt team workflow, a time-boxed 60-day trial with a scheduled review date lowers the stakes. If the trial works, you have objective proof from your own deliverables that the accommodation caused zero hardship.

Send a written recap after every verbal meeting

The interactive process often happens in hallway chats or Zoom calls. After every conversation, send a polite five-line email summarizing what was discussed, what alternative options were offered, and who owes the next step by what date.

FAQ

Do I have to use the words "ADA" or "reasonable accommodation" in my request?

No. Under EEOC Enforcement Guidance (Question 1), you only need to let your employer know in plain English that you need an adjustment or change at work for a reason related to a medical condition. However, stating clearly in writing that you are requesting a workplace accommodation creates an unmistakable date-stamped record.

Do I have to disclose my full medical diagnosis or medical records to my manager?

Generally no. Employers are entitled to know that you have a covered condition and how the resulting functional limitation affects your job duties, not your entire clinical history. Route medical documentation directly to HR or the designated leave/disability administrator, where it must be kept in a confidential medical file separate from your personnel file under 29 C.F.R. § 1630.14(c)(1).

Why should I propose both an Option A and an Option B accommodation?

Under ADA Title I, an employer does not have to grant your exact preferred accommodation if another effective accommodation removes the workplace barrier. Offering a primary Option A and a practical Option B keeps you in control of the alternatives and demonstrates good-faith participation in the interactive process.

What should my doctor include in an accommodation support letter?

Ask your clinician to focus on four specifics: (1) confirmation of an underlying medical impairment, (2) the specific functional limitation it causes, (3) which job duty or workplace condition triggers that limitation, and (4) why the requested adjustment enables you to perform your essential job functions, along with the expected duration. Use the Doctor documentation sheet mode above to print a one-page summary for your visit.

What if my employer says my requested accommodation is an undue hardship or against policy?

Modifying a neutral workplace policy is often the very definition of a reasonable accommodation, and undue hardship requires significant difficulty or expense relative to the employer's overall resources. If your primary option is declined, ask in writing what alternative effective accommodation HR proposes and offer a 60- or 90-day trial period with objective review metrics.

How is an ADA workplace accommodation different from FMLA leave?

FMLA provides up to 12 weeks of job-protected leave for eligible employees at 50+ employee worksites after 12 months and 1,250 hours of service. ADA Title I covers employers with 15+ employees from day one and focuses on workplace adjustments—or targeted leave when effective—that allow you to perform essential job functions.
