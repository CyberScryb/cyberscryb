What actually matters

Answer before the deadline

The deadline to answer is printed on your summons, and it is the only date that matters first. Miss it and the collector can win by default without ever proving the debt. File the answer with the court, send a copy to the collector or its lawyer, and keep proof of both. The countdown box above works from the date you enter, so copy it straight off the summons.

The collector has to prove it

A debt collection lawsuit is the collector's case to prove, not yours to disprove. It has to show the debt is yours, the amount is correct, and it has the right to collect it. That is why the strongest defenses are usually the simplest: wrong person, wrong amount, no proof of ownership, or a time limit that has run out. Your job is to make the collector show its work.

Records beat memory

“I think I paid that” persuades no one. Statements that show the account, payment records with dates, a settlement letter, your written validation request and the reply or the silence that followed: those decide hearings. Gather every record before you file anything, and send new evidence to the court the way your court requires, not by guessing.

Never miss the court date

A no-show can end the case against you, and getting a new hearing takes months. If the date truly does not work, contact the court clerk before the hearing, not after, and ask how to request a change. Treat the court date like the court date it is, and keep every letter and record organized while you wait.

FAQ

What happens if I ignore a debt collection lawsuit?

If you do not answer the lawsuit by the deadline printed on your summons, the collector can ask the court for a default judgment, which is a ruling in the collector's favor without a hearing. After a judgment, the collector can ask the court for collection tools such as wage garnishment or a bank levy, depending on your state. Ignoring the lawsuit never makes the debt go away. Answering on time keeps your defenses alive. This is general information, not legal advice.

How do I answer a debt collection summons?

You file a written answer with the court before the deadline printed on your summons. The answer responds to each numbered claim in the complaint, usually admitting, denying, or saying you do not have enough information to answer, and states your defenses, such as mistaken identity, wrong amount, or an expired time limit to sue. Send a copy to the collector or its lawyer, keep proof of everything you file and send, and keep a copy for yourself. The prep packet mode drafts your answer checklist and defenses from your facts.

What is the statute of limitations on debt?

The statute of limitations is the time limit for a collector to sue over a debt. It varies by state and by the type of debt, and the clock usually runs from the last payment or the last activity on the account. If the time limit has run out, that can be a defense, but you still have to raise it in your answer; the court does not raise it for you. Never admit the debt is yours or make a payment without checking the time limit first, because a payment can restart the clock in some states. This is general information, not legal advice.

What if the debt is not mine?

Mistaken identity is one of the most common defenses in debt collection lawsuits: wrong person, same name, a debt that belonged to a family member, or an account that was never yours. Ask the collector for validation of the debt in writing, gather every record that shows the account is not yours, and state the defense clearly in your answer. Keep copies of everything you send and receive. Do not agree the debt is yours in court or in writing if it is not. This is general information, not legal advice.

What is a default judgment?

A default judgment is what the court enters when the person sued does not answer the lawsuit by the deadline. The collector wins without having to prove the debt at a hearing, and the judgment then lets the collector ask the court for wage garnishment, a bank levy, or a lien, depending on your state. Filing an answer on time is what stops a default judgment. If one was already entered, ask a legal aid organization or a consumer attorney right away about your options; some courts allow a late challenge in limited situations. This is general information, not legal advice.

What should I bring to a debt collection hearing?

Bring the summons and complaint, a copy of the answer you filed, your statements and payment records for the account, any settlement or payment letters, your written validation request and the collector's reply, and a dated timeline of the debt from your own records. Bring copies for the judge and the collector's lawyer, and keep your originals. Organize it in the order the judge will ask about it: the debt, the amount, the payments, and your defense. The prep packet turns your evidence list into a checklist.

Can a debt collector garnish my wages or bank account?

In most cases a collector cannot garnish wages or levy a bank account until it has a court judgment against you. After a judgment, the collector can ask the court for garnishment or a levy, and the rules, limits, and protected income vary by state. Some income, such as Social Security in many states, is protected, but you usually have to claim the protection. Filing an answer and showing up are what keep a judgment from being entered by default. This is general information, not legal advice.
