How to Calculate BMI

BMI is your weight divided by your height squared. In metric: BMI = weight (kg) / height (m)2. In imperial: BMI = (weight (lb) / height (in)2) × 703.

Worked example: someone 5'9" (69 inches) weighing 160 lb. Square the height: 69 × 69 = 4,761. Divide weight by that: 160 / 4,761 = 0.0336. Multiply by 703: 23.6, squarely in the healthy range. The calculator above does this instantly and shows where you land on the scale.

What Your BMI Means

The World Health Organization categories for adults: under 18.5 is underweight, 18.5 to 24.9 is the healthy range, 25 to 29.9 is overweight, and 30 or above is obese (30-34.9 class I, 35-39.9 class II, 40+ class III). These are screening cutoffs used across population health, not a diagnosis of any individual.

The calculator also shows your healthy weight range for your height: the weights that would put your BMI between 18.5 and 24.9. That range is often more useful than the single number, because it gives you a target zone instead of a pass/fail.

What BMI Gets Wrong

BMI cannot tell muscle from fat. A lifter with low body fat can score as overweight; an older adult with little muscle can score as healthy while carrying risky visceral fat. It also ignores where fat sits (belly fat carries more risk than hip fat), and the standard cutoffs were built mainly from European populations. Some health authorities use lower thresholds for Asian populations.

Children and teens use BMI percentiles, not fixed cutoffs. Pregnancy, elite athletic training, and significant muscle loss with age all make the standard categories misleading. Doctors treat BMI as a starting screen alongside waist circumference, blood work, and an actual conversation, never as a verdict on its own. Who Uses a BMI Calculator

People starting a fitness or weight-loss plan use it as a baseline to measure against later. Doctors and nurses record it at checkups as a quick screen. Life and health insurance applications often ask for it. Coaches use it with clients to set realistic targets, and curious searchers just want to know where they stand. Whatever brought you here, the number is a starting point for a conversation, ideally with a professional, not a final answer.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a good BMI?

The World Health Organization defines a healthy BMI for adults as 18.5 to 24.9. Below 18.5 is underweight, 25 to 29.9 is overweight, and 30 or above is obese. These cutoffs are a screening starting point, not a diagnosis.

How is BMI calculated?

BMI is your weight in kilograms divided by your height in meters squared. In imperial units, divide your weight in pounds by your height in inches squared, then multiply by 703. The calculator above does both automatically.

Is BMI accurate?

BMI is a useful screening tool but a rough one. It cannot tell muscle from fat, so athletes can score as overweight while being very lean. It also does not account for age, sex, bone density, or where fat is carried. Doctors use it as a starting point alongside other measures, never as a diagnosis on its own.

What BMI is considered obese?

A BMI of 30 or higher is classified as obese for adults, with 30-34.9 class I, 35-39.9 class II, and 40+ class III (severe obesity). These are population-level categories used for screening.

Does BMI work the same for everyone?

Not exactly. The standard cutoffs were developed mainly from European populations. Some health authorities use lower thresholds for Asian populations, and BMI is interpreted differently for children and teens (percentiles, not fixed cutoffs), pregnant people, the elderly, and very muscular individuals.
