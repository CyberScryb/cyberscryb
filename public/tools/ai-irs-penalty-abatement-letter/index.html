How IRS penalty abatement actually works

Check first-time abatement before you write anything

Starting with the 2026 filing season, the IRS automatically applies first-time abatement to failure-to-file, failure-to-pay, and failure-to-deposit penalties on qualifying 2025 and later returns. No request needed. If your penalty is for 2025 or later and your history is clean, check whether it was already removed before you spend time on a letter.

Reasonable cause is a story with dates and proof

A reasonable-cause request is not an apology. It is a sequence: what happened, the dates it happened, how it kept you from filing or paying on time, and what you did as soon as you could. The letter works when the facts line up with the dates and the proof is attached. Vague hardship with no dates gets a vague answer.

Form 843 is the form, the letter is the argument

Form 843, Claim for Refund and Request for Abatement, is the form for claiming a refund of a penalty already paid or requesting abatement of a penalty still owed. The form alone is a claim with blanks. The letter is what explains the facts and makes the case. Send them together.

A clean three-year history is the first-time test

First-time abatement turns on compliance history, generally no penalties in the prior three years, plus every required return filed and the tax paid or arranged to be paid. If you had a penalty in the last three years, first-time abatement does not fit and reasonable cause is the path. The check mode above walks through this from your facts.

The penalty is separate from the tax

Abatement targets the penalty, not the tax you owe. The underlying tax still has to be paid or arranged to be paid, and interest on the tax is a separate figure that this tool does not compute. The abatement math panel above works only on the penalty amount from your notice.

Everything in writing, everything kept

The notice, your letter, Form 843, proof of the cause, and every IRS reply, in one file. Send it certified mail with return receipt requested so you can prove when it arrived. If the request is denied, the denial notice prints the appeal window, and the file is what decides the appeal.

Common questions

Who qualifies for first-time penalty abatement?
First-time abatement is for a taxpayer with a clean compliance history, generally no penalties in the prior three years, who has filed every required return and paid or arranged to pay the tax owed. Starting with the 2026 filing season, the IRS automatically applies it to failure-to-file, failure-to-pay, and failure-to-deposit penalties on qualifying 2025 and later returns, with no request needed. You still need a written request if your penalty is for a tax year before 2025, if you do not have a clean three-year history, or if you are claiming reasonable cause instead. Run the first-time check mode above with your facts.
What counts as reasonable cause for IRS penalty abatement?
Reasonable cause means something outside your control kept you from filing or paying on time: a serious illness or injury, a death in the family, a natural disaster, a fire, an inability to obtain records, bad advice from a tax professional you relied on, or an IRS error. The letter has to state the facts with dates, explain how the event caused the late filing or payment, and attach whatever proof you have, such as medical records, a death certificate, or a letter from your tax professional.
How long does the IRS take to decide a penalty abatement request?
A phone request for first-time abatement can be approved on the spot. A written reasonable-cause request sent with Form 843 usually takes 6 to 12 weeks for an initial answer, and complex cases take longer. If the request is denied and you appeal, the appeal can take 6 to 12 months. This tool does not predict any outcome or timeline for your case.
Should I use Form 843 or just write a letter?
Form 843, Claim for Refund and Request for Abatement, is the form for claiming a refund of a penalty already paid or requesting abatement of a penalty still owed. The form alone is a claim; the letter is the argument that supports it. Send the letter with the form, state the tax year, the penalty type, and the amount, and keep a copy of everything. This tool drafts the letter and walks through the form; it does not file anything for you. The Form 843 guidance mode above covers what goes where.
What happens if my penalty abatement request is denied?
A denial is not the end. You can appeal the decision, and the appeal is reviewed by an IRS appeals officer rather than the person who denied the request. The appeal usually has to be filed within the time printed on the denial notice, so read that notice carefully and do not miss the window. If the penalty is for a year before 2025 and first-time abatement did not apply, a reasonable-cause argument is the usual next step.
I already paid the penalty. Can I still ask for abatement?
Yes. Form 843 is the form for claiming a refund of a penalty already paid as well as for requesting abatement of a penalty still owed. The letter states the same facts: the tax year, the penalty type, the amount paid, and the reason the penalty should be removed. Keep your payment confirmation and the original notice, and send the request to the address printed on the notice or on the form instructions.
