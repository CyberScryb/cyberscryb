What Makes a Cover Letter Work

A cover letter has one job: make the hiring manager want to read your resume. The structure that does this has four parts. First, a hook tied to THIS role — not "I am writing to apply for the position of," which tells them nothing. Reference something specific: a product they shipped, a problem the team owns, a result you've produced that maps to it. Second, two or three achievements from your background, stated with numbers wherever possible. "Cut checkout latency 40%" beats "improved performance." Third, one paragraph on why this company, with a specific detail that proves you did more than skim the homepage. Fourth, a confident close that asks for the interview directly.

The tone matters as much as the content. Professional doesn't mean stiff. The best cover letters sound like a competent person talking, not a template. Avoid the phrases every hiring manager has read ten thousand times: "passionate team player," "detail-oriented self-starter," "fast-paced environment." If a sentence could appear in anyone's letter, cut it or make it specific.

The Mistakes That Get Letters Deleted

The most common failure is the generic letter: one document blasted at forty companies with only the company name swapped. Recruiters spot it in seconds because nothing in it connects to their specific role. The second failure is length. Anything over a page signals you can't prioritize, which is a bad look for almost every job. The third is repeating the resume. The letter should interpret the resume, not duplicate it — explain why the experience matters for this role, not list it again.

Then there are the unforced errors: wrong company name from a find-and-replace fail, addressing it to the wrong hiring manager, typos in the first paragraph. This generator handles the structure and the tailoring. You handle the final read-through. Always read the letter out loud once before sending; anything that sounds awkward out loud reads worse on screen.

How to Use This Generator Well

The quality of the output tracks the quality of your input. Don't write "5 years experience in marketing." Write what you actually did: "Grew email list from 8k to 41k in 18 months; highest-converting campaign drove $212k in attributed revenue." Specific inputs produce specific letters. Include the job title and company so the letter can speak to the role directly instead of floating in generic space.

After generating, do three things. First, verify every fact — the model only knows what you told it, but check anyway. Second, add one sentence only you could write: something about why this company specifically. Third, match the tone to the industry. A startup and a bank do not want the same letter, which is why the tone selector exists. For more on structure, the cover letter writing guide walks through each paragraph in detail.
