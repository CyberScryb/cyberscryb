Frequently Asked Questions

How much notice should I give when resigning?

Two weeks is the standard in most industries. Senior roles or specialized positions may warrant more. Check your employment contract first — some specify a notice period.

Should I explain why I'm leaving?

Keep it brief and positive. A one-line reason (new opportunity, relocation) is enough. Never use the letter to air grievances — it becomes part of your employment record.

Can I resign by email?

Yes, email is standard now. Tell your manager in person or by video first if possible, then follow up with the written letter the same day so there's a clear record.

What Goes in a Resignation Letter

A resignation letter is short on purpose. It needs exactly four things: a clear statement that you're resigning, your last day, a brief thank-you, and an offer to help with the transition. That's it. The letter is a formal record, not the place to explain your life story, negotiate, or settle scores. Anything beyond those four elements risks saying something you'll regret once it's in your employment file.

State the resignation in the first two sentences. Don't bury it in paragraph three behind throat-clearing. "Please accept this letter as formal notice of my resignation from [role], effective [last day]." Clean, unambiguous, done. Then one genuine sentence of thanks — specific beats generic. "I learned more about client work here in two years than in the previous five" lands harder than "thank you for the opportunities."

What Never Goes in Writing

Whatever your real reasons for leaving, the letter is not the venue. Complaints about your manager, salary grievances, criticism of the company — none of it belongs in a document HR keeps. If you have legitimate issues, raise them in an exit interview verbally, or better yet, after you've left and the reference is secured. The letter should be boring in the best way: professional, warm, forgettable.

Similarly, don't use the letter to fish for a counteroffer. If you want to stay for more money, that's a conversation with your manager, not a paragraph in a resignation letter. And don't announce where you're going in detail. "A new opportunity" is sufficient. Your former employer doesn't need your new employer's name in writing.

The Process Around the Letter

Tell your manager first, in person or on video — never let them learn from an email forward. Keep it short and positive: you're moving on, your last day is X, you'll make the handover smooth. Then send the written letter the same day so the date is on record. After that, actually do the handover well. Document your open projects, introduce your replacement to key contacts, and leave things tidy. The last two weeks are what people remember, and industries are smaller than you think.

Before your last day, save what you're entitled to: pay stubs, benefits info, personal contacts. Return company property promptly. And line up your references while goodwill is high — ask your manager directly whether they'd serve as a positive reference.
