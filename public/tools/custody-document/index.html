How to Use This Tool

A parenting plan is only useful if it is specific enough to follow on a bad day. This tool turns your description of the situation into a structured draft you can take to mediation, to your attorney, or to the other parent. Four steps:

  1. Describe the situation. Include each child's age, where the children live now, the current schedule (formal or informal), and what you want to change or establish. The more concrete the detail, the less generic the draft.
  2. Pick a document type. Parenting Plan is the full agreement. Custody Declaration is a sworn statement of facts for a filing. Modification Request asks the court to change an existing order. Visitation Schedule Proposal covers time-sharing only.
  3. Add the children's ages. Schedules that work for a toddler do not work for a teenager. Ages help the draft match the schedule to the child's stage, including school, activities, and overnight needs.
  4. Generate, review, and edit. Read the whole draft. Fix anything wrong, add anything missing, and have a family law attorney or mediator review it before you file or sign anything. Copy it or print it as a PDF when it is ready.

What a Strong Parenting Plan Covers

Courts approve plans that answer the questions parents actually fight about later. A draft that covers these six areas is far more useful than one that only lists a weekly schedule:

The regular schedule

Name the pattern, not just "50/50." Common patterns include week-on/week-off, 2-2-3 rotation, 2-2-5-5, and every-other-weekend with a midweek evening. State the exact exchange day, time, and location, and who handles transport. A plan that says "Friday at 6 p.m. at school pickup" prevents more arguments than one that says "alternating weekends."

Holidays, breaks, and vacations

Holiday schedules override the regular schedule, so spell them out: who has the children on each holiday, whether holidays alternate by year, how spring break and summer are divided, and how much notice a vacation requires. Include a rule for what happens when a holiday falls on the other parent's regular day.

Decision-making (legal custody)

Separate who the child lives with from who decides. Cover education, medical care, religion, and extracurricular activities. Joint legal custody means both parents decide together; sole legal custody means one parent decides. Say which it is, and say what happens when the parents cannot agree.

Communication rules

Set the channel (text, email, a co-parenting app), the expected response time, and the rule that child-related messages stay businesslike and off the children. Many plans also set phone and video-call access: when the child can call the other parent, and how often.

Dispute resolution

Write down the ladder before you need it: first the parents try to agree directly, then mediation, then the court. A plan with a mediation step saves both parents the cost of a contested hearing over a schedule change.

Modification

Children grow, jobs change, and parents move. A good plan says how it gets changed: by written agreement of both parents, or by asking the court when circumstances have changed substantially. Without a modification clause, even a small change can turn into a filing.

Worked Example

What you type: "Two children, ages 7 and 4. We have an informal 50/50 arrangement but nothing in writing. I want a 2-2-3 schedule, alternating holidays, and joint decisions on school and medical care. Exchanges at school drop-off on weekdays and 6 p.m. on Sundays."

What the draft gives back: a structured parenting plan with a named 2-2-3 rotation (Monday-Tuesday with one parent, Wednesday-Thursday with the other, alternating Friday through Sunday), a holiday table that alternates Thanksgiving, Christmas, and spring break by year, a joint legal custody clause for education and medical decisions, an exchange clause matching the times you gave, and a mediation-before-court dispute clause.

What to check before you use it: that the exchange times match your work hours, that the holiday rotation fits your family's traditions, that the plan names the right school and childcare provider, and that nothing in it contradicts an existing court order. If an order already exists, this draft is a modification proposal, not a replacement.

Limitations: What This Tool Cannot Do

  • It is not legal advice. The draft is a starting point. Only a licensed family law attorney in your state can tell you whether it meets your court's requirements.
  • It does not know your state. Custody forms, filing fees, and required language vary by state and county. Some courts require their own form; this draft may need to be transferred onto it.
  • It cannot resolve a contested case. If the other parent disagrees, safety is a concern, or there are allegations of abuse, neglect, or substance use, bring the draft to a mediator or attorney rather than sending it directly.
  • It does not calculate child support. Support is set by state guidelines from both parents' incomes. A parenting plan and a support order are separate documents.
  • It cannot file anything. A plan becomes enforceable only when a court signs off on it. Until then it is an agreement between parents, useful in mediation but not a court order.
  • It cannot guarantee the other parent will agree. The best use of a clear draft is that it makes agreement easier: it is much simpler to negotiate over a written proposal than over a blank page.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is an AI-generated parenting plan legally valid?

Not until a court approves it — but a clearly written plan is exactly what courts and mediators want to see. Use this tool to draft it, then work with a family law attorney or mediator to get it filed. A well-drafted plan speeds that process significantly.

What should a parenting plan include?

Physical custody schedule (regular weeks, holidays, school breaks), legal custody (who makes decisions about school, medical, religion), communication rules, how disputes get resolved, and how the plan gets modified if circumstances change.

Can I use this if we agree on everything already?

Yes — this is the best use case. If you and the other parent are aligned, this tool gets that agreement into a clear written document that a court can approve without a fight. Informal agreements are unenforceable; written plans are.

What if we disagree on custody?

Use this tool to draft your preferred arrangement and bring it to mediation or your attorney. Having a clear written starting point is better than going in with nothing.

How specific should I be about the schedule?

As specific as possible. Vague plans ("we'll figure out holidays as they come") create conflict. Spell out which parent has the child on each holiday, who handles pickups, and what happens when the schedule changes.

Is this legal advice?

No. This is a drafting assistant designed to help you organize your thoughts and structure your documents. It does not constitute legal advice — always consult a qualified family law attorney before taking legal action.

Will a court accept this document?

Courts want clear, well-organized documents. This tool helps you create a strong starting point, but a family law attorney should review your draft before filing anything with the court.

What's a parenting plan?

A written agreement covering the custody schedule, holiday and vacation arrangements, decision-making responsibilities (education, healthcare, religion), communication guidelines, and how future disputes will be resolved.

Can I use this if I can't afford a lawyer?

Yes. Many courts accept self-filed (pro se) documents. This tool helps you create organized, clear drafts. Legal aid organizations in your area may also review your draft for free or at reduced cost.

Can I edit the draft after generating it?

Yes. Copy the draft into any document editor and change whatever you need, or generate again with a more detailed description. The draft is yours; edit it until it says exactly what you agreed to.

What if the other parent will not agree to a plan?

Draft your preferred arrangement anyway and bring it to mediation. A written proposal gives the mediator something concrete to work from. If mediation fails, the same draft becomes the starting point for what you ask the court to order.
