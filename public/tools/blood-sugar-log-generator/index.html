Who This Blood Sugar Log Is For

A blood sugar log solves a simple but painful problem: glucose numbers are useless without context. Meters store readings, but they don't record that the 214 was an hour after your birthday cake, or that the 63 happened on a day you skipped lunch. Writing readings down with the time, the meal, and a quick note turns raw numbers into a pattern your doctor can actually act on — which is why endocrinologists ask for logs before they change a medication dose.

This generator is built for people managing diabetes on paper: newly diagnosed patients still learning the routine, family caregivers tracking readings for a parent or spouse, and anyone who shows up to appointments with readings scattered across a meter, a phone, and a sticky note. Generate the log, print it, and keep it next to the meter. Everything runs in your browser — no account, no upload, nothing leaves your device.

How to Use This Generator

  1. Enter a name and period. Optional, but it keeps multi-person households from mixing up logs — one for you, one for mom.
  2. Pick your length. 7 days works for a check-in; 30 days gives your doctor a full month of trends to review.
  3. Check the columns that match your testing schedule. Only test fasting and bedtime? Uncheck the meal columns so you're not staring at empty boxes.
  4. Set your target range. The defaults (70–180) are common starting points — replace them with the range your doctor gave you so out-of-range readings are obvious at a glance.
  5. Generate, print, or download. Hit Generate Log, then Print for paper or Download .txt for a digital copy. Record each reading in the moment — backfilled numbers from memory are worth far less than readings written down at the time.

Real Ways People Use This Log

Newly diagnosed and finding your routine. Your doctor says "test fasting and two hours after meals," and suddenly you're testing four times a day with no system. A 30-day log with the full meal columns gives the first month of data structure — and answers the two questions every newly diagnosed patient asks: how often should I test, and what do I write down.

Caregivers tracking a parent's readings. When you're managing a parent's diabetes, the doctor asks "what have the numbers been?" and you're digging through texts. One printed sheet on the fridge becomes the shared record — any family member can write down the morning fasting number, and the notes column captures medication and symptoms for the next appointment.

Spotting meal-driven patterns. Numbers that keep running high after dinner but are fine at breakfast tell a story a single reading never could. A month of before-and-after meal columns makes it obvious which meals are the problem — which is exactly what you and your care team need to adjust diet or timing instead of guessing.

Preparing for an endocrinology visit. A completed log is the most productive thing you can bring to an appointment. Instead of reciting numbers from memory, you hand over weeks of timestamped readings with notes — and walk out with medication decisions based on evidence, not estimates.

Clinical Guide: Understanding Glucose Target Ranges

Regular blood glucose tracking is a cornerstone of effective diabetes self-management and clinical care planning. Maintaining an accurate log helps identify trends such as postprandial glycemic spikes, dawn phenomenon, or nocturnal hypoglycemia.

  • Fasting Blood Glucose: Measured upon waking before food or calorie intake. General ADA targets for non-pregnant adults range between 80–130 mg/dL.
  • Postprandial (Post-Meal) Glucose: Measured 1 to 2 hours after the start of a meal. Standard clinical targets are generally below 180 mg/dL.
  • Bedtime Checks: Ensures glucose is safely stabilized before sleep to avoid overnight hypoglycemia. Typical targets range from 100–140 mg/dL.
  • Hypoglycemia Action Threshold: Blood sugar below 70 mg/dL requires immediate treatment following the "Rule of 15" (15 grams of fast-acting carbs, wait 15 minutes, recheck).

Frequently Asked Questions

Is this the same as a diabetes log book?

Yes — it's a free printable version of the glucose tracking pages in a diabetes log book, with the common columns and a place to note medication or insulin.

How often should blood sugar be tested?

That depends on your diabetes type and treatment. Your doctor sets the schedule — this log has room for fasting, before/after each meal, and bedtime readings.

Can my care team use this?

Yes. Print the completed log and bring it to appointments — the clear layout makes patterns easy for a doctor or nurse to spot.

Do I need to log my blood sugar if my meter stores readings?

A meter stores numbers, not context. A written log adds the meal, the timing, medication, and symptoms alongside each reading — which is what turns a number into a pattern your doctor can act on.

What should I write in the notes column?

Anything that might explain the number: what you ate, exercise, illness, stress, a missed or changed medication dose, and symptoms like shakiness or sweating. Two words is enough — "skipped lunch" explains a 63 better than a blank box ever will.

Can I use this log for gestational diabetes?

Yes. Gestational diabetes usually means testing fasting and 1–2 hours after each meal, which matches this log's columns. Ask your doctor for your pregnancy-specific target ranges and print those on the log instead of the defaults.

Disclaimer: This is a tracking tool, not medical advice. Always follow your doctor's testing schedule and glucose targets; they may differ from the printed default range.
