The Words Nobody Taught You

Almost everyone freezes when it is time to say something about a death. Not because they do not care, but because they are afraid of saying the wrong thing. The fear is misplaced. Grieving people do not grade your wording. They remember who showed up. A short, imperfect message sent today beats a perfect one you agonize over for a week.

A good condolence message has four parts: name the loss specifically, share one real memory or trait of the person who died, offer specific help instead of "let me know if you need anything," and close warmly. That is the whole formula. One specific detail, "I will always remember how your dad made every room laugh," does more than a paragraph of general sympathy ever will.

This tool builds the message around what you actually know: who died, who you are writing to, the tone you want. Then make it yours before you send it. Swap in one true detail only you would remember, and read it once imagining you are the person receiving it. If it feels like something a real person would say, it is ready.

How It Works

Step 1: Say who you are writing to. A message to a close friend reads differently from one to a coworker or a neighbor. Pick the relationship so the tone lands right.

Step 2: Say what happened. Who died, and what is your connection to the grieving person. A few honest sentences is plenty. Do not worry about sounding polished.

Step 3: Add one real memory if you have one. This is the part that turns a generic message into a personal one. A trait, a moment, something they did, anything true.

Step 4: Choose tone and length. Warm and gentle for friends and family, formal and respectful for professional settings, short and simple when you barely knew them, faith-based when you share their beliefs.

Real Ways People Use It

A friend lost a parent: The most common use. You knew their dad from cookouts but not well. The tool builds a message that names him, keeps it honest about your distance, and centers your friend.

A coworker is grieving: The awkward one. You need to be respectful without overstepping, warm without being personal. The formal tone handles this exactly.

Sudden or difficult losses: When the death was unexpected, words feel even harder. A short, simple message that does not try to explain the loss is what people need.

What should I write in a condolence message?

Name the loss specifically, share one real memory or trait, offer specific help instead of "let me know if you need anything," and close warmly. Keep advice, platitudes, and comparisons out of it.

How long should a condolence message be?

Fifty to two hundred words is the sweet spot. A grieving person cannot absorb a long letter, and a few sincere sentences carry more weight than a page of filler.

What should I never say in a condolence message?

Never "I know how you feel," "everything happens for a reason," "they are in a better place" (unless you share their faith), "stay strong," or any version of "at least." These minimize the loss instead of honoring it.

Is it okay to send a condolence message by text?

Yes, especially in the first days. A short, sincere text that arrives fast beats a perfect card that arrives late. A handwritten card afterward makes a good second gesture.

What do I write when I did not know the person who died?

Say exactly that, warmly. Acknowledge their grief, not your distance: "I did not know your father well, but I know how much you loved him." Honesty about the distance reads as respect.
