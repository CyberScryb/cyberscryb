How IRS installment requests actually work

A payment plan is a proposal, not a promise

You propose a monthly amount; the IRS reviews it and either accepts it, asks for different terms, or asks for more information. A letter that states the balance, the tax years, and a sustainable monthly figure gives the reviewer something concrete to say yes to. A vague request gives them a reason to ask again.

File first, then ask

Every required return must be filed before an agreement can be approved. File on time even when you cannot pay in full: filing late adds a separate problem to the one you are already solving.

The monthly figure must survive contact with your budget

The figure that gets accepted is the one you can pay every month without fail. Test terms in the panel above: a longer term lowers the monthly payment but means more interest over the life of the plan. Propose the amount you can sustain, not the amount you wish you could pay.

Interest does not pause

Interest and penalties generally keep accruing on the unpaid balance until it is paid in full, even while an agreement is active. Paying extra when you can, and paying the plan off early when possible, cuts the total you pay.

Stay current or the plan dies

New tax years must be filed on time and new balances paid, or the agreement can default. A payment plan covers the past; it does not excuse the present.

Everything in writing, everything kept

The notice, your request letter, Form 9465, payment confirmations, and every IRS reply, in one file. If the agreement is ever questioned, the file is what decides it. Keep it until the balance is zero and the IRS confirms it.

Common questions

What is an IRS installment agreement?
An installment agreement is a payment plan that lets you pay a tax balance over time in monthly payments instead of all at once. You propose a monthly amount, the IRS reviews it, and if it is accepted you pay that amount each month until the balance is paid. Interest and penalties generally keep accruing until the balance is paid in full.
How do I request an IRS installment agreement?
File every required tax return first, then apply through the IRS Online Payment Agreement tool or by mailing Form 9465, Installment Agreement Request. A written request letter like the one this tool drafts goes with a mailed Form 9465 and states the balance, the tax years, and the monthly payment you propose.
What is Form 9465?
Form 9465 is the IRS form titled Installment Agreement Request. It asks for the tax years involved, the amount owed, and the monthly payment amount and payment date you propose. This tool drafts the cover letter that accompanies it; it does not replace the form itself.
What monthly payment should I propose?
Propose an amount you can actually pay every month without fail, based on your own budget. This tool computes the monthly figure from the balance and the number of months you enter, so you can test terms before you commit: a longer term means a smaller monthly payment but more interest over time. Never propose an amount you cannot sustain, because a missed payment can put the agreement into default.
What happens if I miss a payment?
A missed payment puts the agreement at risk of default, and default can let the IRS resume collection action. If you know a payment will be late, contact the IRS before the due date. Reinstating a defaulted agreement is possible but may involve an additional fee.
Do penalties and interest stop once my agreement is approved?
No. Interest and penalties generally continue to accrue on the unpaid balance until it is paid in full, even while an agreement is active. That is why paying extra when you can, and paying the agreement off early if possible, reduces the total you pay.
Can I request a payment plan if I owe for more than one tax year?
Yes. List every tax year and the balance for each in your request, and propose one monthly payment covering the combined balance. Enter the total balance you owe across all years in the tool above.
Can I change my monthly payment after the agreement starts?
You can ask the IRS to change the amount, through your IRS online account or by phone. A change or restructuring may involve a fee. Try to propose a sustainable amount the first time, and contact the IRS before a payment fails rather than after.
