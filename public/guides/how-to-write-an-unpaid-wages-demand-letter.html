How to Write an Unpaid Wages Demand Letter (With Examples)

Your employer owes you money and is not paying. Before you file a wage claim or hire a lawyer, a written demand letter puts your claim, your math, and a deadline on the record. Here is the exact structure, three real examples, and what to do if the letter gets ignored.

By CyberScryb 📅 Updated November 1, 2026 ⏱ 10 min read
⚡ Key Takeaways (TL;DR)

A demand letter is a formal written request for wages you earned but were not paid, sent before you file a claim or sue. The letter has six parts: the employer's contact info, your employment facts, the exact amount owed with the math shown, the law that backs your claim, a deadline (14 to 21 days), and what happens next if they miss it. Send it by certified mail or another provable method and keep copies. Federal law gives you 2 years to file (3 if the violation was willful) and can double your recovery. This is general information, not legal advice.

Step 1: Confirm You Are Actually Owed the Money

A demand letter only works if the law is on your side, so check your situation against the rules before you write. The most common valid claims:

  • Missing or short final paycheck. Most states require your last paycheck within days of your final day, not on some future schedule the employer invents. The exact deadline varies by state.
  • Unpaid overtime. Under the federal Fair Labor Standards Act, nonexempt employees must get 1.5 times the regular rate for hours over 40 in a workweek. Salaried does not automatically mean exempt; the actual job duties decide.
  • Shorted or missing hours. Hours you worked but that never appeared on the check, including required off-the-clock work like closing up, answering messages, or mandatory training.
  • Illegal deductions. Docking pay for register shortages, broken equipment, or uniforms is illegal if it drops you below minimum wage, and some states ban it outright.
  • Promised bonuses or commissions that met the terms. If the written plan says you earned it and you hit the terms, the employer owes it. Verbal promises are harder but not always worthless.

One caution: the label "independent contractor" on your paperwork does not settle the question. If the company controlled your schedule, methods, and work, you may be a misclassified employee owed full protections. Misclassification fights are fact-heavy, so get a lawyer or your state labor agency involved before you write the letter in that case.

Step 2: Gather Your Proof Before You Write a Word

The letter is only as strong as the records behind it. Collect everything you can before drafting:

  • Pay stubs and direct deposit records. Shows what you were paid, the rate, and which pay periods are short.
  • Your own hours log. If you kept notes, a calendar, or app records of hours worked, they count. Write down what you remember now, while it is fresh, even if the employer "lost" the official timesheets.
  • Schedules, texts, and emails about shifts. Messages from a manager asking you to stay late or come in early prove hours the time system may not show.
  • The offer letter or pay agreement. Your promised rate and pay schedule, in writing.
  • Anything showing the employer's reason for not paying. An email saying "we will catch you up next month" or "you are not getting overtime" is gold.

Organize it all in one place with dates, names, and copies. Our paper trail guide covers a system for exactly this kind of record-keeping, and the same habits help if you later file with an agency.

Step 3: Use the Six-Part Structure

Every effective demand letter has the same six parts, in this order. Nobody involved has time for a long story, so the structure keeps it short and impossible to ignore:

  • 1. Your contact info, the date, and the employer's address. Address it to the person who can actually fix payroll: the owner, payroll manager, or HR lead. A clear subject line up top, such as "Demand for unpaid wages, May 1 to July 15, 2026."
  • 2. Your employment facts. Job title, start date and end date if you left, pay rate, and pay schedule. One paragraph. This establishes exactly who is owed what.
  • 3. The exact amount owed, with the math shown. Not a round number from memory. Break it down: hours times rate, overtime hours times the overtime rate, each pay period listed. The math is the part that makes employers pay, because it shows you can prove the number.
  • 4. The law that backs you. Cite the Fair Labor Standards Act for federal claims and your state's wage law by name. You do not need to write like a lawyer; naming the statute tells them you know your rights.
  • 5. A deadline and what happens next. Fourteen to twenty-one days is the standard window. State plainly that if payment is not received by the date, you will file a wage claim with the Department of Labor or your state labor agency.
  • 6. Your signature and how to pay you. Tell them exactly where to send the money: mailing address or direct deposit details. Remove every excuse for delay.

Step 4: Write the Numbers So They Hold Up

This is the section that separates letters that get paid from letters that get filed away. Be precise, and keep every calculation visible:

  • Itemize by pay period. "Unpaid regular wages: $960 for 40 hours at $24 per hour, pay period May 1 to May 15." Repeat for each period. A lump sum with no breakdown invites an argument.
  • Show the overtime rate explicitly. "Unpaid overtime: $432 for 12 hours at $36 per hour (1.5 times my $24 regular rate)." Spell out the multiplier so there is nothing to dispute.
  • List every category separately. Regular wages, overtime, the short final paycheck, reimbursable expenses, accrued vacation or PTO if your state or policy requires payout. Then a total.
  • Do not inflate the number. Rounding up or adding hours you are not sure about destroys your credibility. If anything, conservative math that you can prove beats an aggressive number you cannot.

Example 1: Missing Final Paycheck

The simplest case. You left the job, the last check never came or came up short:

"November 1, 2026

[Owner or payroll manager name]
[Company name]
[Company address]

Re: Demand for unpaid final wages

Dear [name],

I was employed as [job title] at [company] from [start date] to [end date], at a rate of $[rate] per [hour/week]. My final paycheck covering [dates] was due on [date] and has not been paid.

The amount owed is $[total]: [X] hours at $[rate] for a total of $[amount], [plus any accrued vacation of $[amount] as required by company policy]. My calculation is based on my time records for those dates.

[State] law requires final wages to be paid [within X days / on the next regular payday]. Federal and state wage laws require payment of all wages earned. I am requesting payment of $[total] within 14 days of this letter, sent to [your address or direct deposit details].

If I do not receive payment by [date], I will file a wage claim with the U.S. Department of Labor Wage and Hour Division and [state labor agency].

Sincerely,
[your name, address, phone, email]"

Example 2: Unpaid Overtime

You worked over 40 hours a week and were paid straight time, or not paid at all, for the extra hours:

"November 1, 2026

[Name, company, address]

Re: Demand for unpaid overtime wages, [dates]

Dear [name],

I am employed as [job title] at [company], earning $[rate] per hour, paid [weekly/biweekly]. From [start date] to [end date], I regularly worked more than 40 hours per week but was not paid the overtime rate required by law.

For the pay period [dates], I worked [X] overtime hours, owed at 1.5 times my $[rate] regular rate, or $[overtime rate] per hour, for a total of $[amount]. [Repeat for each pay period.] The total unpaid overtime is $[total]. My hours are documented in my schedule records and [manager's] shift messages.

The Fair Labor Standards Act, 29 U.S.C. section 207, requires overtime pay at one-and-one-half times the regular rate for hours over 40 in a workweek. I am requesting payment of $[total] within 21 days of this letter.

If payment is not received by [date], I will file a complaint with the U.S. Department of Labor Wage and Hour Division, which may result in additional liquidated damages and penalties.

Sincerely,
[your name, address, phone, email]"

Example 3: Shorted Hours and Off-the-Clock Work

The hours on the check do not match the hours you worked, or you were told to clock out and keep working:

"November 1, 2026

[Name, company, address]

Re: Demand for unpaid wages for hours worked, [dates]

Dear [name],

I was employed as [job title] at [company] from [start date] to [end date] at $[rate] per hour. During that period I was required to [close the store after clocking out / answer work messages off shift / attend unpaid training], and those hours were not recorded or paid.

The unpaid time totals [X] hours across [dates], for a total of $[total] at my $[rate] rate. [List each date and the hours if you have them.] Federal law requires payment for all hours worked, including time the employer knew or should have known about.

I am requesting payment of $[total] within 14 days of this letter. If I do not receive payment by [date], I will file a wage claim with [state labor agency] and the U.S. Department of Labor.

Sincerely,
[your name, address, phone, email]"

What Never to Put in the Letter

These mistakes show up constantly and they all weaken your position. Cut every one:

  • A vague amount. "You owe me around three thousand" is not a demand. A specific number with visible math is.
  • Threats you will not carry out. Do not promise to "shut the company down" or "go to the press." Threaten only the step you will actually take: filing the wage claim.
  • Insults, profanity, or emotional attacks. The letter may end up in front of an agency investigator or a judge. Keep it cold and factual.
  • Hours you cannot back up. Every hour in the letter should have a record, a message, or a consistent log behind it. Inflated numbers are the fastest way to lose a claim.
  • Signing anything the employer sends back without reading it. Some employers respond with a partial payment plus a release. Cashing it or signing it can waive the rest of your claim in some states.
  • Admitting you were a contractor if you were not. Do not write "as an independent contractor" anywhere in the letter if the facts say employee. Let the relationship facts speak for themselves.

Send It So You Can Prove They Got It

A demand letter you cannot prove was delivered is a diary entry. Use one of these:

  • Certified mail with return receipt. The classic choice. You get a signed card proving delivery, which becomes an exhibit if you file a claim.
  • Email with a read receipt or a clear reply. Acceptable for many purposes, especially if the employer replies. Screenshot and save everything.
  • Hand delivery with a witness. Works if the workplace is still accessible, but bring someone and note the date, time, and who accepted it.

Keep a copy of the letter, the proof of delivery, and everything from Step 2 together. If the deadline passes with no payment, this packet is what you hand to the agency or your lawyer.

If They Ignore the Letter

An ignored letter is not a failure. It is your evidence that you gave the employer a fair chance to pay, which is exactly what agencies and courts want to see. Your next moves, in order of cost:

  • File with the U.S. Department of Labor Wage and Hour Division. Free. Handles federal minimum wage and overtime claims, and can recover back pay plus liquidated damages.
  • File with your state labor agency. Also free, and sometimes faster or stronger than the federal route. State laws often add penalties the federal law does not have, like California's waiting time penalty for late final paychecks.
  • Small claims court. For smaller amounts, usually a few thousand dollars depending on the state. No lawyer needed, filing fees are low, and the letter plus your math is most of your case.
  • An employment lawyer. For larger claims, retaliation, or misclassification. Many take wage cases on contingency, meaning no upfront cost, and federal law lets prevailing workers recover attorney's fees.

Watch the clock on all of this. Federal claims expire 2 years after the wages were due, or 3 years if the violation was willful, and some state deadlines are far shorter. FAQ

What is an unpaid wages demand letter?

A formal written request you send your employer before filing a claim or suing. It states who you are, what work you did, the exact amount owed with the math shown, the law that supports your claim, and a deadline to pay. It creates a dated paper trail, proves the employer knew about the claim, and often produces payment without any agency or court being involved.

Do I need a lawyer to write a demand letter for unpaid wages?

No. You can write an effective demand letter yourself as long as the facts are accurate, the amount is calculated correctly, and the letter is sent in a provable way such as certified mail or email with a read receipt. A lawyer becomes useful if the amount is large, the employer ignored your letter, or the case involves misclassification or retaliation.

How long do I have to file a wage claim?

Under the federal Fair Labor Standards Act you have 2 years from the date the wages were due, or 3 years if the violation was willful. State deadlines vary a lot: Texas gives you 180 days to file with the Texas Workforce Commission, Illinois gives 1 year, Wisconsin gives 2 years, and Washington and California generally give 3 years. Every pay period has its own clock, so do not wait.

Where do I file an unpaid wages claim?

You have two main free options: the U.S. Department of Labor Wage and Hour Division for federal claims, or your state's labor agency, such as the California Labor Commissioner's Office. Beyond a demand letter and agencies, smaller amounts can go to small claims court, and larger or group claims can be filed as a lawsuit, where an employment lawyer may take the case on contingency.

Can my employer fire me for sending a demand letter or filing a wage claim?

Retaliation for filing a good faith wage complaint is illegal under the Fair Labor Standards Act and most state laws. An employer cannot legally fire, demote, cut hours, or otherwise punish you for asserting your right to be paid. Keep records of any adverse action after you send the letter, because retaliation gives rise to a separate claim for back pay and damages.

What if my employer ignores the demand letter?

An ignored letter is not a dead end; it is your evidence that you gave the employer a fair chance to pay. The next steps are filing a complaint with the Department of Labor Wage and Hour Division or your state labor agency, suing in small claims court for smaller amounts, or talking to an employment lawyer for larger claims. The letter and the delivery receipt become exhibits in any of those.

How much can I recover in unpaid wages?

At minimum, the full back pay you earned. Under the federal Fair Labor Standards Act you can also recover liquidated damages equal to the unpaid wages, effectively doubling the recovery for minimum wage or overtime violations, plus attorneys' fees if you prevail in court. Many states add their own penalties, such as California's waiting time penalty of up to 30 days of wages for a late final paycheck.

What if I was classified as an independent contractor?

The label your employer used does not decide the question; the actual working relationship does. If the company controlled your schedule, your methods, and your work, you may have been misclassified and are owed employee protections including minimum wage and overtime. Misclassification is common enough that agencies and courts hear it constantly, but it is fact-specific, so talk to a lawyer or your state labor agency before writing the letter.

Is this legal advice?

No. Wage and hour law varies by state, and deadlines, penalties, and procedures differ. This guide describes a general method for writing a demand letter. For large claims, misclassification, retaliation, or anything you are unsure about, talk to an employment lawyer or your state labor agency before acting.
