How to Request a Court Fee Waiver

The filing fee should not decide whether you get your day in court. What a fee waiver is, who qualifies, how to ask for one, and what happens if the court says no.

By CyberScryb 📅 Updated November 21, 2026 ⏱ 11 min read
⚡ Key Takeaways (TL;DR)

A court fee waiver, called in forma pauperis in federal court, lets you file a case without paying the filing fee because you cannot afford it. You qualify most often by low household income or by receiving public benefits such as SNAP, SSI, TANF, or Medicaid, and some courts grant one on hardship even if your income is above the line. Get the form from the court clerk or the court website, fill in your income, expenses, assets, debts, and household size honestly, sign it under penalty of perjury, and file it with your case papers. The court grants it, denies it, or sets a short hearing to ask about your finances. If it is denied, pay by the deadline, ask for a payment plan, or ask the court to reconsider. What a court fee waiver is

Filing a case costs money. The filing fee for a civil case in state court is often a few hundred dollars, and in federal court it is higher. A court fee waiver is the court's permission to file without paying that fee, because you cannot afford it. In federal court the request is called an application to proceed in forma pauperis, Latin for in the manner of a poor person. In state court it is usually called a fee waiver application, a request to waive court fees, or an application for a waiver of fees. The name changes. The idea does not.

A waiver is not a free lawyer and it is not a ruling on your case. It is only about the money you owe the court to file. The judge who decides the waiver is usually not deciding whether your case has merit. They are deciding whether your finances leave you unable to pay. That is a narrower question, and it is decided on paper, on the numbers you put on the form.

A waiver can also be partial. Some courts waive the filing fee but not the fee to serve papers, or waive the fees for copies and transcripts but not the filing fee. Read the order when it comes back, because a partial grant still leaves a bill you have to pay, and the deadline for that bill is usually short.

You can ask for a waiver at almost any point when a court fee stands between you and filing: when you start a case, when you file an answer or a counterclaim, when you appeal, and in some courts when you ask for a jury trial. Each step usually needs its own application. A waiver granted in the trial court does not automatically carry over to an appeal.

Who qualifies

Courts use one of three tests, and many use more than one. Which one applies depends on your state and your court, so check the form itself before you assume anything.

  • Income below the court's line. Many courts grant a waiver if your household income falls below a set level, often tied to a percentage of the federal poverty guidelines. The limit rises with household size, so a family of four has a higher limit than a single person. Some courts print the exact numbers on the form or on their website. Others leave the number to the judge.
  • Receiving public benefits. Many courts grant a waiver automatically if you receive public benefits such as SNAP, SSI, TANF, Medicaid, or state general assistance. You usually prove it by attaching a benefits letter or a recent award notice. If you receive one of these, say so on the form and attach the proof. It is often the fastest path to a grant.
  • Hardship. Some courts grant a waiver when your income is above the line but your necessary expenses leave you unable to pay the fee. Rent, utilities, food, medical bills, child care, and transportation to work are the expenses that count. A judge deciding hardship looks at what is left after the bills that keep your household running, not at your gross pay alone.

Household size matters in all three tests. Count the people who live with you and depend on your income, including children and a spouse or partner whose income is part of the household. If your household size changed recently, use the current number and be ready to explain it.

A few things do not disqualify you on their own. Owning a car you need for work, owning a modest home, or having a small amount in savings does not automatically mean you can afford the fee. The question is whether paying the fee would keep you from meeting your basic needs. Say what you own honestly on the form, and let the court decide.

What fees it covers and what it does not cover

A granted waiver usually covers the fees the court charges to process your case. What exactly that means varies, so check your court's order and its fee schedule.

  • Usually covered. The filing fee for your complaint, answer, or appeal. The fee to serve papers on the other side, when the court handles service. Fees for copies of court records tied to your case. Court reporter and transcript fees in many courts, when the transcript is needed for the case.
  • Sometimes covered. Jury fees, where the court charges them. Fees for a court-appointed interpreter. Fees for filing a motion, in courts that charge per motion. Mediation fees, in courts that run a mediation program.
  • Usually not covered. Your lawyer's fees. An expert witness. The cost of your own copies, postage, and travel to court. The other side's costs if you lose, unless the court orders otherwise. A bond or a deposit the court requires for a specific kind of filing, such as an appeal bond in some states.

A waiver can also be revoked. If your finances improve during the case, or if the court later finds that the information on your form was not accurate, the court can order you to pay the fees after the fact. If your situation changes while the case is open, tell the court. It is far better than having the court find out on its own.

How to get the form

Every court has its own form, and using the wrong one is the most common avoidable delay. Get the form from the court where you are filing, not from a general website.

  • The court clerk. The clerk's office at the courthouse has the paper form and can tell you which version your court uses. Ask for the fee waiver application and any local instructions that go with it. The clerk can answer questions about the form and the filing process, but the clerk cannot give legal advice or tell you whether you qualify.
  • The court website. Most courts post their forms online, under a section called Forms, Self-Help, or Access to Justice. Download the form for your specific court and your specific kind of case. A form for family court is not the same as a form for small claims court.
  • State self-help centers. Many states run a self-help site with the fee waiver form, the instructions, and sometimes a guided interview that fills the form in for you. Your state's court website or your local legal aid office can point you to it.
  • Federal court. Federal courts use a standard form, Application to Proceed in District Court Without Prepaying Fees or Costs, often called Form AO 239. It is available from the clerk and from the federal courts website. Federal court also has a separate short form for prisoners filing under the Prison Litigation Reform Act, which has its own rules.

While you are getting the form, ask the clerk three things: whether the fee waiver application is filed with your case papers or before them, how long the court usually takes to decide it, and what the filing fee is if the waiver is denied. Those three answers shape your whole timeline.

Step by step: filling out the fee waiver form

  1. Read the whole form first. Before you write anything, read every section so you know what the court is asking for. Most forms ask for the same things: your income, your expenses, your assets, your debts, your household size, and whether you receive public benefits. Knowing the full list before you start keeps you from guessing at numbers halfway through.
  2. Gather your documents. Pull together your last two or three pay stubs, your most recent tax return, a benefits award letter if you receive public benefits, bank statements for the last month or two, and bills for rent, utilities, medical costs, and child care. The form asks for numbers. The documents are where the numbers come from.
  3. List all income, not just your job. Wages, self-employment income, tips, Social Security, disability payments, unemployment benefits, child support, spousal support, rental income, and any regular help from family. If income varies month to month, give the average and say so. Leaving out a source of income is the fastest way to lose credibility with the court.
  4. List your monthly expenses honestly. Rent or mortgage, utilities, food, transportation, insurance, medical bills, prescriptions, child care, and minimum payments on debts. Use real numbers from your bills, not round guesses. Necessary expenses are what the hardship test turns on, so be specific.
  5. List your assets and debts. Bank accounts and balances, vehicles and their value, real property, and any savings. Then list what you owe: credit cards, loans, medical debt, and any court-ordered payments. A car you need for work and a modest home are not automatic disqualifiers, but the court needs to see them.
  6. State your household size. Count everyone who lives with you and depends on the household income. If a child, a parent, or a partner lives with you and you support them, they count. If someone in the household has their own income, the form usually asks for that too.
  7. Attach your proof. A benefits award letter, recent pay stubs, and bank statements are the usual attachments. Do not attach originals. Bring copies, and keep the originals for yourself.
  8. Sign under penalty of perjury. The form ends with a declaration that the information is true. Read it before you sign. A false statement on a fee waiver form can lead to the waiver being revoked, the case being dismissed, and in some states a perjury charge. If you are not sure about a number, say so on the form instead of guessing.
  9. File it with the clerk. File the fee waiver application with your case papers, or before them if your court requires the waiver decision first. Ask the clerk whether you file the waiver and the case papers together or separately, and get a stamped copy of everything you file for your own records.

A real example: a parent filing for custody lists $1,900 a month in take-home pay, $1,100 in rent, $320 in utilities and food, $240 in child care, and $180 in medical bills. After necessary expenses, about $60 a month is left, and the filing fee is $300. That math, laid out plainly on the form with pay stubs and bills attached, is what a hardship grant is built on. The judge does not need a story. The judge needs the numbers.

Financial disclosure details

The fee waiver form is a financial disclosure, and the court reads it like one. A few details trip people up.

  • Gross pay is not take-home pay. The form usually asks for both. Gross pay is what you earn before taxes and deductions. Take-home pay is what lands in your account. If the form asks for one and you give the other, the numbers will not match your pay stubs, and the court will notice.
  • Irregular income still counts. Seasonal work, gig work, tips, and overtime that comes and goes are all income. Give a monthly average over the last few months and note that it varies. A month with no income does not mean your income is zero.
  • Help from family counts. Regular money from a parent, a partner, or anyone else that pays your bills is income for the form, even if it is not a wage. List it. The court would rather see it declared than discover it later.
  • Joint accounts and shared expenses. If you share a bank account or split rent with someone, say so and give your share. The form usually has a place for a spouse or household member's income. Fill it in even if that person is not a party to your case.
  • Do not hide an asset. A second vehicle, a savings account, a tax refund, or a pending insurance payment are all assets the form asks about. Hiding one can get the waiver revoked and the case dismissed, and it can be treated as a false statement under penalty of perjury. List it and explain why it does not mean you can afford the fee.
  • Keep a copy of everything. Copy the completed form, every attachment, and the stamped copy the clerk gives you back. If the court sets a hearing on your finances, you will be asked the same questions again, and your copy is what keeps your answers consistent.

What happens after you file

The court reviews the application and issues an order. There are three usual outcomes, and the timing varies by court from a few days to a few weeks.

  • Granted. The court waives the fee, in full or in part. Your case is filed and moves forward. Read the order: it says exactly which fees are waived and whether the waiver is full or partial. Keep the order with your case papers.
  • Denied. The court finds you can afford the fee. The order usually sets a deadline to pay, often 10 to 30 days. If you do not pay by that deadline, your case may not be filed, or it may be dismissed. Do not let the deadline pass while you decide what to do.
  • Hearing set. The court wants to ask you questions about your finances before deciding. The hearing is usually short, often 10 to 20 minutes, and it is not about your case. The judge asks about your income, your expenses, and your household. Bring your documents, answer directly, and do not guess at numbers you can look up on the spot.

In some courts the clerk grants routine waivers on the spot when the form shows public benefits or income below the line, and only the close calls go to a judge. In other courts every application goes to a judge. Ask the clerk how your court handles it, so you know whether to expect a quick answer or a wait.

While the waiver is pending, your case is usually on hold. Some courts will not file your papers until the waiver is decided. Others file them and hold the fee. Either way, file the waiver early. Filing it on the last day before a statute of limitations runs out is a risk you do not need.

If your request is denied

A denial is not the end of the case. It is a bill with a deadline, and you have options.

  • Pay the fee by the deadline. The simplest option, and the one that keeps the case moving. The order says how much and by when. Pay it, get a receipt, and keep the receipt with your case papers.
  • Ask for a payment plan. Many courts let you pay the filing fee in installments, often over 30 to 90 days. Ask the clerk whether your court offers an installment order and what form it needs. A payment plan keeps the case alive while you pay.
  • Ask the court to reconsider. If your finances changed since you filed, or if the court missed something on your form, some courts let you file a motion for reconsideration or a new application with updated numbers. Bring the new facts: a lost job, a new medical bill, a changed household. A second application with the same numbers and no new facts rarely changes the result.
  • Ask for a hearing. In some courts you can request a hearing on the denial, where you explain your finances to the judge in person. Bring your documents and be ready to walk through the numbers. A hearing is often the best chance to win a hardship argument that did not come across on paper.
  • Check legal aid. A legal aid office can review your denial, tell you whether the court applied the right test, and sometimes help you refile. Many legal aid offices help with fee waivers for free, and some will represent you in the underlying case as well.

Do not ignore a denial. An unpaid fee after a denial can mean your case is never filed or is dismissed, and a dismissal for failure to pay can cost you the chance to refile if a deadline passes in the meantime. Act before the payment deadline, even if the action is only asking the clerk what your options are.

State variation notes

Fee waiver rules are state law, and they differ in ways that matter. These are the common patterns, not a rule for any one state. Check your court's form and your state's court website for the version that applies to you.

  • California. Uses a standard statewide form, Request to Waive Court Fees, with a published income test tied to the federal poverty guidelines and an automatic grant for people receiving public benefits. The form is the same in every county.
  • New York. Poor person relief is requested by motion, often with a supporting affidavit, rather than on a single statewide form. The court has broad discretion, and the request is commonly made at the same time as the case is filed.
  • Texas. Uses a Statement of Inability to Afford Payment of Court Costs, a sworn statement that replaces the older affidavit of indigency. The other side can contest it, and the court can hold a hearing on the contest.
  • Illinois. Uses a statewide Application for Waiver of Court Fees, with a published income test and a hardship option. The form asks for a full picture of income, expenses, assets, and debts.
  • Federal court. Uses the standard in forma pauperis application, Form AO 239, and the test is whether you cannot pay the fee and still provide yourself and your dependents with the necessities of life. Prisoners filing in federal court have a separate process with partial payment rules.

Some states also waive fees for specific kinds of cases regardless of income, such as domestic violence protection orders. If your case is one of those, ask the clerk whether a separate waiver or exemption applies.

What not to do

  • Do not lie on the form. The form is signed under penalty of perjury. A false statement can get the waiver revoked, the case dismissed, and in some states a perjury charge. If you are not sure about a number, say so instead of guessing.
  • Do not leave income off the form. Tips, gig work, help from family, child support, and benefits all count. An omitted source that the court finds later destroys your credibility, even if the amount was small.
  • Do not use the wrong court's form. A form from another county or another state can be rejected outright, and the rejection can cost you a deadline. Get the form from the court where you are filing.
  • Do not file the waiver at the last minute. A waiver takes time to decide, and your case may not be filed until it is decided. Filing on the last day before a deadline runs out risks losing the case entirely.
  • Do not assume a waiver covers everything. Read the order. A partial grant still leaves fees to pay, and those fees have their own deadline.
  • Do not ignore a denial or a hearing notice. A denial comes with a payment deadline. A hearing notice comes with a date. Missing either one can end your case before it starts.
  • Do not spend money to look poorer. Moving money, giving away assets, or quitting a job to qualify is not a strategy. Courts look at your finances over time, and a sudden change right before filing invites a closer look.
  • Do not treat the waiver as a ruling on your case. A granted waiver says you cannot afford the fee. It says nothing about whether you will win. Prepare your case on its merits, not on the waiver.

When to get a lawyer or legal aid

Most people file a fee waiver on their own, and for a straightforward application that is normal. Get help when any of these apply:

  • Your waiver was denied and you do not understand why.
  • Your finances are complicated: self-employment, irregular income, shared household income, or assets the form does not clearly cover.
  • The other side is contesting your waiver, or the court set a hearing on it.
  • Your case itself is complex, and the fee is only the first hurdle.
  • You are filing in federal court or appealing, where the forms and the rules are stricter.
  • You missed a deadline tied to the waiver, and the case is at risk.

Start with legal aid. Most states have a legal aid organization that helps low-income people with civil cases for free, and many have a specific fee waiver practice. Your local bar association can also refer you to a lawyer for a low-cost consultation. Search for legal aid in your county, or call 211 in the United States to find legal help near you. Bring your completed form, your documents, and the denial order if you have one, so the lawyer can give you a real answer instead of a general one.

If you cannot afford a lawyer for the case itself, a fee waiver is often the first step toward legal aid, not the last. Many legal aid offices use the same financial information to decide whether they can take your case, so the work you do on the waiver form is work you will use again.

FAQ

What does in forma pauperis mean?

In forma pauperis is Latin for in the manner of a poor person. It is the legal term for asking a court to let you file a case without paying the filing fee because you cannot afford it. A court fee waiver is the same request in plain language. In federal court the request is usually called an in forma pauperis application, and in state court it is usually called a fee waiver application. This guide is general information, not legal advice.

Who qualifies for a court fee waiver?

It depends on your state and your court, but the two common paths are income and public benefits. Many courts grant a waiver if your household income is below a set level, often tied to the federal poverty guidelines, and many grant one automatically if you receive public benefits such as SNAP, SSI, TANF, or Medicaid. Some courts also grant one on hardship, when your income is above the line but your necessary expenses leave you unable to pay the fee. The court decides based on the financial information you put on the form, and you sign it under penalty of perjury.

What fees does a fee waiver cover?

A fee waiver usually covers the filing fee for your case, and often the fees for serving papers, court reporter fees, and fees for copies or transcripts tied to the case. It does not cover everything. It generally does not pay your lawyer, any expert witness, or the other side's costs if you lose. Some courts grant a full waiver and some grant a partial one, for example waiving the filing fee but not the service fee, so read the order when it comes back.

How do I apply for a court fee waiver?

Get the fee waiver form from the clerk of the court where you are filing, or from that court's website. Fill in your income, expenses, assets, debts, and household size, sign it under penalty of perjury, and file it with the clerk along with your case papers, or before them if the court requires the waiver first. The clerk or a judge reviews it and issues an order granting it, denying it, or setting a hearing to ask you questions about your finances.

What income limit qualifies for a fee waiver?

There is no single national income limit. Each state and court sets its own threshold, often tied to a percentage of the federal poverty guidelines, and the limit rises with household size. Some courts publish the exact numbers on their website or on the form itself. Others leave it to the judge to decide whether your income and expenses leave you unable to pay. If you receive public benefits such as SNAP, SSI, TANF, or Medicaid, many courts treat that as qualifying on its own. Check your court's form for its exact test before you file.

What happens if my fee waiver is denied?

A denial usually means you have to pay the filing fee by the deadline the court sets, or your case will not be filed or will be dismissed. Many courts offer a payment plan or an installment order so you can pay the fee over time. Some courts let you ask the judge to reconsider, or to hold a hearing where you explain your finances in person. If you disagree with the denial, ask the clerk what the options are in that court, and act before the payment deadline passes, because the deadline is usually short.

Can I get a fee waiver for an appeal?

Usually yes, but it is a separate request. A fee waiver granted in the trial court does not automatically carry over to an appeal. You file a new fee waiver application with the appeals court, often on a different form, and the appeals court decides it on its own. The same rules apply: your income, benefits, expenses, and household size. File the appeal fee waiver at the same time as your notice of appeal so you do not miss the appeal deadline while the waiver is pending.

Is this legal advice?

No. This guide is general information about how court fee waivers work. Fee waiver rules, forms, income limits, and deadlines vary by state and by court, and the right steps depend on your case and your finances. For anything that could affect your case, talk to a lawyer or a legal aid organization before you file.
