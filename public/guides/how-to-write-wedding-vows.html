How to Write Wedding Vows

The classic four-part structure, how long they should be, example lines worth stealing, and how to survive reading them aloud.

The Classic Four-Part Vow Structure

Nearly every great set of personal vows follows the same arc. 1. Opening: address your partner directly — their name, what today means. 2. Memories: one or two specific moments that capture the relationship (how you met, when you knew). 3. Promises: three to four concrete vows — the heart of the whole thing. 4. Close: a forward-looking line about the life you are choosing together.

Why Specificity Beats Poetry

Compare: "I promise to love you forever" versus "I promise to always save you the corner brownie, to laugh at your puns even the terrible ones, and to be the calm to your beautiful chaos." The first could be anyone’s vows. The second could only be yours — and your guests will remember it for years. Concrete promises ("I will learn to make your grandmother’s soup") beat abstract ones ("I will cherish you") every single time.

How Long Should Vows Be?

One to two minutes each when read aloud — roughly 150 to 300 words. Under a minute can feel abrupt; past three minutes, even loving guests drift. Most importantly, match your partner: agree on a target length together so nobody feels lopsided at the altar. Read your draft aloud with a timer — written words always take longer to say than you expect.

Example Lines Worth Stealing (Then Personalizing)

Opening: "Maya, from the moment we both reached for the same book, I knew my life had found its co-author."
Memory: "You nursed me through a broken ankle and a career change in the same year, and never once made me feel like a burden."
Promise: "I vow to choose curiosity over judgment when we disagree, and to always be on your team first."
Close: "Whatever comes next — the adventures and the ordinary Tuesdays — I want all of it, with you."

Notice every line contains a real detail. Steal the shapes, fill them with your own memories — that is the entire method.

Tone: Funny, Serious, or Both?

Match your relationship and your audience. A funny line or two inside heartfelt vows is the sweet spot for most couples — it breaks tension and sounds like you. Pure comedy risks feeling like a roast; pure solemnity can feel stiff if that is not who you are. Read the room: grandparents in the front row moderate the inside jokes, they do not eliminate them.

Delivery: Surviving the Reading

You will probably cry — plan for it. Print your vows in large type on cards (tears blur phone screens and small fonts). Pause, breathe, sip water, keep going; guests love the emotion. Practice aloud three times so the words feel familiar in your mouth. And make eye contact with your partner, not the paper — you are talking to them, not the audience.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do we have to write our own vows?

No — traditional vows are always an option and many couples choose them. Personal vows are a choice, not a requirement. But guests consistently say personal vows are the most memorable part of a ceremony.

Should we show each other our vows beforehand?

Most couples keep them secret for the surprise, but agree on length and tone in advance. Nothing deflates the moment like one partner reading a sonnet while the other reads four lines.

Can we include religious or cultural elements?

Absolutely — weaving in a tradition, a blessing, or a ritual that matters to your families makes vows richer. Just make sure both partners are comfortable with every element.

What if I am not a writer?

You do not need to be. Speak plainly, be specific, and say true things — that outperforms poetry every time. Our vow generator can give you a draft to react to, which is far easier than starting blank.

How far in advance should we write vows?

Start 4-6 weeks out, finish at least a week before. Writing under wedding-week stress produces either tears or clichés — neither is what you want to read aloud.
