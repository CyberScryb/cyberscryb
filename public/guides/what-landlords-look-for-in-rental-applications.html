What Landlords Look for in Rental Applications

The exact screening checklist landlords run on every application, how to strengthen each factor, and what they are legally not allowed to consider.

By CyberScryb 📅 Updated October 17, 2026 ⏱ 9 min read
⚡ Key Takeaways (TL;DR)

Landlords screen applications on six factors: credit history, income verification (many look for income around three times the monthly rent), rental history, employment stability, references, and background/eviction checks. You cannot rewrite the past, but you can strengthen every factor: document your income cleanly, bring references, explain weak spots honestly, and offer sweeteners like a larger deposit or a guarantor. Federal fair housing law bars landlords from considering race, color, national origin, religion, sex, familial status, or disability, and many states and cities add more protections. Criteria must be applied consistently to every applicant.

The Screening Checklist, in Order

Most landlords run the same six-part screen, whether it is a one-page form from a private owner or an automated report from a screening company like TransUnion SmartMove or RentSpree. Knowing the checklist lets you prepare your application like the person it is meant to impress.

1. Credit Score and Credit History

Landlords pull your credit to answer one question: do you pay your debts on time? They look at the score, but they also look at the story: late payments, collections, charge-offs, and outstanding debts. A low score caused by a thin file (young or new to credit) reads very differently from a low score caused by missed payments.

How to strengthen it: pull your own report before applying so there are no surprises, and dispute any factual errors (they are common on tenant screening reports). If your credit is thin or damaged, bring proof of on-time rent and utility payments, recent bank statements showing steady reserves, or an offer to pay a larger deposit. A short written explanation of what caused the problem and what changed helps, especially with private landlords. For the cover letter version of that explanation, see how to write a rental application letter.

2. Income Verification and the 3x Rent Rule

This is the make-or-break factor in most denials. Landlords verify income with recent pay stubs (usually two or three months), an employment letter confirming the job and salary, and sometimes a direct call to the employer. Self-employed applicants typically provide tax returns and bank statements.

The common norm is the 3x rent rule: gross monthly income around three times the monthly rent. On a $1,500 unit that means roughly $4,500 per month in income. It is a widespread rule of thumb, not a law, and some landlords use a lower multiple, count household income from all occupants, or use their own formula.

How to strengthen it: have your pay stubs, offer letter, or tax documents ready before you apply so there is no gap between the application and the proof. If your income is variable (tips, gig work, commissions), bring a longer history to show the average. If you fall short of the multiple, a guarantor or proof of substantial savings is the standard compensating factor.

3. Rental History

Landlords want to see where you lived, how long you stayed, and whether you paid on time and left the place in good shape. Long, stable tenancies signal reliability. They check this through screening reports, court records for evictions, and by calling your previous landlords directly.

Most screening looks back several years, and eviction records can show up for around seven years. The pattern matters more than any single data point: a single old eviction followed by years of clean history is a very different picture from a recent one. Landlords also ask former landlords whether you paid on time, whether there were complaints or damage, and whether they would rent to you again.

How to strengthen it: give your former landlords a heads-up that a call is coming and confirm their numbers. If you have no rental history (first-time renter), substitute employer letters, proof of savings, or a guarantor. If there is an eviction on your record, address it directly with context and evidence of what has changed since; landlords who hear your version first are more likely to keep reading.

4. Employment Stability

Landlords like tenants who stay put, and job-hopping looks like rent risk. What they want: a current job, a reasonable tenure (a year or more at the same employer is reassuring), and a career trajectory that supports the rent. Recent job changes are not automatic denials, especially if the new job pays more, but be ready to document them.

How to strengthen it: bring an employment letter on company letterhead confirming your role, start date, and salary. If you recently changed jobs, include the offer letter. New graduates can often substitute a job offer letter for employment history.

5. References

References are the human layer of screening. Landlords call previous landlords first, and may also contact personal or professional references. The questions they ask are predictable: did this person pay on time, take care of the property, cause complaints, and would you rent to them again?

How to strengthen it: list references who will actually pick up the phone. Warn them the call is coming and confirm their contact details. A written reference letter from a previous landlord, attached to your application, is stronger than a phone number alone; if a past landlord will vouch for you in writing, a letter of recommendation gives the landlord something concrete to read.

6. Background and Eviction Checks

Most screening reports include a criminal background check and an eviction history search. Landlords are mainly looking for recent evictions, serious financial judgments, and in some cases criminal history relevant to the safety of the property and other tenants. Rules on how criminal history can be used vary by state and locality; blanket bans are legally risky for landlords in many jurisdictions.

If a landlord denies you based on a third-party screening report, federal law (the Fair Credit Reporting Act) requires them to give you an adverse action notice naming the reason and the reporting agency, and you have the right to a free copy of the report and to dispute errors. That denial is not necessarily final: see how to appeal a housing denial.

How to strengthen it: run your own screening report first so nothing surprises you. Dispute factual errors in writing. If something real will show up, get ahead of it with context and mitigating evidence rather than hoping it goes unnoticed.

What Landlords May NOT Consider: Fair Housing

The federal Fair Housing Act prohibits landlords from discriminating based on race, color, national origin, religion, sex, familial status (having children), or disability. That means screening criteria must be applied consistently to every applicant: the same income multiple, the same credit cutoff, the same background standards.

Many states and cities add further protected characteristics, commonly including source of income (such as housing vouchers), age, sexual orientation and gender identity, and veteran status. In those jurisdictions a landlord cannot refuse you because you pay with a voucher or because of who you love.

Watch for red flags: different screening standards for different applicants, questions about your religion, family plans, or national origin, or sudden changes in the stated criteria after meeting you in person. If you suspect discrimination, you can file a complaint with HUD or your state or local fair housing agency.

Frequently Asked Questions

What credit score do landlords look for?

Many landlords look for scores in the 620 to 650 range as a rough minimum, and higher-end properties often want 700 or more, but there is no universal cutoff. Private landlords weigh the whole picture more than the number, while corporate complexes tend to use hard minimums set by their screening software.

How do landlords verify income?

The usual method is recent pay stubs, often two or three months' worth, sometimes combined with a letter from the employer confirming the job and salary. Self-employed applicants typically provide tax returns and bank statements. Many landlords also call the employer directly to confirm the job is real and current.

What is the 3x rent rule?

A common screening norm: the landlord wants your gross monthly income to be about three times the monthly rent. On a $1,500 apartment that means roughly $4,500 in monthly income. It is a widespread rule of thumb, not a law, and some markets and landlords use a lower multiple or a different formula.

Can a landlord deny me because of pets?

In most cases, yes. Landlords can generally refuse pets or restrict breeds and sizes as part of their screening criteria. The major exception is assistance animals: under federal fair housing law, landlords generally must allow reasonable accommodations for service animals and emotional support animals, and pet fees or deposits typically cannot be charged for them.

How far back do landlords check rental history?

Most screening reports and landlord references cover the last few years of rental history, and eviction records can show up for around seven years. What matters most is the pattern: long, stable tenancies with on-time rent beat short stays, and a single old eviction with years of clean history since is very different from a recent one.

What can a landlord NOT consider when screening applicants?

Under the federal Fair Housing Act, landlords may not discriminate based on race, color, national origin, religion, sex, familial status, or disability. Many states and cities add more protected characteristics, such as source of income, age, or sexual orientation and gender identity. Screening criteria must be applied consistently to every applicant.

This guide is general information for educational purposes only and is not legal advice. Screening standards and fair housing protections vary by state and locality; confirm local rules before applying or screening.
