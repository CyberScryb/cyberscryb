How to Pick One

Match the tone to the relationship first. A coworker gets the formal, brief section. A close friend gets the warm, personal one. Match the situation second: a sudden loss and a long illness read differently, and getting that wrong is what makes a message feel off. Then add one detail only you know, a name, a memory, a trait, because that is what turns a template into a message. Everything below is ready to copy, paste, and adjust. Short Card Lines (copy-ready one and two liners)

  1. I am so sorry for your loss. Thinking of you and your family.
  2. Please accept my sincere condolences on the passing of [Name].
  3. Wishing you strength and comfort during this difficult time.
  4. [Name] will be deeply missed. I am so sorry.
  5. Holding you and your family in my thoughts.
  6. My heart goes out to you. So sorry for your loss.
  7. Sending love and sympathy to you and yours.
  8. No words can take away the pain, but please know I am thinking of you.
  9. May the memories of [Name] bring you some comfort.
  10. I was saddened to hear the news. With deepest sympathy.
  11. Gone from our sight, but never from our hearts.
  12. I am here for you, today and in the weeks ahead.

Messages to a Friend

  1. I am heartbroken for you. [Name] was one of a kind, and I will miss [him/her] too. I am bringing dinner Thursday, no need to answer the door.
  2. I do not have the right words, so I will just say this: I love you, and I am not going anywhere. Call me at 2 AM if you need to.
  3. I am so sorry about [Name]. I will always remember [him/her] [one memory], and I am so glad our lives overlapped. Here for whatever you need.
  4. This is unfair and I know it. I am here to listen, to sit in silence, to bring coffee, or to handle the stuff you do not have energy for. You tell me.
  5. I keep thinking about [Name] and about you. Grief has no timeline, so take all of it you need. I will be checking in, no reply required.
  6. I wish I could take some of this weight off you. Since I cannot, I am doing the practical stuff instead: laundry, groceries, rides. Just say the word.

Coworker Messages

  1. I was very sorry to hear about your [relationship]. Please accept my sincere condolences to you and your family.
  2. Thinking of you during this difficult time. I have your projects covered, so take whatever time you need.
  3. I am so sorry for your loss. We are all here for you, and there is no rush to come back until you are ready.
  4. Please accept my heartfelt sympathy on the passing of your [relationship]. If you need anything at all, my door is open.
  5. I heard about [Name] and wanted you to know the whole team is thinking of you. Take the time you need, we have got everything handled.
  6. Condolences on your loss. [Name] sounded like a wonderful person from everything you shared. Wishing you and your family peace.

Faith-Based Messages (use only when you know the faith is shared)

  1. Praying for you and your family. May you feel God's comfort surrounding you in this season of loss.
  2. "Blessed are those who mourn, for they will be comforted." (Matthew 5:4) Holding you in prayer.
  3. May [Name] rest in peace, and may your faith bring you strength in the days ahead.
  4. I am praying that the Lord gives you peace that passes understanding right now. So sorry for your loss.
  5. Trusting that [Name] is in the arms of God, free from pain. You and your family are in my prayers.
  6. May the God of all comfort be near to you and your family. Thinking of you and lifting you up in prayer.

Loss of a Parent

  1. I am so sorry about your [mom/dad]. Losing a parent changes everything, and I am thinking of you.
  2. Your [mom/dad] raised someone I am proud to call my friend. [He/She] clearly did something right. I am so sorry.
  3. I never met your [mom/dad], but from everything you have told me, [he/she] was remarkable. Holding you in my thoughts.
  4. There is no age where losing a parent gets easy. I am here, for the hard days and the ordinary ones that sneak up on you.
  5. I am so sorry. I hope the good memories start to outweigh the pain sooner rather than later, and I will be here until they do.
  6. Your [dad/mom] had that rare gift of making everyone feel welcome. I will never forget [one memory]. Deepest sympathy to you and your family.

Loss of a Child

  1. There are no words, so I will not try to find them. I am so deeply sorry, and I am here.
  2. I am heartbroken for you and [partner]. [Name] mattered, and [he/she] always will. I will say [his/her] name with you whenever you want.
  3. I cannot imagine what you are going through, and I will not pretend to. I am here to listen, to help, or to just sit with you.
  4. I am so sorry. No parent should ever have to write this sentence, and no parent should ever have to read it. Thinking of your family constantly.
  5. [Name] was loved, and that love does not end here. I am bringing meals all next week, and I will keep showing up long after everyone else goes quiet.

Loss of a Spouse or Partner

  1. I am so sorry about [Name]. The two of you had something rare, and I admired it. I am here for whatever the coming weeks bring.
  2. Losing your person is losing your everyday. I am thinking of you, and I will keep checking in long after the casseroles stop coming.
  3. I will miss [Name] too. [He/She] had a way of [one trait] that made every room better. You do not have to go through this alone.
  4. I am heartbroken for you. Take everything one day at a time, and let me handle the practical stuff: rides, errands, paperwork, whatever.
  5. [Name] loved you fiercely, and that was obvious to everyone who knew you both. I am so sorry, and I am not going anywhere.

Pet Loss

  1. I am so sorry about [Name]. [He/She] was a great [dog/cat], and I know how much [he/she] meant to you.
  2. Losing a pet is losing a family member. I am thinking of you, and I am sorry you are going through this.
  3. [Name] had the best life with you, and that matters. So sorry for your loss.
  4. I will miss seeing [Name] [one memory]. [He/She] was one of the good ones. Here if you want to talk.
  5. Some animals are just family, and [Name] was definitely that. I am so sorry. Take all the time you need to grieve.

Sudden or Unexpected Loss

  1. I am in shock hearing about [Name]. I cannot imagine what you are feeling. I am so, so sorry, and I am here.
  2. This is devastating news, and I do not have words big enough for it. Please let me help with anything practical in the coming days.
  3. I am stunned and heartbroken for you. [Name] was [one trait], and this loss is enormous. I will be checking in.
  4. I do not know what to say except that I am here, I am sorry, and you do not have to be strong around me.
  5. My heart broke when I heard. Take this one hour at a time. I am handling [specific practical help] so you do not have to think about it.

After a Long Illness

  1. I am so sorry about [Name]. I know this road has been long, and I hope there is some comfort in knowing [he/she] is at peace now.
  2. You gave so much of yourself caring for [Name], and that love was evident to everyone. I am thinking of you.
  3. I am sorry for your loss. The grief after a long illness is its own complicated thing, and whatever you are feeling is okay.
  4. [Name] fought hard, and so did you. I hope you can rest now too. I am here, and I will keep showing up.
  5. May the end of [Name]'s suffering bring you some small comfort. I am so sorry, and I am thinking of your family.

Distant Acquaintance

  2. Thinking of you and your family during this difficult time.

  4. My sincere sympathy to you and yours. [Name] will be missed.

What Not to Write

Even the best example fails if it contains one of these. Strike them from any message you send:

  • "I know how you feel." You do not, and claiming otherwise can make a grieving person feel unheard.
  • "Everything happens for a reason." To someone in fresh grief, this minimizes the loss.
  • "They are in a better place." Only safe when you know the person shares the faith behind it. Otherwise it can sound dismissive.
  • "Stay strong" or "be strong." This asks them to perform composure for your comfort.
  • Any advice. "You should take time off," "at least she is not suffering." Grief is not a problem to solve in a card.
  • Your own loss stories. "When my uncle died" turns their grief into a story about you. Keep the focus on them.
  • "What happened?" Never ask for details, and never mention a cause of death the family has not shared publicly.
  • "Let me know if you need anything." It puts the work on them. Name something specific instead.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I sign a sympathy card?

Sign the card the way the person knows you. Use your full name for formal relationships, your first name for friends. A short warm line before the name works well: "Thinking of you," "With love," or "With deepest sympathy," followed by your name.

Is a short sympathy message okay, or does it look like I do not care?

A short message is fine and often better. Two or three sincere sentences are a complete condolence. Long, rambling messages can overwhelm a grieving person. What matters is that you reached out, not how many words you used.

What should I not write in a sympathy card?

Avoid "I know how you feel," "everything happens for a reason," "they are in a better place" (unless you know faith talk is welcome), "stay strong," any advice, and any comparison to your own losses. Do not ask what happened or mention an unannounced cause of death.

Is it okay to text a sympathy message instead of sending a card?

Yes. A text is a perfectly acceptable sympathy message today, especially for coworkers, neighbors, and acquaintances. A handwritten card carries more weight for close relationships, but a sincere text always beats silence.

Can I personalize one of these examples?

You should. Swap in the person's name, your relationship to them, and one real detail, a memory or a trait. That single personal touch turns a generic line into a message that actually lands.
