How to Prepare for a VA Disability Board Hearing

Your VA disability claim was denied and you asked the Board for a hearing. What the three docket options mean, the evidence that matters, and how to walk in prepared.

By CyberScryb 📅 Updated November 14, 2026 ⏱ 11 min read

You get a hearing by filing a Notice of Disagreement, VA Form 10182, and picking the hearing docket, one of three docket options, and the choice matters because you generally cannot switch later. What a VA disability Board hearing is

The Board of Veterans' Appeals is the part of the Department of Veterans Affairs that reviews appeals of VA disability claim decisions. When the VA regional office denies your claim, or grants a lower rating than you believe is right, you can appeal to the Board. The Board hearing is the last step in that appeal where you get to speak to the person deciding your case.

The hearing is run by a Veterans Law Judge, often called a VLJ. The judge reviews your claim file, asks you questions, listens to your testimony and your representative, and later issues a written decision. The hearing is informal and non-adversarial. There is no VA lawyer on the other side arguing that your claim should be denied. That is the biggest difference between a Board hearing and a courtroom hearing, and it changes how you prepare: you are not fighting an opponent, you are giving the judge the facts and evidence the file is missing.

You do not get a Board hearing automatically. You ask for one when you appeal, by filing a Notice of Disagreement, VA Form 10182, within one year of the rating decision you are appealing. On that form you pick one of three docket options, and the hearing option is the one this guide covers. If you already filed your appeal and picked a different docket, talk to your representative about what options you still have, because you generally cannot switch dockets later.

The three Board docket options

The docket you pick on the Notice of Disagreement decides how the Board handles your appeal. Pick before you file, and talk to an accredited representative first, because the choice is hard to undo.

  • Direct Review. The judge decides based only on the evidence already in your claim file. No new evidence, no hearing. This is the fastest docket, and it fits when the file already has everything the judge needs and the denial looks like a mistake on the existing record.
  • Evidence Submission. You can send new evidence within 90 days after the Board receives your appeal, but there is no hearing. This fits when the file is missing a key record or a medical opinion, and you can get it quickly.
  • Hearing. You get a hearing before a Veterans Law Judge, and you can send new evidence within 90 days after the hearing. This is the slowest docket by a wide margin, and it fits when your testimony matters, when the file has gaps that only you can explain, or when the case turns on how the condition affects your daily life in ways the records do not show.

The hearing docket is the right pick when the story behind the records is the case. It is the wrong pick when the file is already complete and you just want a faster decision. If you are not sure which docket fits, a VSO representative can read your file and tell you, for free.

The hearing types

A Board hearing comes in three formats. The Board assigns the format, and your hearing notice says which one you have.

  • Virtual tele-hearing. A hearing by video from your home, or from a VA facility, using a video connection the Board provides. This is now the most common format. You need a quiet room, a stable connection, and a device with a camera and microphone. Test the connection before the hearing day, and have a phone number ready as a backup in case the video drops.
  • Video hearing. A hearing by video from a VA regional office or another VA facility near you, while the judge sits in Washington, DC. You go to the facility, sit in a hearing room, and talk to the judge on a screen. A VA employee is usually in the room with you to run the equipment.
  • In-person hearing. A hearing in person at the Board's offices in Washington, DC, with the judge in the room. This is the least common format now. You travel to DC at your own expense unless your representative arranges otherwise, so ask about travel costs before you request it.

The format does not change the standard the judge applies. A virtual hearing is not a lesser hearing. What matters is the evidence and your testimony, not the room you sit in.

The timeline that leads to a hearing

Knowing where you are in the timeline matters, because the wait is long and the deadlines inside it are strict.

  1. The rating decision. The VA regional office decides your claim and sends a rating decision. Read it the day it arrives. It says what was granted or denied, the rating assigned, the evidence the VA considered, and the reasons for the decision. The reasons section tells you exactly what evidence was missing.
  2. The one-year appeal window. You have one year from the date of the rating decision to file the Notice of Disagreement, VA Form 10182. Miss it and the decision becomes final, and you would have to file a new claim or a Supplemental Claim with new evidence. File early, not at the deadline.
  3. The Board receives your appeal. The Board dockets your appeal in the order received. The hearing docket moves slower than the other two, and the wait is commonly measured in years. Use the wait. Gather records, get a medical opinion, and write your timeline while you have time.
  4. The hearing notice. The Board sends a notice with the date, time, and format of your hearing. Keep your address current with the VA at all times, and read every notice the day it arrives. If the date does not work, contact the Board and your representative right away to ask about rescheduling, and do it in writing.
  5. The hearing. You testify, the judge asks questions, your representative makes your case, and the hearing is recorded. The judge does not decide on the spot.
  6. The 90-day evidence window. After a hearing, you have 90 days to send any new evidence. Get it in before the window closes, because evidence sent late may not be considered.
  7. The written decision. The judge issues a written decision that grants the claim, denies it, or remands it for more development. The decision is mailed to you and your representative.

The deadlines that matter

VA deadlines are strict, and missing one can end an appeal that would otherwise have won. Confirm every date against your own VA notices and with your representative.

  • One year to appeal. The Notice of Disagreement must reach the VA within one year of the rating decision. Mark the date the decision arrives and file early.
  • 90 days after the hearing. New evidence is due within 90 days after the hearing date. If your medical opinion or records are not ready by the hearing, you still have this window, but do not count on it. Get the evidence ready before the hearing when you can.
  • 120 days to appeal a denial. If the Board denies your claim, you generally have 120 days to appeal to the U.S. Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims. The deadline is printed in the decision. Mark it the day the decision arrives, and talk to a representative immediately.
  • Keeping your address current. The VA sends hearing notices and decisions to the address on file. If you move and do not tell the VA, the hearing still counts, and you can miss a hearing you never knew about. Update your address the day you move, and keep proof that you filed the change.
  • Responding to remands. If the Board remands your claim for more development, the regional office will ask for a new exam or more records. Attend every exam and answer every request. A missed exam can be used against your claim.

The evidence and documentation checklist

Build your file before the hearing, organized by date, so the judge can follow it without your narration. The hearing is your chance to add what the file is missing, so bring the strongest records you have. If a document is not in English, it needs an English translation, and the translator must sign a certificate saying the translation is complete and accurate.

  • Your VA claim file. The rating decision you are appealing, the Notice of Disagreement, and every VA notice since. These prove where your claim stands and what the VA already decided.
  • Service treatment records. Your medical records from service, including sick call visits, hospital records, and any treatment for the condition you are claiming. If a record is missing from your file, request it from the National Personnel Records Center early, because the request can take months.
  • Service personnel records. Your DD214, performance reports, awards, and any incident reports or line-of-duty determinations that show what happened during service. These prove the event or exposure behind the claim.
  • Current medical records. Records from your VA doctors and any private doctors treating the condition now, including diagnoses, treatment notes, prescriptions, and test results. Current treatment shows the condition is ongoing and how severe it is.
  • A medical opinion or nexus letter. A written opinion from a doctor that connects your current condition to your service. The strongest opinions say the connection is at least as likely as not, explain the reasoning, cite the records the doctor reviewed, and describe the doctor's qualifications. A one-line letter with no reasoning carries little weight. Ask your representative what your claim needs.
  • Lay statements and buddy statements. Written, signed, dated statements from people who know your story: family who see how the condition affects you day to day, friends or coworkers who have watched you miss work or struggle with tasks, and fellow service members who were there when the injury or event happened. Include full names, addresses, and phone numbers. A witness who testifies at the hearing is stronger than a letter, but a detailed letter is far better than no witness at all.
  • Your own statement. A written account of your condition, in date order, that matches your claim and your testimony exactly. What happened in service, when symptoms started, how they have changed, and what a bad day looks like now. Write it with your representative. If your statement, your records, and your testimony disagree on a date or a detail, the judge will notice.
  • Work and daily-life records. Pay stubs that show missed days, letters from employers, performance reviews that mention the condition, disability paperwork from work, and any records of jobs lost or hours cut. For a higher rating, these records prove how the condition limits your ability to work.
  • VA exam reports. The Compensation and Pension exam reports already in your file. Read them before the hearing. If an exam got a fact wrong, or the examiner did not review a record, note it and tell your representative, because the judge reads those reports closely.
  • Your timeline. A one-page list of dates: when you entered service, when the injury or event happened, when symptoms started, when you first sought treatment, when you filed the claim, and when each decision arrived. Dates are the backbone of a VA claim, and a clear timeline is easier for a judge to follow than a stack of loose papers.

Preparing your testimony

You will testify at the hearing, under oath, about your service, your condition, and how it limits your life. The best preparation is to write your story down before the hearing, in date order, with the document behind each fact. If a sentence has no date and no document behind it, cut it or find the document. Practice telling the story with your representative, out loud, more than once.

  1. Start with your service. When you served, where, your job, and what happened. Name the event, the place, and the date. Keep it to what happened, in order.
  2. Then when symptoms started. When you first noticed the condition, what it felt like, and whether you sought treatment in service. If you did not seek treatment, have the honest reason ready. Many veterans tough it out, and the judge has heard that before, but a reason is defensible and silence is not.
  3. Then your life now. A typical day, a bad day, and a good day. What you can no longer do, what you avoid, what help you need. Be specific. "My back hurts" is a sentence. "I cannot stand for more than ten minutes, so I cook sitting down and I stopped mowing the lawn" is evidence.
  4. Then work. What the condition has cost you at work: missed days, tasks you cannot do, jobs you lost or turned down, hours cut. Bring the pay stubs and employer letters that back it up.
  5. End with what you are asking for. The benefit or rating you are seeking, in one sentence.

Practice answering the hard questions before the hearing, because the judge will ask them:

  • Inconsistencies. If your claim, your statement, and your testimony disagree on a date, a place, or a detail, the judge will ask about it. Check every date against your documents before the hearing, and if you genuinely do not remember a detail, say so instead of guessing.
  • Gaps in treatment. If there are years with no treatment records, have the honest reason ready: no insurance, no VA clinic nearby, you were told to live with it, you did not know you could file. A reason is defensible. No reason looks like the condition was not there.
  • Other causes. The judge may ask about injuries or conditions after service that could explain the symptoms. Have the facts ready, in date order, and do not guess. If a later injury is unrelated, say so plainly and say why.
  • Your worst days. The rating depends on how bad the condition gets, not how good it is on a good day. Describe your worst days honestly. Do not exaggerate, and do not minimize. Judges trust witnesses who concede the small points, and distrust witnesses who deny everything.

Answer the question asked, then add the context. Do not guess at dates, places, or names. If you do not remember, say so. If you do not understand the question, say so and ask for it to be repeated. This is especially important on a video or phone hearing, where a misunderstood question can produce a wrong answer that looks like a lie.

What happens at the hearing

The hearing is smaller and less formal than a courtroom, and everything you say is under oath and recorded.

The judge calls the case and states what is being decided. The judge reviews your file and asks you questions about your service, your condition, and how it limits your daily life and work. Your representative can question you to bring out the important facts, and can make a short argument for your claim. There is no VA lawyer on the other side. The judge may ask questions at any point, and may interrupt to clarify a date or a fact. A hearing usually runs under an hour, though complex cases run longer. The judge does not decide on the spot. A written decision comes later, by mail.

A few things that help in the room:

  • Arrive early and dress neatly. For a video or in-person hearing, arrive at least 30 minutes early. For a tele-hearing from home, log in early and test your connection. Dress as you would for a job interview. First impressions are real, and the judge is watching.
  • Address the judge as Your Honor. Speak clearly, and wait for the judge to finish before you answer.
  • Answer the question asked. If the judge asks a yes-or-no question, answer it, then add the context. Rambling answers lose the judge's attention and your credibility.
  • Do not interrupt. When the judge is speaking, write down what you disagree with and raise it when it is your turn.
  • Admit what is true. If a date in your claim is wrong, say so and correct it. If you do not remember, say so. Judges decide credibility fast, and the calm side usually wins it.
  • Bring the documents, not just the story. If you say you missed twenty days of work, have the pay stubs in your hand. If you say you were treated on a date, have the record. The judge will not wait while you search your phone.
  • Turn your phone off. For a tele-hearing, silence every other device and close every other app. A notification in the middle of your testimony breaks the record and your train of thought.

Hearing-day logistics

The practical details matter, because a missed hearing can mean a denied appeal.

  • Confirm the date, time, and format. Read your hearing notice the day it arrives and again the day before the hearing. Times and formats change. If anything is unclear, call your representative or the Board and confirm.
  • Plan to arrive early. VA facilities have security screening, and lines can be long. Arrive at least 30 minutes early, and allow extra time if you are traveling from far away. If you are delayed, call your representative and the Board as soon as you can.
  • For a tele-hearing, pick the room in advance. A quiet room with a door that closes, good light on your face, and nothing distracting behind you. Test the video link the day before with the same device and the same connection you will use. Keep the Board's phone number and your representative's number on paper next to you in case the video drops.
  • Bring identification. Bring your hearing notice and a photo ID. For a video hearing at a VA facility, you will need both to get in the building.
  • Arrange childcare. Children are generally not allowed in the hearing room, and a child who needs your attention will distract you from the hearing. Arrange childcare for the day, and bring only the people who need to be there.
  • Do not bring food, and leave weapons at home. VA facilities do not allow food in the hearing room, and weapons are prohibited in the building. Bring water and a snack for the wait outside the room, and eat before you go in.
  • Know the building rules. Most VA facilities have security screening. Leave anything that could be taken for a weapon at home, and expect your bags to be searched.

Working with a VSO, agent, or attorney

You can represent yourself at a Board hearing, but most veterans do not, and the representative usually costs nothing up front. The options:

  • Veterans Service Organization representative. Accredited representatives from organizations like the DAV, VFW, American Legion, and Paralyzed Veterans of America help for free. They read your file, spot the gaps, gather records, write the appeal, and speak at the hearing. You do not need to be a member of the organization to get help. This is the first place to start.
  • Accredited agent. An individual accredited by the VA to represent veterans. Agents may charge a fee, usually only if they win, often a percentage of past-due benefits. Get the fee in writing before you sign anything.
  • Accredited attorney. A lawyer accredited by the VA to handle disability claims. Like agents, attorneys usually charge only if they win, often a percentage of past-due benefits. Ask what the fee covers: the appeal, the hearing, and any further appeal. Some attorneys charge for costs like medical records on top of the percentage.

Whoever you choose, check that they are accredited by the VA before you sign a power of attorney. The VA keeps a list of accredited representatives, agents, and attorneys. If someone asks for a large fee up front, promises a result, or is not on the VA's list, walk away. Bring your rating decision, your Notice of Disagreement, and your timeline to the first meeting, so the representative can give you a real answer instead of a general one.

Costs, fees, and what help actually costs

A VA disability appeal can be expensive, but the help that matters most is free, and the cost of losing a claim you could have won is usually higher than the cost of getting help.

  • Filing the appeal: there is no fee to file a Notice of Disagreement or to get a Board hearing.
  • VSO representative: free. This is the best deal in the system, and it costs nothing.
  • Agent or attorney: usually a percentage of past-due benefits, only if they win, and the percentage is capped by law. Get the fee agreement in writing before you sign, and ask what it covers and whether costs like medical records are extra.
  • Medical opinions: a private doctor's nexus letter can cost several hundred dollars or more, and the VA does not pay for it. Ask your representative whether your claim needs one before you pay, because not every claim does.
  • Medical records: private doctors and hospitals may charge per page for copies. Budget for this early, and ask your representative whether the VA can get the records for you.
  • Travel: if your hearing is in person in Washington, DC, you pay your own travel unless your representative arranges otherwise. A tele-hearing or video hearing costs nothing to attend.
  • Your time: the preparation, the hearing, and the follow-up. The hearing itself usually runs under an hour, but the preparation is the real work, and it is time well spent next to a denial that could have been avoided.

When to get a representative

You are allowed to represent yourself at a Board hearing, but the claim file is thick, the rules are detailed, and the stakes are years of benefits. Most veterans with a hearing date should talk to an accredited representative, and the consultation with a VSO is free. Get a representative if any of these apply:

  • You have a hearing date and do not have a representative yet.
  • You are not sure which docket to pick, because the choice is hard to undo.
  • Your claim was denied for lack of a nexus, which means the VA says the condition is not connected to your service, because that is the hardest denial to beat alone.
  • Your claim involves multiple conditions, a secondary condition, or an increased rating, because the evidence requirements get complicated fast.
  • You have a prior denial, a prior Board decision, or a remand, because the history changes what the judge needs to see.
  • You do not understand the reasons in your rating decision.
  • You missed a deadline, because the claim may still be fixable if you act fast.
  • Someone has offered to help you for a fee and you are not sure they are accredited by the VA.

If you cannot afford an attorney, start with a VSO. Bring your rating decision, every VA notice, and your timeline, so the representative can give you a real answer instead of a general one.

What happens after the decision

The judge issues a written decision after the hearing. There are three possible outcomes, and each one has its own next step.

  • Grant. The Board grants the claim or the higher rating. The claim goes back to the regional office to implement the decision and pay benefits. Check the effective date and the rating in the decision, because errors happen, and your representative can challenge them.
  • Deny. The Board denies the claim. You generally have 120 days to appeal to the U.S. Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims. Talk to your representative the day the decision arrives, because the appeal is usually decided on the hearing record, not a new hearing, which is why the record you built at the hearing matters so much.
  • Remand. The Board sends the claim back to the regional office for more development, such as a new exam, a medical opinion, or more records. A remand is not a denial. Attend every exam and answer every request, because the claim comes back to the Board after the development is done.

If the Board denies your claim, do not assume the case is over. A representative can tell you whether an appeal to the Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims, a new Supplemental Claim with new evidence, or a Higher-Level Review fits your case, and each option has its own deadline.

Mistakes that backfire

  • Missing the one-year appeal deadline. A late Notice of Disagreement means the rating decision becomes final. File early, not at the deadline, and keep proof that you filed.
  • Picking the wrong docket. The hearing docket is the slowest, and you generally cannot switch later. Pick the docket that fits the evidence you have, not the one that sounds best. Talk to a representative before you file.
  • Missing the hearing. If you do not show up, the judge can decide the case on the file alone, without your testimony. Keep your address current with the VA, read every notice the day it arrives, and never assume a hearing was moved unless the Board tells you in writing.
  • Not updating your address. The VA sends notices to the address on file. If you move and do not tell the VA, the hearing still counts. Update your address the day you move.
  • Exaggerating or minimizing. One contradiction, one statement that does not match the records, and the judge doubts everything else you say. Describe your worst days honestly, and your good days honestly too.
  • Inconsistencies between your papers and your testimony. If your claim, your statement, and your testimony disagree on a date or a detail, the judge will find it. Check every date against your documents before the hearing.
  • Skipping VA exams. A missed Compensation and Pension exam can be used against your claim. Attend every exam, and tell your representative if an exam got a fact wrong.
  • Bringing a story instead of a file. "My back is bad" is not evidence. The service record, the current treatment record, the medical opinion, and the lay statement are. If it is not written down, dated, and in your file, the judge may never hear it.
  • Paying an unaccredited helper. Only an accredited representative, agent, or attorney should give you advice or file papers for you. If someone asks for a large fee up front and promises a result, walk away.
  • Talking about the claim on social media. Assume the VA can see it. Do not post about your claim, your hearing, or your symptoms, and do not post anything that contradicts what you told the Board.

FAQ

What is a VA disability Board hearing?

A VA disability Board hearing is a hearing before a Veterans Law Judge at the Board of Veterans' Appeals, the part of the Department of Veterans Affairs that reviews appeals of VA disability claim decisions. You can ask for a hearing when you appeal a rating decision by filing a Notice of Disagreement, VA Form 10182, and choosing the hearing docket. The hearing is informal and non-adversarial. There is no VA representative arguing against you. The judge asks questions about your claim, listens to your testimony, and later issues a written decision. This guide is general information, not legal advice.

What are the three Board docket options?

When you appeal to the Board you pick one of three dockets. Direct Review means the judge decides based on the evidence already in your file, with no new evidence and no hearing. Evidence Submission means you can send new evidence within 90 days after the Board receives your appeal, but there is no hearing. Hearing means you get a hearing before a Veterans Law Judge, and you can send new evidence within 90 days after the hearing. You pick the docket on the Notice of Disagreement, and you generally cannot switch later, so talk to an accredited representative before you file.

How long does it take to get a VA Board hearing?

The hearing docket is the slowest of the three Board dockets. Veterans commonly wait years from the appeal to the hearing date, and the wait varies by docket and by the Board's workload. The Board sends a hearing notice with the date, time, and format. Keep your address current with the VA so the notice reaches you, and use the wait to gather evidence and prepare your testimony rather than waiting until the notice arrives.

Do I need a representative for a VA Board hearing?

You can represent yourself, but most veterans use a representative. Accredited Veterans Service Organization representatives, such as those from the DAV, VFW, or American Legion, help for free. Accredited agents and attorneys can also represent you, and they usually charge a fee only if they win, often a percentage of past-due benefits. Ask any representative to put the fee in writing before you sign anything, and check that they are accredited by the VA.

What evidence should I bring to a VA Board hearing?

Bring your service treatment records, your private medical records, the VA exams already in your file, any medical opinion or nexus letter that connects your condition to your service, lay statements from family, friends, or fellow service members who saw what happened or see how the condition affects you now, and work records that show missed days or lost jobs. Organize everything by date. The hearing is your chance to add evidence the file is missing, so bring the strongest records you have, not every page you own.

What happens at a VA Board hearing?

The hearing is informal. A Veterans Law Judge runs it, usually by video or phone, and there is no VA lawyer arguing the other side. The judge reviews your file, asks you questions about your service, your condition, and how it limits your daily life and work, and lets you and your representative make your case. Your representative can question you to bring out the important facts. The hearing is recorded. The judge does not decide on the spot. A written decision comes later.

What happens after a VA Board hearing?

After the hearing, the judge issues a written decision that grants the claim, denies it, or remands it, which means sending it back to the regional office for more development, such as a new exam or more records. If the Board denies your claim, you generally have 120 days to appeal to the U.S. Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims. If the Board remands, the claim goes back for more work and then returns to the Board. Your representative can explain what the decision means for your claim.

Is this legal advice?

No. This guide is general information about how VA disability Board hearings work. VA rules, deadlines, and procedures change, and the right steps depend on your individual claim. For anything that could affect your claim, talk to an accredited Veterans Service Organization representative, an accredited agent, or an accredited attorney before your hearing date.
