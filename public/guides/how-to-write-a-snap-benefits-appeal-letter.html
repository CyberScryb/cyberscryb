How to Write a SNAP Benefits Appeal Letter

Denied, cut, or terminated SNAP benefits are not the final word. A written appeal letter asks for a fair hearing, and if you send it fast enough your benefits usually keep coming while the appeal is pending. Here is the deadline that matters, the evidence that changes decisions, the exact structure, and a full sample letter.

What a SNAP appeal is

SNAP, the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program, is the federal food assistance program, run by each state's agency. When that agency denies your application, reduces your monthly amount, ends your benefits, or takes too long to decide, you have the right to ask for a fair hearing. A fair hearing is a review by a hearing officer who was not involved in your case. The officer looks at the agency's reason, looks at your evidence, and issues a written decision.

The appeal letter is how you ask for that hearing. It does not have to be long or written in legal language. It has to do three things: identify your case, name the decision you are appealing, and ask for a fair hearing. The hearing itself is where the argument happens. The letter is what gets you in the door, and the deadline on it is the part that matters most.

The two deadlines that matter

There are two different deadlines, and confusing them is the most common way people lose an appeal before it starts.

  • The 90-day hearing deadline. Federal rules generally give you 90 days from the date of the notice to request a fair hearing. This is the deadline for asking at all, and it covers denials, reductions, terminations, and delays. Your notice states the exact deadline and where to send the request. If the notice says something different from 90 days, follow the notice.
  • The 10-day deadline to keep your benefits going. If your benefits are being reduced or ended and you want them to continue at the current level while the appeal is pending, you usually have to request the hearing within the advance notice period, generally 10 days after the notice was mailed. This is called aid pending, or continued benefits. If you miss this shorter window, you can still appeal within 90 days, but your benefits will stop or drop while you wait for the hearing.

The practical rule is simple: send the appeal the day you get the notice. Do not wait to gather documents first. You can send the letter now and add evidence later, before the hearing. The 10-day window is short, and the mail is slow.

Why SNAP benefits get denied, reduced, or cut

The notice you received should state the agency's reason. Read it carefully, because the reason decides what evidence you need. The most common reasons fall into a few groups.

  • Income counted wrong. The agency counted income you do not actually receive, counted gross pay instead of what you take home after a change, or counted a one-time payment as regular income. Pay stubs and an employer letter are the fix.
  • Household size miscounted. Someone who lives with you and eats with you was left off the case, or someone who moved out is still counted. A lease, a utility bill in the person's name, or a school record can show who actually lives in the household.
  • Missing verification. The agency asked for a document and says it never arrived, or it decided before your document was processed. If you sent it, the proof is the fax confirmation, the upload receipt, the email, or the date-stamped copy you kept.
  • Missed interview or missed recertification. The agency scheduled an interview you did not attend, or a recertification deadline passed. If you never got the appointment letter, or you called to reschedule and were not given a new time, say so in the letter and at the hearing.
  • Work requirements. Able-bodied adults without dependents face time limits on benefits unless they meet work requirements or qualify for an exemption, and the rules and exemptions change. If you were cut for this reason, check whether an exemption applies to you, such as a medical condition, caring for an incapacitated household member, or participation in a drug or alcohol treatment program.
  • Resources over the limit. The agency counted a bank balance, a vehicle, or another asset above the limit. Bank statements showing the actual balance, and documentation of what the money is for, such as rent already due, can matter.
  • Failure to report a change. The agency says you did not report a change in income or household composition. If you did report it, the proof is the date you reported it and how: in person, by phone, online, or by mail.

Some denials are correct under the rules. If the notice says your income is over the limit and your pay stubs confirm it, an appeal will not change that, and your time is better spent reapplying when your circumstances change. The appeal is for cases where the agency's facts are wrong, the agency applied the wrong rule, or the agency never got the document that proves your case.

The evidence checklist

The hearing officer decides on the record. Your letter should list the documents you are enclosing, and the hearing is where the documents do the work. Gather what applies to your reason for denial and send copies, never originals.

  • The notice itself. The determination notice, with the date, the decision, and the reason. This is the document you are appealing, and the hearing officer needs it to know what is being challenged.
  • Your application and any recertification forms. Copies of what you submitted, so the officer can see what the agency had when it decided.
  • Pay stubs and employer letters. Recent pay stubs showing actual earnings, and a letter from your employer if your hours changed, you lost the job, or the pay the agency used was from a period that no longer reflects your income.
  • Housing and utility costs. Rent receipts, a lease, utility bills. These matter when the dispute is about deductions, such as the shelter deduction, that lower your countable income.
  • Medical expenses. Receipts and bills for out-of-pocket medical costs, especially for household members who are elderly or disabled, since medical deductions can change the benefit amount.
  • Child care costs. Receipts or a statement from the child care provider showing what you pay while you work or attend training.
  • Bank statements. Statements showing the actual balance when the dispute is about resources, with any large deposits explained.
  • Proof you sent what the agency says it never got. Fax confirmations, upload receipts, certified mail receipts, email confirmations, or a dated copy of the document with a note of when and how you delivered it.
  • Your own timeline. A one-page list of dates: when you applied, when you were interviewed, when you sent each document, when the notice arrived. Dates are the backbone of an appeal, and a clear timeline is easier for a hearing officer to follow than a stack of loose papers.

The structure of the letter

A SNAP appeal letter has six parts. Keep it to one or two pages. The hearing officer reads dozens of these; the ones that get a fair hearing scheduled quickly are the ones that identify the case, name the decision, and state the facts.

  1. Header and identification. Your full name, address, phone number, date of birth if the form asks for it, and your case number. The case number is on the notice. Without it, the agency has to hunt for your file.
  2. The request. One sentence, up front: "I am requesting a fair hearing to appeal the decision described below." Do not bury the request in the third paragraph. The person opening the mail should know in five seconds what you want.
  3. The decision you are appealing. The date of the notice, what it decided, and the reason it gave. Quote the reason in the notice's own words if you can. This is the decision the hearing officer will review, so name it exactly.
  4. Why the decision is wrong. The facts, in order. What the agency said, what the truth is, and what document proves it. One paragraph per fact is plenty. Do not argue the whole case here; state the facts and point to the enclosures.
  5. Continued benefits. If your benefits are being reduced or ended, add a sentence asking that your benefits continue at the current level pending the hearing decision. This is the aid-pending request, and it only works if the hearing request arrives within the advance notice period, so send the letter fast.
  6. Enclosures and signature. List the enclosed documents, sign the letter, and date it. Keep a copy of the signed letter with everything else.

Sample SNAP benefits appeal letter

Adapt this to your facts. Replace every bracketed item with your own information, and delete any line that does not apply to your case. Do not send it with brackets still in it.

Sample letter

[Your full name]
[Your address]
[Your phone number]
[Your email]

[Date]

[State agency name]
Fair Hearings Office
[Agency address from your notice]

Re: Request for fair hearing — SNAP case number [case number], notice dated [date of notice]

Dear Sir or Madam:

I am requesting a fair hearing to appeal the decision in the notice dated [date of notice]. The notice states that my SNAP benefits will be [denied / reduced from $[amount] to $[amount] / terminated] effective [date], because [quote the reason from the notice].

This decision is incorrect because [state the key fact in one or two sentences, for example: the income the agency counted includes a one-time payment I received in [month] that is not regular income, and my current pay stubs show my regular monthly income is $[amount]].

[Add a second paragraph if there is a second fact, for example: I submitted the requested verification on [date] by [fax / online upload / in person], and a copy of the confirmation is enclosed. The agency's notice says the verification was not received.]

I am requesting that my benefits continue at the current level pending the hearing decision.

Enclosed: copy of the notice dated [date], [list your documents, for example: pay stubs for [dates], rent receipt dated [date], verification confirmation dated [date]].

Please send confirmation of the hearing date to the address above. I can be reached at [phone number].

Sincerely,
[Your full name]

How to send it

Your notice states where to send the hearing request: usually a mailing address, a fax number, or an online portal, and sometimes a phone number. Send it in writing, even if the notice also allows a phone request, because a written request creates a record with a date on it. If you request by phone, write down the date, the time, the name of the person you spoke to, and any confirmation number, and follow up in writing the same day.

Send the letter by certified mail with return receipt requested, or by fax with a confirmation page, or through the agency's online portal with a screenshot of the submission confirmation. Whichever method you use, keep the proof: the certified mail receipt, the fax confirmation, or the screenshot. The proof of when you sent it is what decides whether you met the deadline, and whether your benefits continue while you wait.

Do not send originals of anything. Send copies of the notice, the pay stubs, and the other documents, and keep the originals for the hearing.

What happens after you send it

Three things usually happen next, in this order. First, the agency confirms the hearing request and sends a hearing date, usually by mail. If you do not hear anything within a couple of weeks, call the hearings office and ask for the status, and write down who you spoke to. Second, you may be offered an informal review or a pre-hearing conference where a supervisor looks at the case again. Some cases are fixed at this stage, when the missing document turns up or the error is spotted. Third, the hearing itself, where the hearing officer hears both sides and issues a written decision, generally within 60 days of your hearing request.

Before the hearing, you have the right to review your case file. Ask the agency how to do this, and read the file before the hearing so you know exactly what the agency is relying on. If the file contains a document you never saw, or a note that contradicts what you were told, that is something to raise at the hearing.

If the hearing officer rules in your favor, the agency has to correct the decision, which can include restoring benefits and paying back benefits you should have received. If the officer rules against you, some states allow a further appeal to a court, and you can also reapply for SNAP if your circumstances change. A legal aid organization in your state can tell you which of those fits your case.

Mistakes that backfire

  • Waiting to gather documents before sending the letter. The 10-day window for continued benefits closes fast. Send the appeal first, then gather the evidence before the hearing. The letter can be one page; the evidence comes later.
  • Throwing away the notice. The notice has the case number, the decision, the reason, the deadline, and the address to send the appeal to. Without it, you are guessing. Keep every notice the agency sends, even the ones that look like routine mail.
  • Accepting a verbal denial. A caseworker telling you on the phone that you do not qualify is not a decision you can appeal. Ask for the decision in writing, with the reason and your appeal rights. If the agency will not put it in writing, request a fair hearing in writing and state that no notice was received.
  • Missing the hearing. If you do not show up, the hearing officer can decide the case without you, and the agency's version of the facts stands. If you cannot make the date, contact the hearings office as soon as you know, and ask for a new date in writing. Keep the proof of that request too.
  • Bringing no evidence. Saying the agency is wrong is not the same as showing it. The hearing officer decides on the record, and a pay stub, a receipt, or a confirmation page is worth more than a paragraph of explanation.
  • Not asking for continued benefits. If your benefits are being cut, the letter needs the sentence asking that they continue pending the hearing. Without that sentence, and without sending it inside the advance notice period, the benefits stop while you wait.

FAQ

What is a SNAP appeal?

A SNAP appeal is a request for a fair hearing before an impartial hearing officer when your state agency denies, reduces, or ends your SNAP benefits, or takes too long to decide your application. You make the request in writing, usually by sending a letter like the one in this guide, and the hearing officer reviews the agency's decision against the evidence. This guide is general information, not legal advice.

How long do I have to appeal a SNAP denial?

Federal rules generally give you 90 days from the date of the notice to request a fair hearing. But if your benefits are being reduced or ended and you want them to continue while the appeal is pending, you usually have to request the hearing within the advance notice period, generally 10 days after the notice was mailed. Read the notice you received: it states the exact deadline and where to send the request. Do not wait for the 90 days if your benefits are being cut.

Can I keep getting SNAP benefits while I appeal?

Usually yes, if you request the fair hearing within the advance notice period, generally 10 days after the notice was mailed. Your benefits then continue at the current level until the hearing decision, which is called aid pending. If you win, nothing changes. If you lose, the agency may ask you to repay the benefits you received during the appeal, so keep that in mind. If your benefits were denied on a new application rather than cut from an existing case, there are no benefits to continue, and the appeal is about getting them started.

What if I never got a written notice?

The agency is required to send a written notice explaining any denial, reduction, or termination, the reason, and your right to a fair hearing. If you were told verbally that you were denied, or your benefits simply stopped, go to the office or call and ask for the written notice and the case number. If the agency will not provide one, you can still request a fair hearing in writing and state that no notice was received, and keep a copy of your request. A missing notice is itself something to raise at the hearing.

Do I need a lawyer for a SNAP fair hearing?

No. You can represent yourself, bring a friend or family member, or bring an authorized representative. Many people handle fair hearings on their own, and the hearing is meant to be informal. Legal aid organizations in many states help with SNAP appeals for free, and some community organizations and food banks have benefits navigators who help with the paperwork. If the case involves a complex issue such as an overpayment claim or an intentional program violation charge, talk to legal aid before the hearing.

What happens at a SNAP fair hearing?

A hearing officer who was not involved in your case runs the hearing, usually by phone or in person. The agency presents its reason for the decision, you present your evidence and explain why the decision is wrong, and the officer asks questions. You can bring documents, witnesses, and a representative. You can also review your case file before the hearing. The officer then issues a written decision, generally within 60 days of your hearing request. If you disagree with the decision, some states allow a further appeal to a court.

What if I miss the appeal deadline?

A late request can still be worth sending, with an explanation of why it was late, such as never receiving the notice or a medical emergency. The agency may still accept it, but it is not required to, and late requests can be denied. If the appeal deadline has passed, you can also reapply for SNAP, and a new application starts a new case with new deadlines. Do not let a missed deadline stop you from reapplying if you still need benefits.

Is this legal advice?

No. This guide is general information about how SNAP appeals work. SNAP is a federal program run by state agencies, and the procedures, deadlines, and forms vary by state. For anything that could affect your benefits, check the notice you received, your state agency's appeal instructions, or a legal aid organization in your state before you send the letter or attend a hearing.
