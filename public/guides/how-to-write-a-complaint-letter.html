How to Write a Complaint Letter

A calm, firm letter with facts, evidence, and a deadline gets refunds and fixes. Here is the structure that works, with an annotated template.

By CyberScryb 📅 Updated October 11, 2026 ⏱ 7 min read
⚡ Key Takeaways (TL;DR)

A complaint letter that gets results leads with facts, not feelings: what happened, when it happened, who was involved, and what you want done. Keep it to one page, attach your evidence, state a specific reasonable request with a deadline, and name the next escalation step. Firm and professional beats angry every time.

What Makes a Complaint Letter Get Results

Companies receive thousands of complaint letters, and the ones that get resolved share five traits. First, they are facts-first: order numbers, dates, names, and a clear sequence of events, not adjectives. Second, they document everything: receipts, photos, and prior correspondence are attached and referenced by number. Third, they make a reasonable ask — a refund, replacement, or repair — not a demand for ten times the value in damages. Fourth, they set a deadline, usually 10 to 14 business days. Fifth, they name an escalation path: what you will do next if the deadline passes. Letters missing these get deprioritized because nobody knows what the writer actually wants. Letters with all five are easy to route, approve, and close.

The Facts-First Structure

Use this five-part skeleton for every complaint letter:

  • Header and subject line: your name, address, phone, and email at the top; then the date; then the recipient's name and address; then a subject line with the order or account number, for example: Re: Defective washing machine — Order #48291
  • Paragraph 1 — the facts: who you are, what you bought, when, and what went wrong. Two to four sentences, all verifiable
  • Paragraph 2 — what you already tried: the calls, chats, or visits, with dates and reference numbers. This stops them from sending you back to the start
  • Paragraph 3 — the ask: one specific resolution (refund, replacement, repair, credit), the amount or terms, and your deadline. Reasonable asks win; outrageous ones get ignored
  • Paragraph 4 — the escalation: what you will do if the deadline passes: escalate to a manager, file with the BBB, contact the regulator, or dispute the charge. Then sign off professionally

Annotated Example Template (Defective Product / Bad Service)

Here is the structure above applied to a common scenario — a washing machine that broke down three weeks after delivery, plus missed repair appointments. The numbers in brackets point to the notes below.

[1] [Your Name]
[Street Address, City, ST ZIP]
[Phone] · [Email]

[2] October 11, 2026

[Manager Name], Customer Service
[Company Name]
[Corporate Address]

[3] Subject: Defective Washing Machine — Order #48291 (Delivered September 12, 2026)

[4] Dear [Manager Name],

[5] On September 1, 2026, I purchased a Model WX-400 washing machine from your company for $649.00 (Order #48291, receipt attached). It was delivered September 12, and on October 2 — three weeks later — the machine stopped mid-cycle and displays error code E41. Your manual states this code indicates a control-board failure.

[6] I called support on October 3 (reference #88342) and was promised a technician visit on October 7. No one arrived, and no one called. I called again October 8 (reference #89117) and was told a technician would come October 9; again, no one arrived. I have attached the chat transcript of my October 8 conversation.

[7] I am requesting either a replacement unit or a full refund of $649.00 by October 25, 2026. A product that fails within three weeks, combined with two missed service appointments, is not acceptable.

[8] If I have not received a resolution by that date, I will escalate this matter to the Better Business Bureau and file a complaint with the state attorney general's office. I would prefer to resolve this directly with you.

[9] Sincerely,
[Your Name]

Enclosures: (1) purchase receipt, (2) delivery confirmation, (3) October 8 chat transcript, (4) photo of error code E41

  • [1]–[2] Full contact details on both sides. The recipient can reach you and route the letter without searching
  • [3] The subject line carries the product, the order number, and the delivery date — the three things a support agent needs to look you up
  • [5] Every fact is checkable: dates, model, price, order number, error code, and a citation to their own manual. No adjectives, no emotion
  • [6] Listing prior attempts with reference numbers proves this is not your first complaint and blocks the standard "please contact support" deflection
  • [7] The ask is specific, reasonable, and dated. Replacement OR refund gives them a way to say yes; the deadline forces a timeline
  • [8] The escalation is a real step you are willing to take, stated calmly. Note the final sentence — it keeps the door open
  • [9] Numbered enclosures make your evidence part of the letter instead of loose files nobody reads

Tone: Firm but Professional

The fastest way to lose a complaint is to sound like one. All-caps paragraphs and insults make the reader defensive, and defensive readers deny. Worse, anger costs you leverage: a letter full of abuse can be flagged and filed away, while a calm, firm letter forces a substantive reply. Two hard rules. First, never threaten what you will not do — an empty lawsuit threat marks you as bluffing and kills credibility; only name escalation steps you will actually take, like the BBB or a chargeback. Second, state the impact, not your feelings: "I took two unpaid half-days off work for appointments your technicians never kept" lands harder than "your service is a disgrace." Where to Send It (and What to Attach)

Escalate in this order — skipping steps wastes your strongest options:

  • The company's support channel first. Email beats phone because it creates a paper trail. If chat or email fails, find the corporate or executive customer relations address (often listed on the company's investor or contact page)
  • The Better Business Bureau. Filing with the BBB forwards your complaint to the company and tracks whether they responded — many companies resolve BBB complaints quickly to protect their rating
  • Your state attorney general or the FTC. For fraud, deceptive practices, or scams, the attorney general's consumer protection division takes complaints online; the FTC complaint helps establish a pattern even if it does not resolve your case individually
  • Your card issuer, as a last resort. A chargeback or dispute reverses the payment, but use it only after the company has had a fair chance — issuers ask what you tried first, and this letter is your proof

Always attach: the receipt or invoice, order and delivery confirmations, photos of the defect or damage, and reference numbers or transcripts of every prior contact. Keep the letter to one page and let the attachments carry the detail — a two-paragraph letter with four enclosures outperforms a four-page letter with none.

Follow-Up Timeline: When to Escalate

Send one letter, then work this timeline:

  • Day 1: send the letter by email (with attachments) and, for serious disputes, a copy by postal mail or certified mail to the corporate office
  • Day 7: if no acknowledgment at all, forward the same letter to a manager or executive relations contact with a note that you are following up
  • Your deadline (10–14 business days): if no resolution, send one final notice restating the ask — then follow through on the escalation you named
  • After the deadline: file the BBB and attorney general complaints, post a factual review naming dates and reference numbers, or initiate the chargeback. One escalation at a time, each documented

Keep every email, reference number, and screenshot in one folder. If the dispute ever reaches a regulator or small claims court, your paper trail is your case.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I start a complaint letter?

State who you are, what you bought or used, and the account or order number in the first two sentences. Then describe what went wrong with specific dates. Never open with your feelings — open with facts the reader can act on.

How long should a complaint letter be?

One page, or roughly 200 to 300 words. Anything longer gets skimmed or ignored. If you have extensive documentation, put it in attachments and reference them by number in the letter.

What is the correct format for a complaint letter?

Your contact details at the top, then the date, the recipient name and address, a subject line with the order or account number, a greeting, a body of three to five paragraphs (facts, prior attempts, your ask with a deadline, escalation warning), and a professional sign-off.

Where should I send my complaint letter?

Start with the company's official support channel, then escalate to a corporate office or executive team if ignored. Outside the company, the BBB, your state attorney general, or a federal regulator fits most cases, and a chargeback is the last resort for unresolved card payments.

What should I do if I get no response to my complaint letter?

Follow up once at your stated deadline, then escalate: resend to a manager or executive team, file with the BBB or your state attorney general, or post a factual review. Each escalation gets one letter, not a barrage.
