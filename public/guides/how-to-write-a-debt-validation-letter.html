The direct answer: you have 30 days from the collector's first contact to demand, in writing, that they prove the debt is yours and that they have the right to collect it. The Direct Answer: Make Them Prove It

A debt collector contacts you about a debt. Maybe it is yours. Maybe it is someone else's with a similar name. Maybe the amount is wrong, or it was already paid, or the collector has no paperwork at all. You do not have to take their word for it. Federal law gives you the right to demand proof before you pay a cent.

That right lives in the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act, section 1692g. It says: within 30 days after the collector's initial communication with you, you can dispute the debt in writing and demand validation. Once your timely written request arrives, the collector must stop collecting until it verifies the debt and mails you the verification. That is the whole game. The letter below is how you play it.

The 30-Day Window: Deadline Math

This is the part people get wrong, and getting it wrong costs you the right entirely.

  • The clock starts at first contact. The 30 days run from the collector's initial communication with you about the debt. That can be a phone call, a letter, a text, or an email. Not from the date of the debt. Not from the validation notice. From first contact.
  • The collector also owes you a written notice. Within 5 days of that initial communication, the collector must send you a written notice stating the amount of the debt, the name of the creditor, your 30-day right to dispute, and your right to demand the original creditor's name and address.
  • Do not wait for the written notice. Your 30-day clock started at the initial communication, not when the notice arrives in the mail. If the first contact was a phone call on the 1st and the letter reaches you on the 8th, your deadline is the 31st, not the 38th.
  • Mail early. Day 30 is the cutoff for your request reaching them, and mail takes days. Send the letter at least a week before the deadline. Certified mail with a postmarked receipt is your proof of timing.
  • After day 30, they can assume the debt is valid. The law lets the collector treat an undisputed debt as valid after the window closes. You can still dispute later, but you lose the automatic cease-collection power that only a timely request triggers.

One request in writing, before day 30. That is the deadline. Everything below assumes you are inside it.

Step 1: Confirm Who Is Contacting You and Whether the FDCPA Applies

The validation right covers third-party debt collectors: agencies and firms collecting someone else's debt. It generally does not cover the original creditor collecting its own account (your original credit card company calling about your own card, for example). State laws may still give you rights against original creditors, so check your state's rules if that is your situation.

Write down the collector's full name, address, phone number, the account or reference number they gave you, and the date and form of their first contact. This record is what anchors your deadline math.

Step 2: Write the Letter With What It Must Include

A validation letter is short and cold. It has a job: dispute, demand, and give them nothing to use against you. Include these:

  • Your identification. Full name and mailing address. No phone number, no email beyond what they already have.
  • Their identification. Collector name and address, plus the account or reference number from their notice.
  • A clear dispute. "I dispute this debt in whole" or "I dispute this debt in part and request validation of the disputed portion." Plain and unambiguous.
  • A demand for validation under FDCPA 1692g. Name the statute. Ask for verification of the debt: documentation showing the debt is yours, the amount, and how the amount was calculated.
  • The original creditor's name and address. If the debt was sold or transferred, ask for the original creditor's name and address. The law entitles you to it.
  • Proof they can collect in your state. Ask for evidence the collector is licensed or bonded to collect in your state, where required.
  • A request to stop contact except in writing. You can also limit communication to mail only, which cuts the phone pressure.

Step 3: Leave Out What Must Never Go In

This is where people hurt themselves. A validation letter is not a negotiation letter. It is a demand for proof. Keep it sterile.

  • Never admit the debt is yours. Not "I owe this but." Not "I recognize this account." Not even a partial acknowledgment. Admitting the debt in writing can restart or extend the statute of limitations in some states.
  • Never give payment or bank information. No account numbers, no routing numbers, no "here is what I can pay." The letter is about their proof, not your money.
  • Never agree to a payment plan in this letter. A payment agreement is a separate decision with statute-of-limitations consequences in some states. Do not bundle it with a validation request.
  • Do not explain your finances. Hardship stories, income details, and explanations are ammunition for a collector, not a legal requirement. They go in a different letter, if anywhere.
  • Do not threaten what you cannot do. Skip the threats. The statute does the work.

Step 4: Add Your Sample Dispute Points

You do not need to prove anything. But naming specific doubts makes your letter stronger and your record clearer. Use any that apply:

  • Not my debt. "I do not recognize this debt, the account, or the creditor."
  • Wrong amount. "The amount claimed does not match any account I have held. Provide an itemized breakdown of principal, interest, and fees."
  • Already paid or settled. "This account was paid in full / settled on [date, if you know it]. Provide documentation showing a balance remains."
  • Stolen identity or mixed file. "This may be identity theft or a mixed credit file. Provide the account application or contract bearing my signature."
  • Time-barred debt. "This debt may be outside my state's statute of limitations. Provide the date of last payment and the date of first delinquency."
  • Debt buyer with no paperwork. "Provide the complete chain of assignment from the original creditor to your firm showing you own this debt."
  • No license. "Provide proof you are licensed to collect in my state."

Step 5: Send It Certified Mail and Keep Records

How you send the letter matters as much as what is in it. The collector's obligations trigger when they receive your request, and in a dispute, the burden is on you to show you sent it on time.

  • USPS Certified Mail, return receipt requested. This is the standard. The green card comes back signed, and you have proof of delivery and a date.
  • Keep a copy of everything. The signed letter, the certified mail receipt, the return receipt green card, the collector's original notice, and every envelope with its postmark. Keep them together in one folder.
  • Log every contact. Date, time, method, who you spoke with, what was said. Phone calls from collectors after your timely request are potential violations. A contemporaneous log is evidence.
  • Check your credit reports. After you dispute, the collector must report the account as disputed to any credit bureau it reports to. Pull your reports from AnnualCreditReport.com and verify.
  • File complaints if they ignore you. A collector that keeps collecting without validating is violating the FDCPA. Report it to the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB), your state attorney general, and the FTC. A consumer attorney can evaluate damages.

The Template: Copy, Fill, Mail

Everything above in one letter. Replace everything in brackets. One page is fine. The CFPB also publishes its own sample debt dispute and validation letters at consumerfinance.gov if you want a second source.

[Your Full Name]
[Your Mailing Address]
[City, State, ZIP]

[Date]

[Collector Name]
[Collector Address]
[City, State, ZIP]

Re: Account/Reference # [number from their notice]

To whom it may concern:

I am writing in response to your communication dated [date of first contact] regarding the above-referenced account. I dispute this debt in its entirety and request validation under the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act, 15 U.S.C. 1692g.

Please provide the following:

1. Verification of the debt, including documentation showing this debt belongs to me.
2. An itemized breakdown of the amount claimed, showing principal, interest, and fees.
3. The name and address of the original creditor, if different from the current creditor.
4. A copy of any judgment, if one exists.
5. The date of first delinquency and the date of last payment.
6. Proof that you are licensed to collect debts in my state.

I do not admit this debt or any portion of it. Do not report this account to any credit reporting agency without also reporting that it is disputed.

Until you provide the requested validation, cease all collection activity on this account. Please contact me by mail only at the address above.

Sincerely,
[Your Full Name]

  • The dispute line is unambiguous: you dispute the whole debt. Partial disputes are allowed but full is the default.
  • The 1692g citation names the statute so there is no ambiguity about what right you are exercising.
  • The six requests cover identity, amount, creditor, judgment, dates, and license. Adapt to your dispute points above.
  • "I do not admit" is the sentence that protects your statute-of-limitations position.
  • Mail-only contact shuts down the phone pressure. They must honor a written communication preference.

After You Mail It: What Happens Next

  • Collection must stop. Your timely written request triggers the cease-collection duty. Calls, letters, and lawsuits over this debt must pause until they validate.
  • They validate, or they walk away. A legitimate collector with real paperwork sends verification and resumes. A debt buyer with no paperwork sometimes just stops. Both are wins.
  • The verification must be real. A one-line printout of your name and an amount is thin. Real verification connects the debt to you: account records, the contract, the chain of assignment. If what arrives looks fabricated, a consumer attorney is your next call.
  • The debt is not gone automatically. Failed validation stops this collector. The underlying debt may still be sold to another collector, who then gets their own initial communication and their own 30-day clock. Stay organized so you can repeat this.

Frequently Asked Questions

What happens if the collector does not validate the debt?

If your request was timely and in writing, the collector must stop all collection activity until it provides verification. Calls, letters, and lawsuits over that debt must pause. If it keeps collecting anyway, that is an FDCPA violation. Report it to the CFPB, your state attorney general, or the FTC, and talk to a consumer attorney about damages. See the template above for the exact wording to use.

Does a validation request stop collection activity?

Yes, but only if the request is in writing and made within the 30-day window. Once received, the collector must cease collection until it obtains verification (or the name and address of the original creditor) and mails it to you. After the 30 days expire, the collector may generally assume the debt is valid and keep collecting.

What is the difference between validation, verification, and dispute?

Validation is your FDCPA 30-day right: you demand the collector prove the debt is yours and that it can collect. Verification is what the collector must produce in response, usually account documentation from the creditor. A dispute is the broader term for challenging the debt in whole or in part. In practice one letter does all three: it disputes the debt and demands validation.

Can a collector report the debt to credit bureaus during validation?

Collection activity must stop until the collector provides verification, and credit reporting can count as collection activity. Regardless, once you dispute, any reporting must include that the account is disputed. Negative items generally stay on credit reports up to 7 years from the date of first delinquency, and a failed validation does not erase a debt you actually owe.

Does requesting debt validation restart the statute of limitations?

No. A validation request does not restart the statute of limitations. What can restart or extend it, depending on your state, is making a payment, making a payment promise in some states, or acknowledging the debt in writing. That is exactly why a validation letter never admits the debt or offers payment.

Does the FDCPA apply to original creditors?

Generally no. The FDCPA's validation rules apply to third-party debt collectors, not to the original creditor collecting its own account. State debt collection laws may still give you rights against original creditors, so check your state's rules.

What should a debt validation letter include?

Your name and address, the collector's name and address, the account number they reference, a clear statement disputing the debt and demanding validation under FDCPA 1692g, requests for verification, the original creditor's name and address, and proof of collection license in your state. Never admit the debt and never include payment or bank information.

When does the 30-day validation period start?

From the initial communication: the collector's first contact with you about the debt, whether call, letter, text, or email. The collector must also send a written notice within 5 days of that first contact. Your clock starts at the initial communication, not when the written notice arrives, so do not wait for the letter. Mail your request at least a week before day 30.
