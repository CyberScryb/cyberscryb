How to Write an ESA Accommodation Request Letter

Direct answer: this is a two-letter job. First, your licensed mental health provider writes a letter documenting the disability-related need, because that letter is what carries the legal weight. Then you write your landlord a short, factual request letter attaching it. No template, no online certificate, and no registry entry can substitute for the provider's letter.

Step 1: Understand what you are actually asking for

You are asking for a reasonable accommodation under fair-housing law: permission to keep an assistance animal in housing that has a no-pets policy. The animal is not a pet in this process, which is why the usual pet fees and deposits generally do not apply. The request goes to your landlord or property manager in writing, and you keep a copy of everything.

Step 2: Get the provider letter first

Everything else rests on this. Schedule with a licensed mental health provider you actually see, a therapist, psychologist, or psychiatrist, and ask directly: can you document that I have a qualifying condition under fair-housing law and that the animal is part of my support plan? A proper letter includes the provider's license information, a statement of the disability-related need, the animal's role, and the date. It does not need to name your diagnosis. Be wary of any service that offers to sell you an instant letter without an assessment; landlords and housing authorities look for a real treatment relationship, and a paper-mill letter can undermine a real case.

Step 3: Ignore the certificates and registries

There is no government ESA registry and no official ESA certification. Sites that sell certificates, ID cards, or registry entries are selling paper, not legal status. A landlord can lawfully give no weight to an online certificate, and paying for one does not strengthen your request. Spend that effort on the provider relationship instead.

Step 4: Write the request letter to your landlord

Keep it to one page. Identify yourself and the unit. State that you are requesting a reasonable accommodation under fair-housing law to keep an assistance animal, attach or reference your provider's letter, name the animal and its species, and ask for written confirmation. Do not include your diagnosis, treatment history, or medical records. Your landlord needs to know there is a documented disability-related need, not the details of your care.

A working structure:

  • Header. Your name, address and unit number, the date, and the landlord or manager's name and address.
  • Paragraph one. You are requesting a reasonable accommodation for an assistance animal under fair-housing law. Name the animal and species.
  • Paragraph two. State that documentation from your licensed mental health provider is attached (or available on request), and that it confirms the disability-related need.
  • Paragraph three. Ask for written confirmation of the accommodation and name a reasonable date to hear back. Sign it.

Step 5: Know what the landlord can and cannot ask

When the disability is not obvious, the landlord may ask for the provider's letter and may verify the provider's license and treatment relationship with you. They generally may not demand your diagnosis, your medical records, or an independent exam, and they may not require the animal to wear a vest or carry special identification. They may not charge pet rent, pet fees, or a pet deposit for the animal, though you remain liable for any actual damage the animal causes. If a landlord refuses or ignores the request, document it in writing and talk to a fair-housing organization or attorney before assuming your options.

Step 6: Keep the paper trail clean

  • Send the request in writing, email with a read receipt is fine, and save a copy.
  • Keep the provider letter, your request, and every landlord reply in one folder.
  • Note dates of every conversation about the animal, in person or by phone.
  • Do not move the animal in before you have the accommodation confirmed in writing.

FAQ

What is an ESA accommodation request letter?

A letter you send your landlord or property manager asking for a reasonable accommodation under fair-housing law so your emotional support animal can live with you despite a no-pets policy. It identifies the disability-related need, references your licensed mental health provider's documentation, and asks for a specific accommodation.

Do I need an ESA letter from a therapist?

Yes. A letter from a licensed mental health provider who has assessed you is what carries legal weight. It states that you have a qualifying disability-related need and that the animal provides support related to that need. A template, a letter you write yourself, or a paid online certificate does not substitute for this.

Are online ESA certificates or registries legitimate?

There is no government ESA registry and no official ESA certification. Websites that sell certificates, ID cards, or registry entries are selling paper, not legal status. A landlord can lawfully give no weight to an online certificate. What matters is a letter from a licensed provider with an actual treatment relationship.

What should an ESA letter include?

A provider's letter generally includes the provider's license information, a statement that the patient has a qualifying condition under fair-housing law, that the animal is part of the treatment plan, and the date. It does not need to name your diagnosis, and you should not volunteer your diagnosis to the landlord yourself.

What can my landlord ask about my ESA?

When your disability is not obvious, a landlord may ask for the provider's letter and may verify that the provider is licensed and has a treatment relationship with you. They generally may not demand your medical records, your diagnosis, or an exam, and they may not require the animal to wear a vest or special ID.

Can a landlord charge pet fees for an ESA?

Generally no. A landlord may not charge pet rent, pet fees, or a pet deposit for an assistance animal, because the animal is not treated as a pet under fair-housing law. You can still be liable for actual damage the animal causes.

What should I say in the request letter to my landlord?

Keep it short and factual: identify yourself and the unit, state that you are requesting a reasonable accommodation under fair-housing law for an assistance animal, attach or reference your provider's letter, name the animal and species, and ask for written confirmation. Do not disclose your diagnosis or treatment details in this letter.

This guide is for general information only and is not legal advice. Fair-housing rules are detailed and fact-specific; talk to a fair-housing organization or attorney about your situation before you act.
