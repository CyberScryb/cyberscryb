How to Respond to an IRS Notice CP14 (Balance Due)

Direct answer: a CP14 is the IRS's first notice that you owe a balance for a tax year. It is not a levy and not a final demand. Read it like a checklist, verify the amount against your own return and payment records, then do one of three things: pay it in full, dispute it if it is wrong, or request an installment agreement if you cannot pay in full. Do not ignore it. Ignoring a CP14 is the one response that reliably makes things worse.

Step 1: Read the notice like a checklist

Before you react, find these items on the notice itself:

  • The tax year. Which year's return produced the balance.
  • The amount. Tax, penalties, and interest as stated on the notice.
  • The notice date and any response date. The notice states what to do and by when. Those dates come from the notice, not from a general rule.
  • The contact number. The number printed on the notice is the one to use if you need to dispute or discuss the balance.
  • The payment stub. If you pay by mail, the stub goes with the payment so it lands on the right account and year.

Keep the notice. Do not throw it away after reading it: it is the reference for every later step, and you will need the notice number and tax year if you call.

Step 2: Verify the amount before you pay or dispute anything

CP14 balances are sometimes wrong, and sometimes right but already handled. Check three things:

  • Your filed return. Does the balance match what your return showed as owed, or did the IRS change something?
  • Payments already made. Did a payment cross the notice in the mail, or was a payment applied to the wrong year? Check bank records and your IRS online account.
  • Amended returns or corrections. If you filed an amended return after the original, the notice may predate the correction.

If the amount is right, move to step 3. If it is wrong or already paid, move to step 4. Either way, you now know which path you are on instead of guessing.

Step 3: If the balance is right, choose pay in full or a payment plan

Pay in full if you can, using the payment method and date stated on the notice, and keep the confirmation. Paying in full stops further interest and penalties from accruing on that balance.

Request an installment agreement if you cannot pay in full. Apply through the IRS Online Payment Agreement tool, or mail Form 9465 with a request letter. Propose a monthly amount you can sustain every month without fail, and stay current on new tax years while you pay: a new unfiled return or unpaid new balance can default the agreement. Interest and penalties generally continue while any balance is unpaid, even on a payment plan, so paying extra when you can and paying the plan off early reduces the total you pay.

Step 4: If the balance is wrong or already paid, dispute it in writing

Call the number on the notice with your return, your payment records, and the notice in front of you. State the discrepancy plainly: what the notice says, what your records show, and what correction you are asking for. Then follow up in writing if the IRS asks for it, and keep a copy of everything you send plus every reply.

Do not pay a balance you believe is wrong just to make the notice go away, and do not pay the same balance twice. A duplicate payment is its own cleanup job.

Step 5: What not to do

  • Do not ignore it. Further notices follow, and the collection process advances. The CP14 is the earliest, cheapest point to act.
  • Do not pay a caller. Real IRS notices arrive by mail. Calls, texts, or emails demanding immediate payment through gift cards, wire transfers, or cryptocurrency are scams.
  • Do not miss new filings. A CP14 covers a past year. New years still need filed returns on time, or the problem compounds.
  • Do not promise a payment you cannot make. A payment plan you default on is worse than a smaller honest proposal made the first time.

Step 6: Keep the file until the balance is zero

The notice, your return for that year, payment confirmations, any dispute letters, IRS replies, and the installment agreement if you made one, in one folder. Keep it until the IRS confirms the balance is zero. If the balance is ever questioned again, the file is what settles it.

FAQ

What is IRS Notice CP14?

CP14 is the IRS's first notice that you owe a balance for a tax year. It states the amount owed, the tax year, and how to pay or respond. It is a notice, not a levy and not a final demand: it is the start of the collection process, and responding now keeps your options open.

Do I have to pay a CP14 balance immediately?

Pay in full if you can, by the date and method stated on the notice. If you cannot pay in full, you do not have to: you can request an installment agreement, which lets you pay over time in monthly payments. Ignoring the notice is the one option that makes things worse.

What if the CP14 amount is wrong?

Compare the notice against your filed return and your payment records. If a payment you made is missing, or the IRS changed something you disagree with, contact the IRS using the number on the notice and explain the discrepancy with your records in front of you. Keep copies of everything you send and every reply you receive.

What if I already paid the balance on my CP14?

Check the payment date against the notice date: payments and notices often cross in the mail. If your payment cleared before the notice was issued, contact the IRS with the payment confirmation so the account can be corrected. Do not pay the same balance twice.

What if I cannot pay a CP14 balance in full?

Request an installment agreement through the IRS Online Payment Agreement tool or by mailing Form 9465 with a request letter. Propose a monthly amount you can sustain every month, and stay current on new tax years while you pay, or the agreement can default.

What happens if I ignore a CP14?

The IRS sends further notices, and the collection process advances. Interest and penalties continue while the balance is unpaid. A CP14 is the earliest, cheapest point to act: paying, disputing, or requesting a plan now is far easier than unwinding later collection steps.

Is a CP14 notice a scam?

Real IRS notices arrive by mail, never by phone call, text, or email demanding immediate payment. The IRS does not ask for gift cards, wire transfers, or cryptocurrency. If a message pressures you to pay immediately through an unusual method, it is a scam: verify the balance through your IRS online account or the number on a mailed notice.
