How to Study for an Exam

A simple study schedule that actually works: assess your level, plan backwards from the exam date, give weak subjects more weight, use active recall, run a mock test, and rest properly. Includes a worked 14-day example.

By CyberScryb 📅 Updated October 15, 2026 ⏱ 9 min read
⚡ Key Takeaways (TL;DR)

Studying works when you test yourself, not when you re-read. Rate every topic as strong, shaky, or weak, then give weak topics twice the time. Study in 25 to 50 minute focused blocks with breaks. Close the book and recall answers from memory, then review each topic again after 1, 3, 7, and 14 days. Do a full mock exam under real conditions 2 to 3 days before the test. Sleep 7 to 8 hours every night during study weeks; a tired brain retains almost nothing.

Step 1: Assess Your Level Honestly

Before you schedule anything, find out where you actually stand. List every topic on the exam and rate each one: strong, shaky, or weak. Be brutal. "I have read the chapter" is not strong. Strong means you could answer questions about it right now, cold, without notes. Shaky means you sort of know it but would stumble. Weak means you could not start an answer.

The fastest way to do this is a diagnostic: take an old exam, a practice set, or even a quick quiz you write yourself, and see what you miss. Students usually overrate themselves by a full letter grade, so let the mistakes do the talking. Your whole schedule flows from this rating, so spend an hour getting it right instead of building a plan on guesswork. If you want this broken down further, our guide on making a study plan walks through the rating process in detail.

Step 2: Plan Backwards From the Exam Date

Mark the exam date on a calendar and work backwards. Block out the last two days before the exam for review and one mock test only, no new material. Then divide the remaining days across your topic list. This matters because people who plan forwards always run out of time on the hard topics at the end. Planning backwards forces every topic to get a real slot, and it shows you immediately if you started too late to cover everything, which tells you to cut the nice-to-have topics early instead of discovering it the night before.

A simple rule: give each topic at least one study block and one separate review block. If the math does not fit in your days, merge small topics or drop the lowest-weight ones now, on purpose, rather than rushing through everything badly.

Step 3: Give Weak Subjects More Weight

The biggest mistake students make is studying their strong subjects because it feels good. It is also worthless: a strong topic gains almost nothing from extra hours, while a weak topic can gain a full letter grade in the same time. Weight your time by weakness. A practical split: give each weak topic about twice the study time of a shaky topic, and give strong topics only brief maintenance review. Concretely, if you have 20 hours total and the split is 3 weak, 4 shaky, and 3 strong topics, spend roughly 10 hours on the weak ones, 7 on the shaky ones, and 3 keeping the strong ones fresh. Protect this ratio. The pull to spend time where you are comfortable is strong, and it is exactly what your schedule is there to prevent.

Step 4: Use Active Recall and Spaced Review

Re-reading notes and highlighting feel like studying. They are not, or barely are. The evidence on this is consistent: the most effective technique is active recall, pulling an answer out of your head with the book closed. Flashcards, practice questions, blank-page summaries, explaining the concept out loud like you are teaching it. All of these force retrieval, and retrieval is what builds memory.

The second half is spacing. You do not need marathon sessions; you need repeated encounters at increasing gaps. A practical cadence: learn a topic on day one, review it briefly on day 2, again on day 4, again on day 8, and once more around day 15. Each review should be active, not passive: answer questions about the topic, do not just re-read it. One recall session of 30 minutes beats three hours of highlighting. Every time.

Step 5: Schedule a Mock Test

Two to three days before the exam, take a full mock test under real conditions: same time limit, same format, no notes, no phone, no pausing. This does three things. It exposes gaps your study sessions missed, because being tested is different from practicing. It trains your pacing, so you do not run out of time on question 8 of 20. And it kills exam-day panic, because your brain has already done this exact performance once.

After the mock, spend your remaining time only on what it exposed. Do not learn new material now; patch the holes you found. If you cannot find a practice exam for your subject, build one from your notes by turning each heading into a question, then answer under a timer. Preparing for the SAT specifically? Step 6: Rest Day Rules

Rest is part of the schedule, not a reward for finishing it. Sleep 7 to 8 hours every night during your study weeks. Memory consolidation happens during sleep; cutting sleep to study is trading retention for hours, and the trade loses. Take one full rest day per week if your timeline allows it. On the day before the exam, do a light review of your strongest material only, nothing new, nothing heavy, and go to bed on time. Studying until 2 AM the night before is the single most common way students throw away two weeks of good work. Treat your sleep schedule as a line item in the plan, same as a study block.

A Worked 14-Day Example

Say your exam is in 14 days, it covers five topics, and your honest rating came out: 1 weak, 2 shaky, 2 strong. You have about 3 hours a day.

  1. Days 1 to 2: Learn the weak topic. Two full blocks per day on the weak topic only. End each day by writing a blank-page summary from memory.
  2. Days 3 to 5: Learn shaky topic A and B. One topic per day and a half. Use flashcards and practice questions, not re-reading. Spend 30 minutes at the end of each day re-testing the weak topic from day 1 (spaced review).
  3. Days 6 to 8: Deepen. Revisit the weak topic with harder practice questions. Do mixed problem sets across the weak and shaky topics. Brief maintenance pass over the two strong topics.
  4. Days 9 to 11: Drill. Full practice sets under light time pressure. Every mistake becomes a flashcard. Re-review anything you missed on days 1 to 8.
  5. Day 12: Mock test. Full exam, real conditions, timed. Score it honestly. Your remaining study is only what the mock exposed.
  6. Day 13: Patch holes. Fix only what the mock exposed. Light review of strong topics. No new material.
  7. Day 14: Rest day. Light skim of your best material in the morning. Then stop. Exercise, eat, sleep early.

Total: about 39 focused hours across 14 days, with the weakest topic getting nearly half of the serious time. That beats 60 hours of passive re-reading. If your exam covers more than five topics, compress the learning days and protect days 12 to 14 no matter what; the mock and the rest day are the two days you never cut.

Common Mistakes

  • Re-reading instead of recalling. Reading the same page five times is the illusion of studying. Close the book and test yourself.
  • Studying strong topics for comfort. It feels productive and costs you grades on the weak ones. Weight by weakness, always.
  • Marathon sessions with no breaks. Focus collapses after about 50 minutes. Work in 25 to 50 minute blocks with 5 to 10 minute breaks, and walk around during the break.
  • Starting too late. Two weeks is the floor for a real exam. Less than that and you are cramming, which only holds for simple recall tests.
  • No mock test. If you have never sat a timed full exam, exam day will be the first time you feel the pressure. Do not let that be the plan.
  • All-nighters. Sleep deprivation wipes out recall. Every hour past midnight costs you more on exam day than it earned you the night before.
  • Ignoring the exam format. Studying essay exams like multiple-choice exams wastes everything. Match your practice to the actual format: timed writing for essays, problem sets for math, definitions and recall for vocabulary-heavy tests.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many hours per day should I study for an exam?

For most students, 2 to 4 focused hours a day beats 8 distracted hours. Use 25 to 50 minute blocks with short breaks between them. Once you pass about 5 hours in a day, retention falls off hard, so stop and rest instead of grinding through.

How far in advance should I start studying for an exam?

Two weeks is the practical minimum for a major exam, three to four weeks is comfortable, and six or more weeks is ideal for big standardized tests like the SAT or bar exam. Less than a week and you are cramming, which only works for simple recall tests.

How do I study for multiple exams at once?

Rank your exams by difficulty and date, then give each its own daily block instead of switching between subjects every few minutes. Study the hardest exam first while your mind is fresh. Use your off days and lighter days to rotate review of the easier ones.

What is the best way to review material for an exam?

Active recall is the best review method: close the book and pull the answer out of your head, using flashcards or practice questions. Re-reading notes feels productive but it barely helps. Pair recall with spaced review, revisiting each topic after 1, 3, 7, and 14 days.

Is cramming ever okay?

Cramming works for simple fact-heavy tests like vocabulary quizzes or formula lists, where you can load short-term memory the night before. It fails for exams that require understanding, problem solving, or essays. If you must cram, use active recall instead of re-reading, and accept that the knowledge will fade fast.
