How to Write a Farewell Speech for a Coworker

A farewell speech that lands in three minutes: open warm, thank specifically, tell one story, close with a toast. Full examples for a departing coworker and a retirement.

By CyberScryb 📅 Updated October 13, 2026 ⏱ 7 min read
⚡ Key Takeaways (TL;DR)

A good farewell speech runs two to three minutes and has four beats: a warm opening, specific thanks tied to something they actually did, one short story that shows who they are, and a forward-looking close. Write bullet points, not a script. One story beats five adjectives, and the speech is about them, not you.

The 4 Beats of a Farewell Speech

You have been asked to say a few words. The room is holding cake, the person of honor is pretending not to be emotional, and you have about three minutes before attention drifts. Every farewell speech that works follows the same four beats:

  1. Open warm and direct. One line that names why everyone is here. "I am not great at goodbyes, so I wrote a few things down" is honest and instantly disarming. Say their name early.
  2. Thank them for something specific. Not "your dedication" — the thing they did. The late-night save, the mentorship, the way they made the hard weeks bearable. Specificity is what makes the room nod.
  3. Tell one short story. A single anecdote that shows who this person is beats a paragraph of adjectives. Pick the story the room already half-knows — shared history is the point.
  4. Close forward-looking. A wish for what is next, a toast if drinks are involved, an open door. End on motion, not on loss: "Here is to what is next" beats "we will miss you so much" as a final line.

Example: Short Farewell Speech for a Coworker Leaving

About two minutes spoken. Warm, one light moment, specific:

I will keep this short, because if I go long I will get emotional and nobody wants that before cake.

When Dana joined this team four years ago, I figured she would be good. I did not figure she would become the person everyone goes to when something is actually on fire. The product launch last spring does not happen without her — three all-nighters, zero complaints, and she still remembered everyone's coffee order.

My favorite Dana story, and I know half of you were there: the client demo where the entire system went down ten minutes before go-time. Dana is under the table with a laptop, calm as anything, and gets us back up with two minutes to spare. The client never knew. That is who she is — the calm in the room when it matters.

Dana, they have no idea how lucky they are. Thank you for making this team better every day you were here. Everyone — raise a glass. To Dana, and to whatever is next.

Example: Retirement Farewell Speech (Short Version)

Retirements run longer and warmer. Same four beats, bigger canvas:

Thirty-two years. I did the math this morning, and that is longer than some of us in this room have been alive — sorry, I had to.

When I started here, Robert was the person they assigned to show me the ropes, which mostly meant he taught me which meetings could be emails and which emails could be ignored. But he also taught me the actual job: how to read a room, how to tell a client a hard truth without losing them, how to do this work with integrity when nobody is watching.

Robert, this place runs the way it does because of people like you, and people like you are rarer than we admit. Enjoy every minute of what is next — you have earned it about ten times over. To Robert.

Delivery Tips: Notes, Pace, and Nerves

  • Bring bullet points, not a script. A card or your phone with the four beats and the key names. Notes keep you on track when emotion hits; reading verbatim makes you sound robotic.
  • Slow down. Nerves make everyone rush. Deliberately pause after the story — the silence is where the feeling lands.
  • Look at the person, not the room. You are talking to them; the room is just listening in. Eye contact with the guest of honor keeps it personal instead of performative.
  • Rehearse once, out loud. Not to memorize — to find the spots where you stumble and to time it. Two to three minutes spoken is roughly 250 to 400 words on the page.
  • It is fine to get emotional. A catch in your voice reads as sincerity, not weakness. If you might fully lose it, that is what the bullet points are for — glance down, breathe, continue.

What NOT to Do

  • Do not roast. Gentle, affectionate humor the room already shares is fine. Sarcasm, embarrassing stories, and jokes at their expense are not — especially with managers or clients in the room.
  • Do not make it about you. "This is so hard for me" centers the wrong person. Your feelings get one line, max.
  • Do not go past five minutes. The room's goodwill is finite. Two to three minutes is the sweet spot; five is the absolute ceiling.
  • Do not wing it. "I will just speak from the heart" produces rambling. The heart provides the material; the bullet points provide the shape.
  • Do not bring up company politics. No layoff commentary, no reorg jokes, no speculation about why they are really leaving. The speech is a gift, not a forum.

When a Message Is Enough Instead

Not every goodbye needs a speech. If you are not the one asked to speak, or the farewell is informal — a card going around, a Slack thread — a written message is the right format. Frequently Asked Questions

How long should a farewell speech be?

Two to three minutes spoken — roughly 250 to 400 words. Long enough for an opening, one real story, and a toast-style close. Past five minutes the room checks out.

How do you start a farewell speech?

One warm, direct line naming why everyone is gathered, then the person's name and one true thing about working with them. Skip the "I was forced to do this" jokes — they burn your best ten seconds.

What should you include in a farewell speech for a coworker?

Four beats: a warm opening, specific thanks tied to something they actually did, one short story that shows who they are, and a forward-looking close. One story beats five adjectives every time.

Should a farewell speech be funny or serious?

Warm with one or two light moments. Humor works when it is affectionate and the room already knows the story. Avoid roasts, sarcasm about the company, and jokes that need explaining.

What should you never say in a farewell speech?

No company negativity, no speculation about why they are leaving, no inside jokes half the room will not get, and do not turn it into your own moment — the speech is about them.

Do I need notes for a farewell speech?

Yes — bullet points on a card or phone, not a memorized script. Notes keep you on track when emotion hits; reading verbatim sounds robotic.
