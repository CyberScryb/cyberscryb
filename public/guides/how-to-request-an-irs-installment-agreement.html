How to Request an IRS Installment Agreement

Direct answer: if you owe the IRS and can't pay in full, request a payment plan through the IRS Online Payment Agreement tool at IRS.gov. If you owe $50,000 or less (combined tax, penalties, and interest) and can pay within 72 months, you usually get an instant decision. The setup fee is $22 when you apply online with direct debit. File your return first even if you can't pay — filing on time avoids the 5%-per-month failure-to-file penalty no matter what you owe.

What an IRS installment agreement actually is

An installment agreement is the IRS's formal long-term payment plan: you agree to pay your tax debt in monthly installments, and the IRS agrees not to use levies or other enforced collection while the agreement is in good standing. Interest and penalties keep accruing, but the failure-to-pay penalty drops from 0.5% to 0.25% per month once the agreement is active. That's the one real discount the program gives you, and it's worth taking even if you could limp along without it.

There are two plan flavors. A short-term payment plan gives you up to 180 days to pay in full and has no setup fee — pick it if you can clear the balance within six months. A long-term installment agreement stretches payments up to 72 months and carries a one-time setup fee. Most people who can't pay in full land on the long-term plan.

Step 1: file every required return

You cannot get approved with unfiled returns outstanding. If you haven't filed for some years, file them first — even late returns. You can request a payment plan that covers multiple tax years in one application. File on time going forward: the failure-to-file penalty is 5% per month (capped at 25%), which is roughly ten times the failure-to-pay penalty. People who file late and can't pay get crushed by both penalties at once.

Step 2: apply through the Online Payment Agreement tool

This is the fast path. Go to IRS.gov, sign in to (or create) your IRS online account, and open the Online Payment Agreement application. You'll need:

  1. Your identity verification (the online account setup handles this).
  2. The tax years and balance you want covered.
  3. Your bank routing and account numbers if you choose direct debit (do this — it's the cheapest fee).
  4. The monthly payment amount you can actually afford, every month.
  5. The day of the month you want payments withdrawn.

If you qualify for a streamlined plan ($50,000 or less, payable within 72 months), you get a decision immediately. Print or save the confirmation. If the system can't approve you — usually because you owe more than $50,000 or have unfiled returns — it tells you what to do next.

Step 3 (paper alternative): file Form 9465

If you prefer paper or can't use the online tool, complete Form 9465, Installment Agreement Request and mail it to the IRS service center for your region (the address is on the last page of the form instructions). Expect 30 to 60 days for a response. Start making the monthly payment you proposed anyway while you wait — interest and penalties keep running during processing, and sending payments early reduces what you owe.

Step 4: stay current on everything new

This is the part that sinks people. While the agreement is active you must stay current on all new tax obligations: enough withholding or estimated payments to cover each year's taxes, plus every monthly installment on time. If you owe a new balance next April, the IRS can default your agreement. Adjust your W-4 or make quarterly estimated payments the same year you set up the plan.

Who qualifies: the thresholds that matter

  • Guaranteed installment agreement: owe $10,000 or less, can pay within 36 months, filed on time and paid everything owed for the past 5 years, no offer in compromise in the past 5 years. The IRS must grant this one.
  • Streamlined long-term plan: owe $50,000 or less in combined tax, penalties, and interest, can pay within 72 months. No financial statement required. Instant online decision.
  • Short-term payment plan: owe $100,000 or less (individuals) or $25,000 or less (businesses), can pay within 180 days. No setup fee.
  • Over $50,000: still possible, but you'll submit a financial statement (Form 433-F for individuals) and the review takes longer. A tax professional is worth considering here.

What it costs: 2026 setup fees

The fee is one-time and usually gets added to your balance rather than charged separately. The cheapest path by far is applying online with direct debit:

  • Online, direct debit: $22
  • Online, other payment method: $69
  • Phone, mail, or in person, direct debit: $107
  • Phone, mail, or in person, other payment method: $178
  • Low-income taxpayers (AGI at or below 250% of the federal poverty level): fee reduced to $43, and waived entirely with direct debit. Claim it with Form 13844.
  • Reinstating a defaulted agreement: $10 online, $89 by phone or mail.

One more cost trap: if you pay monthly by credit or debit card, the third-party processor adds a convenience fee of roughly 1.75–1.85% on top of every payment. Direct debit from a bank account avoids it.

The real cost: interest never stops

An installment agreement is not a freeze. Interest accrues on the unpaid balance at the federal short-term rate plus 3%, compounded daily — around 8% annually in recent quarters. The failure-to-pay penalty keeps ticking at 0.25% per month while the agreement is active. And any future tax refunds are automatically applied to your balance; your monthly payments are still due when that happens.

Example math on a $12,000 balance over the full 72 months: the minimum monthly payment the IRS will accept is roughly $167 ($12,000 ÷ 72), and at ~8% interest you'll pay around $3,000 in interest over the life of the plan. There is no prepayment penalty, so every extra dollar you send above the minimum cuts the total interest. If you can pay it off in 30 months instead of 72, do it.

What can kill your agreement

  • Missing a monthly payment. One slip doesn't auto-terminate, but it starts the clock. Call the IRS before the due date if you can't pay.
  • Owing a new tax balance. A new unpaid year can default the whole agreement. Keep withholding or estimated payments current.
  • Missing a filing. All required returns must stay filed while the agreement runs.

If the agreement defaults, the IRS can resume levies and liens, and you pay a reinstatement fee to get it back. If your request is rejected outright, you can appeal with Form 9423 (Collection Appeal Request) — collections generally pause for about 30 days while the appeal is pending.

FAQ

How long does it take the IRS to approve an installment agreement?

Apply through the IRS Online Payment Agreement tool and most streamlined requests (owed $50,000 or less) get an instant decision. A mailed Form 9465 usually takes 30 to 60 days. While you wait, make the monthly payment you proposed anyway so you do not fall behind.

Can I request an IRS payment plan if I owe more than $50,000?

Yes, but you cannot use the streamlined online process. You will need to submit a financial statement (Form 433-F for individuals) and the IRS may require more documentation. The $50,000 cap and 72-month window only cover the streamlined long-term plan.

Will the IRS take my tax refund while I am on a payment plan?

Yes. Any future tax refunds are automatically applied to your outstanding balance while the agreement is active. Your monthly payments are still due even when a refund gets applied.

What happens if I miss a payment on an IRS installment agreement?

One missed payment does not automatically kill the agreement, but it puts you at risk of default. Defaulting lets the IRS resume collection actions, and reinstating the agreement costs a fee. If you know a payment will be late, contact the IRS before the due date.

Can I change my monthly payment amount after the agreement starts?

Yes. You can request a change through your IRS online account or by phone. Changing or restructuring an agreement can carry a fee (currently $10 online, $89 by phone or mail), so try to get the amount right the first time.

Do penalties and interest stop once my installment agreement is approved?

No. Interest keeps accruing on the unpaid balance at the federal short-term rate plus 3%, compounded daily. The late-payment penalty drops from 0.5% to 0.25% per month once the agreement is active, which cuts the penalty rate in half but does not stop it.

Can I pay off an IRS installment agreement early?

Yes, and you should if you can. There is no prepayment penalty. Paying extra toward the principal cuts the total interest you pay over the life of the agreement.

Do I need to file my tax return before requesting a payment plan?

Yes. You must file all required tax returns before the IRS will approve an installment agreement. File on time even if you cannot pay, because filing on time avoids the failure-to-file penalty, which is 5% per month.
