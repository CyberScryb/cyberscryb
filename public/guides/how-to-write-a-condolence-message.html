How to Write a Condolence Message

What a condolence message is actually for, a simple 4-part structure, what to say and what to never say, examples for every relationship, and a worked example.

What a Condolence Message Is Actually For

A condolence message has one job: comfort the living. It is not meant to fix the grief, explain the loss, or make the pain go away, because nothing you write will do that. What it does is tell a grieving person they are not alone and that the person they lost mattered to someone besides them. That is the entire bar. A short, sincere sentence clears it. A long, awkward message full of platitudes does not, even if it was written with better intentions. Write to the person reading it, not about your own discomfort with death.

The 4-Part Structure

When words will not come, follow this structure. It works for cards, texts, emails, and DMs, and it keeps you from rambling or freezing up.

  1. Name the loss directly. Say the word. "I was so sorry to hear your father passed away" beats "I heard the news," which forces a grieving person to wonder what you mean. Naming it is respectful, not harsh.
  2. Share one real memory or trait. One specific detail is worth more than a paragraph of general praise. "I will always remember how he made the whole room laugh at the reunion" proves the person mattered to you. If you did not know them well, name a trait instead: "From everything you have told me, she sounded like a remarkable woman."
  3. Offer specific help, not "let me know." "Let me know if you need anything" puts the work on a person who has no energy left. Name something concrete instead: "I am dropping off dinner Tuesday, no need to answer the door," or "I can take your shift Thursday if you need the time." Specific help gets accepted; vague help gets ignored.
  4. Close warmly. A simple, human ending: "You and your family are in my thoughts," "I am thinking of you," or "I am here for you." Sign your name.

What Never to Say

These phrases sound comforting to the person writing them and land badly on the person reading. Strike them all from your draft:

  • "I know how you feel." You do not, even if you have lost someone too. Every grief is different, and this claim can make the person feel misunderstood rather than seen.
  • "Everything happens for a reason." To someone freshly grieving, there is no reason that justifies this. It minimizes the loss.
  • "They are in a better place." This one is only safe when you know the person shares the faith it comes from and would find it comforting. Otherwise it can sound like you are glad the person is gone.
  • "Stay strong" or "be strong." It asks a grieving person to perform composure for your comfort. They are allowed to fall apart.
  • Advice of any kind. "You should take some time off," "maybe you should see someone," "at least she is not suffering." Grief is not a problem you are qualified to solve in a card.
  • Comparing your losses. "I know exactly what you are going through, my uncle died last year" turns their grief into a story about you. Keep the focus on them.
  • Asking for details. "What happened?" is natural curiosity, not comfort. Do not ask in a condolence message. If the cause of death has not been shared publicly, do not mention it at all.

Tone: Pick the One That Fits

The right tone depends on the relationship and the situation, not on what sounds most poetic.

  • Warm and gentle. For close friends and family. Personal, specific, a little longer. This is where a real memory belongs.
  • Formal and respectful. For coworkers, bosses, clients, and their families. Keep it brief, dignified, and professional. "Please accept my sincere condolences" is the classic opener for a reason.
  • Short and simple. For texts, acquaintances, and social media comments. Two or three sentences is plenty. "I am so sorry for your loss. Thinking of you" is a complete message.
  • Faith-based. Only when you are confident the person shares the faith. A verse or a prayer mentioned sincerely can be a real comfort. If you are not sure, use the warm and gentle tone instead.

Examples for Different Relationships

A close friend

"I was heartbroken to hear about your mom. I will never forget how she always had dinner on the table for anyone who walked through the door. I am bringing food by Thursday evening, and I will sit with you or give you space, whatever you need. I love you, and I am here."

A coworker

"I was very sorry to hear about your husband. Please accept my sincere condolences. I have got your projects covered while you are out, so take whatever time you need. We are all thinking of you."

A neighbor

"We were so sorry to hear about Jim. He was a good man, and this street will not be the same without him. We are right next door if you need anything at all. Dinner is on us this weekend."

A distant relative

"I was sorry to hear about Aunt Carol. I remember her kindness every time the family got together. My thoughts are with you and the whole family."

A pet loss

"I am so sorry about Max. He was a great dog, and I know how much he meant to you. Losing a pet is losing a family member, and I am thinking of you."

When you did not know the deceased

"I never got to meet your grandmother, but from everything you have told me, she was clearly one of a kind. I am so sorry for your loss."

Timing and How to Deliver

Sooner is better than perfect. Send the message within the first few days if you can, but a late message is still far better than silence. People remember that you wrote far more than they remember exactly when. As for the medium: a handwritten card is the gold standard for close relationships, a text or email is completely fine for coworkers, acquaintances, and neighbors, and a comment on a funeral home page or social media post is acceptable when that is the channel the family is using. Do not overthink the channel. The message is what matters. One more thing: follow up in a month or two. Grief outlasts the funeral, and a short "thinking of you, how are you holding up" weeks later means more than you would expect.

A Worked Full Example

Your friend Sarah lost her father. You knew him casually, from barbecues and family parties. Here is a complete message using the four-part structure:

Dear Sarah,

I was so sorry to hear about your dad passing away. [Names the loss.]

I will always remember him holding court at the grill at your Fourth of July party, telling stories while everyone else just listened. He had that way of making the whole backyard feel welcome. [One real memory.]

I am going to drop off a lasagna on Wednesday so you have one less thing to think about. You do not need to answer the door, I will leave it on the porch. [Specific help.]

You and your family are in my thoughts. I am here, whatever you need.
David

Notice what is not in there: no platitudes, no advice, no "call me if you need anything," no guessing about the cause. It is specific, warm, and short enough to read in one breath.

Frequently Asked Questions

What do I write in a condolence message?

Follow four parts: name the loss directly, share one real memory or trait of the person who died, offer specific help instead of a vague "let me know," and close with a warm line. Keep it short, sincere, and focused on the grieving person, not on your own feelings.

How long should a condolence message be?

Three to five short sentences is the sweet spot, about 60 to 150 words. Long messages can overwhelm someone in grief. A brief, specific note is always better than a long, rambling one.

Is it okay to send a condolence message by text?

Yes. A text is a perfectly acceptable condolence message today, especially for acquaintances, coworkers, and neighbors. A handwritten card carries more weight for close relationships, but a sincere text always beats silence.

Can I mention the cause of death in a condolence message?

Only if the family has shared it publicly. If the cause has not been announced, do not speculate or ask about it. Focus on the person and the loss, not on what happened.

What do I say to a coworker I barely knew?

Keep it formal, brief, and respectful: "I was sorry to hear about your [relationship]. Please accept my sincere condolences. If you need anything at work while you are away, just say the word." Do not invent closeness you do not have.

What do I write when I did not know the person who died?

Write to the person you do know. "I never had the chance to meet [name], but I know how much they meant to you. I am so sorry for your loss." It is honest, and it comforts without pretending.
