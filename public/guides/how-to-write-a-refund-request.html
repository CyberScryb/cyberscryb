How to write a refund request someone can act on

You paid for something. It arrived broken, never arrived, or did not match what you ordered. Now you have another job: explaining the problem clearly enough to get it handled.

By CyberScryb · October 9, 2026 · 5 min read

A useful request gives the seller a purchase to locate, a problem to investigate, and a decision to make. You do not need a legal-sounding introduction. You need the right facts in the right order.

This guide covers a request to the seller. A refund request does not replace a marketplace claim, payment-provider dispute, or any required complaint process. Those routes can have their own instructions and deadlines.

1. Check where the request belongs

Open the order confirmation and the seller's current returns or complaints page. Check the contact channel, what information they ask for, and any stated return window. Save the policy or instructions relevant to your purchase alongside the receipt. A support email may be useful, but it might not start a return in the seller's system.

If you bought through a marketplace, check its process too. Do not assume contacting the seller pauses a separate deadline. If timing is tight, consult the relevant provider's instructions while you prepare your message.

2. Gather the facts before writing the story

Put these details in front of you:

  • The purchase: item or service, purchase date, seller, and order or reference number if available.
  • The amount: what you paid and what you are requesting back. Explain a partial amount rather than leaving someone to calculate it.
  • The problem: what happened, when you noticed it, and how it differs from what was ordered or promised.
  • Previous contact: the date, channel, reference number, and relevant response.
  • Supporting records: the receipt, a clear photograph, delivery information, or the relevant message.

Use descriptions another person can check. “The left seam opened the first time I wore it” explains more than “terrible quality.” If you are unsure why something failed, describe the failure without assigning a cause.

3. Ask for one clear outcome

Say whether you want a refund, replacement, or repair. If you want a refund, state the amount when you know it. If you would accept a replacement instead, say so after the main request. “Please make this right” leaves too much of the decision unstated.

A requested outcome is a request, not a finding that the seller owes it. What is available can depend on the facts, the agreement, and applicable rules. You can be direct without claiming an entitlement you have not checked.

4. Build the message around the purchase

Use a subject line with the order reference and the purpose of the message. Then keep the body to a few focused paragraphs: purchase, problem, prior contact if relevant, and requested resolution.

The message does not need to prove how frustrating the experience was. It makes the problem and the requested action easy to identify. Add a reply-by date if useful, but make clear it is your requested response date, not a deadline the company has already accepted.

5. Match the evidence to the claim

Choose records that support the point you are making. For a damaged item, a photograph showing the damage is more useful than twelve unrelated screenshots. For an incorrect charge, the relevant receipt and transaction details may be enough to explain the discrepancy.

Keep your originals. Share copies through the company's appropriate channel, removing unrelated personal information where practical. Do not include passwords, card security codes, or a full payment-card number in an ordinary complaint message.

Only write “attached” after you attach the files. If you are using a form that cannot accept them, write “available on request” and ask where to send them.

6. Review the draft and keep the version you send

The Refund Request Letter Builder organizes your details into an editable draft. Fill in the facts, choose the resolution, then read the result as the person receiving it. Check every date, reference, amount, and statement about previous contact.

Copy the finished text into the seller's required channel, or download a text copy or print it. The tool does not send the request or upload evidence. Its inputs stay in the current page; download what you need before closing or refreshing it.

After sending, save the actual submitted message and confirmation. A draft in a folder shows what you prepared; a sent message or submission receipt helps show what you submitted and when.

7. Follow up on the open question

If the seller gives a response date, record it. If no date is given, choose a personal check-in date that fits the situation. Keep that reminder separate from any formal deadline in a return policy or dispute process.

A follow-up can be brief: “I am following up on case R-215, submitted September 13. I requested a $48 refund for the damaged lamp. Please confirm whether you need a return and when I can expect a decision.” Add new information only when it changes the case.

If the request stalls, check the seller's escalation process and your marketplace or payment-provider options. Do not let repeated promises of a callback distract you from a separate process you need to start.

Common sticking points

What if I do not have the receipt?

Look for an order email, account history, or transaction record. Explain what you have and ask what the seller can use to locate the purchase. Do not invent an order number to complete a form.

What if the seller offers store credit?

State whether you accept it. If you still want a refund, repeat that request and ask for the reason it was declined. Keep the offer and your response in the same record.

For another example format, see the FTC's sample customer complaint letter. Use the instructions that apply to your actual purchase.
