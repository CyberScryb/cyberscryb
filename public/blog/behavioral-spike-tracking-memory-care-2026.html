Tracking Behavioral Spikes in Memory Care

Published June 2, 2026 · 9 min read

Here's something most people don't realize about dementia care: those sudden outbursts aren't random. When someone starts pacing, yelling, or shoving things away out of nowhere, they're usually trying to tell you something. They just can't put it into words anymore.

The trick is catching the pattern before the crisis hits. That means writing things down — what happened, when it happened, what was going on right before. Give it a week and the triggers start showing up on their own.

The Anatomy of a Behavioral Spike

A lot of these spikes run on a schedule. The big one is sundowning — confusion and agitation that ramps up in the late afternoon and gets worse into the evening. But behind most other spikes there's usually a physical trigger:

  • Silent infections: If someone who's normally calm suddenly starts screaming or hitting, check for a UTI first. In elderly patients, a drastic behavior change is the number-one sign — not pain, not fever.
  • Sensory overload: Shift changes bring new voices, rattling keys, doors opening and closing. All that noise at once is enough to set off pacing or someone trying to leave.
  • Pain they can't describe: Constipation, a bad tooth, arthritis, even a shirt tag rubbing wrong. They feel it, they can't explain it, and it comes out as pushing or hitting.

How to Use the ABC Logging Method

Care staff use a simple framework called ABC — Antecedent, Behavior, Consequence. When something happens, you jot down three things, no emotions, just facts:

  1. Antecedent — what happened right before? Time, room, lighting, noise, what was going on. ("4:30pm, dinner trays arriving, dining room loud, lights dim.")
  2. Behavior — what did they actually do? Describe it plain, no labels. Write "pushed plate away, yelled 'get out'" — not "was aggressive."
  3. Consequence — what calmed them down? ("Moved to the quiet parlor, offered warm tea, settled in about 15 minutes.")

Analyzing the Data for Clinical Patterns

Keep it up for at least a week — paper or digital, whatever you'll actually use. Then look for patterns:

  • Same time every day? Check med timing, hunger, and lighting at that hour.
  • Same people or crowds? Note whether it only happens around certain staff, visitors, or when the room fills up.
  • What actually worked? Write down the interventions that calmed things down and share them with family and visiting nurses so everyone's doing the same thing.

FAQ

Why should I log behaviors digitally?

Paper logs get lost or ruined — spills happen in care settings. A digital log lets you search past dates, spot daily patterns, and print a clean summary to hand the doctor at care plan meetings.

When should behavioral spikes trigger a doctor's call?

If the change is sudden and drastic — new incontinence, hallucinations, combativeness overnight — call the doctor now and ask about a UTI or other acute issue. Don't wait around logging patterns when something changed that fast.
