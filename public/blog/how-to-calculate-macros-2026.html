How to Calculate Your Macros (2026): Protein, Carbs, Fat

Calories Decide the Scale. Macros Decide the Rest.

Calories are still the whole ballgame for whether you gain, lose, or hold weight. That part doesn’t change. What macros control is everything a bathroom scale can’t show you: whether the weight you lose is mostly fat or partly muscle, how hungry you are by 3pm, and whether you have the energy to get through a workout or a double shift. Two people eating the identical 2,000 calories a day can end up in very different places after a few months if one of them is eating mostly protein and vegetables and the other is eating mostly bread and soda. Same calorie math. Different results.

The Macro Math, Step by Step

Protein and carbs each supply 4 calories per gram. Fat supplies 9 calories per gram. That difference is the entire reason a "30% fat" diet and a "75% fat" diet (keto) don’t look nearly as different on a plate as the percentages suggest — fat is more than double the energy density of the other two macros.

The calculation itself is four steps:

  1. Get a daily calorie target (your TDEE, adjusted for cutting, maintaining, or bulking).
  2. Pick a split: what percent of those calories should be protein, carbs, and fat.
  3. Protein grams = (calories × protein%) ÷ 4. Carb grams = (calories × carb%) ÷ 4.
  4. Fat grams = (calories × fat%) ÷ 9.

Worked example: a 2,075-calorie target (a 20% cut from a 2,594 TDEE) on a Balanced split (30% protein / 40% carbs / 30% fat). Protein = (2,075 × 0.30) ÷ 4 = 156g. Carbs = (2,075 × 0.40) ÷ 4 = 208g. Fat = (2,075 × 0.30) ÷ 9 = 69g. Multiply back out and you land within a couple calories of the original target — the rest is just rounding.

How Much Protein You Actually Need

Protein gets singled out for a reason: your body can’t manufacture it efficiently from the other two macros, and your muscles depend on it directly. Sports nutrition research generally supports 0.7 to 1 gram per pound of bodyweight for people trying to build or keep muscle, with some benefit up to roughly 1.2g/lb during an aggressive cut, when your body is more likely to break down muscle for energy alongside fat.

That bodyweight number is worth keeping in your back pocket, because percentage splits can drift away from it at unusual calorie levels. On a very low-calorie target, even a 40% protein split might land under the bodyweight-based floor. On a large surplus, even 20% can land well above it. When the two disagree, the bodyweight number is the one with research behind it.

Macros for Weight Loss, Specifically

A calorie deficit is what drives fat loss. No macro split changes that. What the split changes is how the deficit feels and what, specifically, you lose along with the fat. A higher-protein split (35 to 40% of calories) is the most consistently supported choice for cutting: protein is the most filling macro gram for gram, and it gives your body a reason to protect muscle instead of burning it for fuel right alongside the fat. If you only adjust one macro while cutting, protein is the one worth protecting.

What a Macro Calculator Can’t See

Any macro calculator inherits the same honest limits as the TDEE calculation underneath it: the starting calorie number runs 10 to 20% off for a given person, since it’s built from population averages, not your actual metabolism. The percentage splits above are common, well-documented starting points — not a prescription from a dietitian who knows your labs, your medications, or a condition like kidney disease or diabetes that changes the macro math entirely. Treat the output as a two-to-three-week starting point. Track how you feel and what the scale does, then adjust the calories or the split — not both at once, so you can actually tell what moved the needle.
