1. Normal Wear & Tear vs. Actionable Damage

Under landlord-tenant statutes in all 50 states, landlords are strictly prohibited from deducting the cost of repairing normal wear and tear from a tenant's security deposit.

Normal Wear & Tear (Non-Deductible)

  • Small pinholes from picture hanging nails
  • Carpet traffic patterns and natural fiber wear
  • Fading paint due to sun exposure
  • Minor scuffs on baseboards or doors
  • Loose doorknobs or aging faucet washers

Tenant Damage (Deductible)

  • Large holes, gouges, or unauthorized drywall anchors
  • Pet urine stains, burns, or chewed carpeting
  • Broken windows, mirrors, or cabinet doors
  • Unauthorized custom paint colors left unprimed
  • Excessive filth, grease buildup, or abandoned furniture

2. The 50-State Statutory Return Deadline Table

Every state imposes a strict statutory window within which a landlord must either return the full deposit or provide an itemized written statement of deductions accompanied by paid receipts:

State Return Window Governing Statute Bad Faith Statutory Penalty
California 21 calendar days Cal. Civ. Code § 1950.5 Up to 2x deposit in statutory damages
New York 14 calendar days N.Y. Gen. Oblig. Law § 7-108 Forfeiture of right to withhold + 2x punitive
Texas 30 calendar days Tex. Prop. Code § 92.109 $100 + 3x wrongfully withheld sum + attorney fees
Florida 15 to 60 days Fla. Stat. § 83.49 Forfeiture of claim upon notice failure
Washington 30 calendar days RCW 59.18.280 Up to 2x deposit + court costs
Illinois 30 to 45 days 765 ILCS 710/1 Double deposit amount + attorney fees

3. Forfeiture of Claims: The Landlord's Biggest Mistake

In almost every jurisdiction, if a landlord fails to return the deposit or provide the itemized deduction letter with receipts within the statutory window, they legally forfeit all rights to retain any portion of your deposit. Even if genuine damages exist, the landlord loses the legal right to self-remedy out of your deposit once the calendar deadline passes.

4. Step-by-Step Recovery Protocol

  1. Verify Forwarding Address Notice: Ensure you have documented written proof (text, email, or letter) that you provided your landlord with your new mailing address upon move-out.
  2. Calculate the Exact Statutory Deadline: Count calendar days starting the day after you surrendered the keys and vacated the property.
  3. Send a Formal Demand Letter: Draft a professional letter citing your state statute, move-out date, forwarding address notification date, and deadline expiration. State clearly that failure to pay within 10 to 14 business days will prompt immediate filing in Small Claims Court for the principal plus statutory bad-faith damages.
  4. Deliver via USPS Certified Mail: Send with Return Receipt Requested so you receive physical or digital proof of delivery signed by the landlord or property management agent.
