What Happens If You Skip Jury Duty

Published October 12, 2026 · 7 min read

A jury duty summons arrives, the date does not work, and ignoring it feels like the easiest option. No letter to write, no call to make, no proof to gather. Just do not go.

This is the one move that turns a manageable scheduling problem into a legal one. Here is what actually happens when you no-show, what it costs, and what to do instead.

The summons is a court order, not an invitation

This is the fact everything else follows from. A jury summons is issued by a court and carries the force of a court order. Ignoring it is not like skipping a doctor's appointment. It is failing to appear when a court told you to appear, and courts treat it that way.

That does not mean a SWAT team shows up at your door. But it does mean the consequences below are real and documented, not scare tactics.

What happens first: the second chance

In practice, most courts do not punish a first no-show immediately. What usually happens is one of these:

  • A second summons. Your name goes back in the pool and you get another date. This is the most common outcome, and it fools people into thinking nothing happened. Something did: you now have a record of a prior no-show.
  • A failure-to-appear notice. A letter informing you that you missed your date and ordering you to appear or respond by a new deadline. This is your warning shot. Take it seriously.
  • A hearing before a judge. Some courts summon no-shows to explain themselves directly to the judge. You stand there, under oath, explaining why you ignored a court order. This is as unpleasant as it sounds.

None of these are good, but they are the mild outcomes. The escalation beyond them is where it gets expensive.

Fines: what a no-show actually costs

Fines for missing jury duty vary enormously by jurisdiction. In many places, the standard fine runs a few hundred dollars for a first no-show. In some jurisdictions, fines reach over a thousand dollars per day of missed service. Contempt of court findings carry their own fines on top.

Exact numbers depend on where you live, which is why you should check your summons or call the clerk's office rather than trusting a number from the internet. But the direction is the same everywhere: the fine costs more than a day of your time, and it does not make the summons go away. You still have to serve.

The worst realistic outcome: repeated no-shows can lead to a bench warrant in some jurisdictions. A bench warrant can surface during a traffic stop or any other police contact. You go from a scheduling problem to being taken into custody over a summons you could have deferred with a phone call.

The scenario that makes it worse: no-show after a denial

There is one situation that escalates faster than a plain no-show. If you filed a deferral or excusal request and it was denied, the court has your name flagged. You asked, they said no, and then you did not show up anyway.

Courts read that as defiance, not forgetfulness. The judge in the failure-to-appear hearing sees a person who knew the date, knew the rule, asked for permission, was denied, and ignored the order. Fines go up. Contempt becomes more likely. The margin for leniency shrinks to nothing.

If your request is denied, go. Bring your documentation to check-in. Courts routinely reconsider hardship claims in person, and the clerk who denied your letter often grants the same claim face to face. Showing up costs a day. Not showing up after a denial costs a day plus a fine plus a worse record.

"I never got the summons" and other things that do not work

Summonses are usually sent by regular mail, not certified. The court assumes delivery, and courts have heard every version of "I never got it." If you genuinely never received a summons, the right move is to contact the clerk's office the moment you learn about it. Courts are generally reasonable about real mail problems: forwarded addresses, stolen mail, moves mid-summons cycle.

What does not work is claiming ignorance after the fact as a strategy. "I thought it was junk mail" is not a defense. Neither is changing your address without telling anyone and hoping the summons cannot find you. The obligation follows you, and the eventual hearing is worse when the court believes you knew.

What to do instead: the deferral request

The safe path takes ten minutes. A deferral request says: I cannot serve on this date, I can serve on this future date. Courts grant these routinely, sometimes with no reason required at all.

File it before your summons date. Two to four weeks ahead is ideal, but even a few days before beats nothing. If your situation is documented, medical, financial, caregiving, work, attach the proof and the request becomes nearly automatic.

And if it is denied, the fallback is simple: show up with your documentation. In-person hardship claims at check-in succeed often enough that going is worth it even when you expect a yes to be denied.

The bottom line

A no-show does not make jury duty go away. It converts a scheduling problem into a fine, a hearing, or a warrant, and you still have to serve afterward. A deferral request does the opposite: it keeps you legal, costs ten minutes, and usually gets approved.

File the request. Attach the proof. If denied, go anyway.

FAQ

What happens if I skip jury duty?

You can be fined, held in contempt of court, or issued a bench warrant depending on the jurisdiction. Most courts send a second summons or a notice first, but a no-show after a denied deferral request is taken especially seriously.

Can you get a warrant for missing jury duty?

Yes, in some jurisdictions. Repeated no-shows can lead to a bench warrant, which can surface during traffic stops or other police contact. This is the worst realistic outcome, and it is entirely avoidable by filing a deferral request.

How much is the fine for missing jury duty?

Fines vary widely by jurisdiction, from a few hundred dollars to over a thousand per day of missed service in some places. Check your summons or call the clerk's office for the exact figure where you live.

What if I never got the jury duty summons?

Summonses are usually sent by regular mail, and courts assume delivery. If you genuinely never received it, contact the clerk's office as soon as you learn about it. Courts are generally reasonable about real mail problems but not about claims of ignorance made after the fact.

Is there a safe way to get out of jury duty?

The safe path is a deferral request: ask to serve on a specific future date instead of ignoring the summons. Courts grant deferrals routinely. Filing the request before your summons date keeps you on the right side of the law no matter what.
