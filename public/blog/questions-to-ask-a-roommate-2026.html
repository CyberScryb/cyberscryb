27 Questions to Ask a Potential Roommate Before You Sign Anything

Published October 12, 2026 · 7 min read · Roommate Guide

Most people pick a roommate based on one coffee and a gut feeling. The gut feeling is picking up on vibes; it is not checking whether your sleep schedules can coexist or what happens when the electric bill doubles. Ask these in person, over coffee, before anyone signs. How they answer matters less than whether they answer honestly.

Money (ask these first)

  1. What is your work situation right now, and how stable is it? You are not interrogating them. You are checking whether the rent money is real. Someone between jobs with savings is a different risk than someone vague about income.
  2. Have you ever been late on rent? What happened? The right answer is a story with an ending ("my hours got cut, I told my roommate on the 25th, we worked it out"). The wrong answer is defensiveness. Defensiveness about money is data.
  3. How do you want to handle bills: one person pays and gets reimbursed, or everyone pays their own share directly? This reveals their tolerance for being the banker, and whether they have thought about it at all.
  4. If your income dropped suddenly, what would you do? You want to hear a plan: savings, a second gig, telling the household early. "I do not know" is honest and fine, as long as it is honest.
  5. How should we split rent if the rooms are different sizes? Do not wait for move-in to discover you have opposite theories of fairness. Ask now. If you want the math, see our rent splitting guide.

Guests and Partners

  1. How do you feel about overnight guests? Give me a number, in nights per week. "I do not mind guests" is not an answer. Numbers are answers. Two nights a week is a policy. "Whatever" is a future fight.
  2. Are you seeing anyone right now who would be around a lot? Ask it directly and without judgment. A partner who stays over five nights a week is a fourth roommate who is not paying, and it is better to price that in now than discover it in month four.
  3. What would you consider a fair rule if someone's partner basically moves in? Listen for whether they have thought about other people's comfort, not just their own situation.

Schedule and Sleep

  1. What hours do you sleep and work? The 5 AM riser and the 2 AM gamer can coexist, but only with quiet hours written down and both of them knowing the deal going in.
  2. Are you a light sleeper? A light sleeper who works early mornings will not survive a night owl roommate without ground rules. Better to know now.
  3. Do you work from home? How much of the day? Someone on calls from the kitchen table eight hours a day changes the whole house. It affects noise, utility bills, and who gets the common space during the day.

Cleaning and Shared Space

  1. What does "clean enough" look like to you? Be specific: dishes, bathroom, floors. "I am pretty clean" means nothing. Same-day dishes versus same-week dishes is a real gap, and it is exactly the kind of gap that breeds passive aggression.
  2. How do you feel about a rotating chore schedule? If they hate the idea of structure, you are signing up for the "whoever notices does it" system, which always means one person does it.
  3. How do you feel about sharing food, cookware, cleaning supplies? Label-everything people and share-everything people can live together, but only if they agree on which system they are using before the first grocery run.
  4. Smoking, vaping, drinking: what is your situation, and what do you expect from the house? Ask plainly. This is a lease-violation category in many rentals, so it is not a preference question, it is a risk question.

Pets, Parking, and the Rest of Logistics

  1. Any pets now or planned? "I might get a dog" is a different answer than "no pets." Check the lease too; many forbid them outright.
  2. Do you have a car? What are the parking expectations? In buildings with one assigned spot, this is a real negotiation, not a detail.
  3. What temperature do you keep the place? The thermostat wars are real, they are year-round, and they cost actual money. 68 and 76 are not the same bill.
  4. How do you feel about having friends over for dinner or game nights? Some people want a social house. Some want a quiet one. Both are fine. The mismatch is the problem.

Conflict Style (the most important section)

  1. When something bothers you, do you say it right away or sit on it? Two "sit on it" people in one house means problems surface as explosions. One direct person and one avoider means the direct one feels like the bad guy. Know the dynamic you are entering.
  2. Have you ever had a roommate conflict? How did it end? Everyone has had one. The story tells you everything: do they take responsibility, do they blame, did they communicate or just leave?
  3. What is something a past roommate did that drove you crazy? This is the back door into their real pet peeves. The answer is always specific, and it is always true.
  4. If we disagree about something household-related, how should we settle it? You are asking them to pre-commit to a process: talk it out, house vote, cooling off first. Process is what keeps a disagreement from becoming a feud.

Exit Plans

  1. How long are you planning to stay? A 12-month lease with someone planning to leave in five months is a problem you can see coming, which makes it solvable.
  2. If you had to leave early, what would you consider fair: how much notice, and would you help find a replacement? The answer becomes the move-out clause. Get it in words before it matters.
  3. Is there anything about your situation I should know that would affect the house? An open door for the thing they were nervous to mention: a medical schedule, a custody arrangement, a relative who visits for a month every summer. Whatever it is, it is easier to plan around than to discover.

What the Answers Tell You

You are not scoring them. You are looking for three things. Honesty: specific answers beat smooth ones. "I am messy and I know it, I need the rotation" is a better roommate than someone who claims to be clean but cannot say what that means. Compatibility on the big four: money reliability, sleep schedule, cleaning standards, and guest tolerance. Small gaps are fine; big gaps on more than one of these are a no. Conflict style: someone who has never once mentioned a problem until they exploded will do it in your house too.

One red flag that overrides everything else: evasiveness about money. Vague income, dodged questions about late rent, irritation at being asked. Everything else is negotiable. That is not.
