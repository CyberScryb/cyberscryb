7 Password Security Mistakes You're Probably Making

By CyberScryb Team · April 8, 2026 · 6 min read

Most password advice is stuck in 2012. "Use at least 8 characters. Include a capital letter. Add a number. Change it every 90 days." All of that is out of date, and some of it is actively harmful. Modern attackers do not brute-force passwords character by character — they run billions of leaked credentials through stuffing scripts, they crack hashes with GPUs that cost less than a used car, and they wait for you to reuse a password across sites so they can pivot from the breach of a forum nobody cares about into your bank account.

Here are seven mistakes we still see constantly in 2026 — from junior devs up to senior engineers — along with a fix for each one. None of the fixes require you to memorize anything.

Mistake 1: Reusing the same password across sites

This is still the number-one way accounts get compromised. Credential stuffing attacks automate "email + password" pairs against thousands of sites per minute. A single leaked forum from 2019 can unlock Gmail accounts in 2026.

Fix: Use a password manager. Any of the big four (1Password, Bitwarden, Dashlane, KeePassXC) will do. Generate a unique random password for every site. If you forget the master password you are in trouble — but you will not reuse passwords ever again, which is the single highest-impact change you can make to your personal security posture.

Mistake 2: Trusting the "strength bar" on signup forms

The colorful bar that turns green when you type "P@ssw0rd!" is mostly theater. Most signup forms score passwords by counting character classes, not by estimating actual entropy. "P@ssw0rd!" is on every password cracker's first-thousand-guesses list. The bar says green. The password is trash.

Fix: Check your candidate passwords with an entropy-based tool. The CyberScryb Password Checker runs 100% in your browser (your password never leaves your device), calculates actual Shannon entropy, detects common patterns, and gives you a realistic 2024-GPU crack-time estimate instead of the inflated numbers you see on most sites.

Mistake 3: Using a password that looks random but is not

Passwords like Tr0ub4dor&3 (famously mocked by xkcd) feel strong because they look random to a human. They are not. Modern crackers know every substitution (a→@, o→0, s→$) and burn through these variants almost as fast as plain dictionary words.

Fix: Use one of two patterns. Either a long random string from a password manager (20+ characters, all classes), or a four-word passphrase from a large wordlist (correct horse battery staple-style). Both are strong; both are easy to audit with a real entropy checker. Random substitutions on a known word is the worst of both worlds.

Mistake 4: Changing passwords on a fixed schedule

This one is officially deprecated by NIST (since SP 800-63B, published 2017), yet corporate IT departments still mandate 90-day rotations. The research is unambiguous: forced rotation makes users pick worse passwords, because they start appending month numbers or ascending integers (Winter2025!, Winter2026!).

Fix: Only rotate passwords when there is a reason — a breach disclosure, a shared account whose user left, a leaked credential in a pwned passwords check. Otherwise, a strong password should last as long as the account does.

Mistake 5: Using SMS as the second factor

SMS-based 2FA is better than nothing, but it is the weakest form of multi-factor auth on the market in 2026. SIM-swap attacks are cheap, carriers are inconsistent about verifying identity before porting a number, and the SMS itself crosses networks you do not control.

Fix: Use an authenticator app (TOTP) or, better, a physical security key (YubiKey, Titan Key, or any FIDO2 hardware). Security keys are immune to phishing in a way that nothing else is — they bind the authentication to the origin of the site, so even a perfect clone of your bank's login page cannot reuse the response. If you have any critical account (email, bank, crypto), spend the $25 and put a hardware key on it.

Mistake 6: Storing passwords in plain text files, notes apps, or browser auto-fill without encryption

Every year, we audit at least one dev team whose "password policy" is a shared Google Doc. Or a Slack message from 2022. Or a note titled "passwords" on an iPhone that syncs unencrypted to the cloud.

Fix: This is what password managers are for. Even the free tier of Bitwarden is fine. If you cannot install a third-party tool for compliance reasons, use your operating system's built-in keychain with a strong master password and full-disk encryption. A sticky note under the keyboard is not a joke — it is measurably more secure than a plain-text file on a laptop.

Mistake 7: Not checking whether your password has already been leaked

Here is the uncomfortable truth: your current passwords are probably already in a breach database somewhere. Thousands of breaches have leaked billions of credentials, and even careful users have had at least one account compromised.

Fix: Run your most important passwords through haveibeenpwned.com/Passwords (or our guide on how to check if a password has been leaked). HIBP uses a k-anonymity protocol so you only send the first 5 characters of the SHA-1 hash, never the password itself. If your password appears in the database, rotate it immediately and never reuse that pattern again. And while you are there, test your future passwords with the CyberScryb Password Checker so you can spot weak candidates before you ever commit them.

A short checklist to do this week

If you do nothing else, do these five things in the next seven days:

  1. Install a password manager and import your browser-saved passwords.
  2. Generate unique random passwords for your email, bank, and any account with payment info.
  3. Turn on TOTP or hardware-key 2FA for those same accounts.
  4. Run your email address through haveibeenpwned.com and rotate anything flagged.
  5. Test your new passwords with the CyberScryb Password Checker to confirm the entropy is actually high.

That is it. No week-long overhaul, no buying five security books. Just the five highest-leverage actions, ordered by ROI.

One last note on password-manager paranoia

Every so often a password manager has a security incident and people ask "see, isn't putting all my eggs in one basket a bad idea?" The honest answer is: the basket is still stronger than what you were doing before. Even after the LastPass 2022 incident, the average user who had a strong master password was safer than the average user who was reusing passwords across 50 sites. The math is not even close.

If you want to be extra careful, pick a password manager that is open-source, audited, and uses client-side encryption with a master password that never leaves your device (Bitwarden and KeePassXC both qualify). Then you get the "single basket" convenience without the "trust a vendor forever" downside.
